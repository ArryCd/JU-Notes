L01 Introduction to Drude's Free Electron Theory — Historical Context & Assumptions Week 1▾

Paul Drude (1900) proposed the first quantitative model for metallic conduction by treating conduction electrons as a classical ideal gas — the "free electron" model. Despite being pre-quantum, it achieves remarkable successes and its failures are equally instructive, pointing directly to the need for quantum mechanics.

Drude's Five Assumptions

  • Free electrons: Valence electrons detach from atoms and roam freely — the "electron gas". Positive ionic cores are fixed background charges.
  • Independent electrons: Electrons interact with ionic cores at collision events only — no electron–electron interactions (independent electron approximation).
  • Instantaneous randomising collisions: Each collision is instantaneous and completely randomises the electron's velocity direction. No memory of pre-collision state.
  • Relaxation time τ: The mean time between successive collisions. Between collisions, electrons travel freely (no force except applied fields). τ ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ s at room temperature.
  • Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics: Classical velocity distribution. This is the fatal flaw Sommerfeld corrected.

Electron Number Density

Electron number density (Z_v = valence)
n = Z_v · ρN_A / A Cu: ρ=8.96 g/cm³, A=63.55, Z_v=1 → n = 8.49×10²² cm⁻³ = 8.49×10²⁸ m⁻³

Physical Picture: Electron Scattering

"The idea was this: these electrons are scattering from ionic cores and going from one to the other — this is how the scattering is occurring and this is what leads to resistivity. It is like a billiard ball which is hitting different points and then moving around this metal." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 8
L02 Drude EOM & Electrical Conductivity — Full Derivation Week 1▾

Equation of Motion with Damping

An electron (charge −e, e>0) in electric field E: driving force eE. Collision "friction": −mv/τ (on average, momentum lost per unit time = mv/τ). Full EOM:

▸ Derivation: σ = ne²τ/m (Drude)
Full EOM: m(dv/dt) = eE − (m/τ)v
Transient solution (v(0)=0): v(t) = (eEτ/m)(1 − e^{−t/τ}) → v_d = eEτ/m as t→∞
Steady state (dv/dt=0): eE = mv_d/τ → v_d = eEτ/m
Current density (n electrons/vol, charge −e, moving at −v_d): j = nev_d = (ne²τ/m)E
Ohm's law j = σE gives:
σ = ne²τ/m    ρ = 1/σ = m/(ne²τ)    (Drude)

Numerical Verification — Copper

n=8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³, e=1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, m=9.1×10⁻³¹ kg, τ≈2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s σ = (8.5×10²⁸ × (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)² × 2.5×10⁻¹⁴) / 9.1×10⁻³¹ ≈ 5.9×10⁷ Ω⁻¹m⁻¹ Experiment: 6.0×10⁷ Ω⁻¹m⁻¹ ✓ — excellent agreement!

Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

In Drude model, τ ∝ 1/T (more phonon scattering at higher T) → ρ = m/(ne²τ) ∝ T. This predicts linear ρ(T) at high T — correct! Metals do show ρ ∝ T above the Debye temperature. At low T, Bloch–Grüneisen theory (quantum) gives ρ ∝ T⁵.

L03 Hall Effect — Part I: Setup, Geometry & Derivation of R_H Week 1▾

Hall Effect Geometry

▸ Derivation: Hall Coefficient R_H from Drude Theory
Setup: Current J in ŷ, B = B₀ẑ. Steady-state EOM: 0 = e(E + v×B) − mv/τ
x-component: 0 = eE_x + ev_yB₀ − mv_x/τ
y-component: 0 = eE_y − ev_xB₀ − mv_y/τ
Hall condition: No current in x → J_x = 0 → v_x = 0 at steady state
From x-equation with v_x=0: 0 = eE_x + ev_yB₀ → E_x = −v_yB₀
From y-equation with v_x=0: v_y = eE_yτ/m = J_y/(ne), so E_x = −J_yB₀/(ne)
R_H = E_x/(J_yB₀) = −1/(ne)   (negative → electrons are carriers)
Hall measurement quantities
Hall resistivity: ρ_H = R_H·B₀ = −B₀/(ne) Hall voltage: V_H = −IB₀/(net)   (t = sample thickness) Hall resistance: ℛ_H = V_H/I = R_H·B₀/t = −B₀/(net)
L04 Hall Effect — Part II: Applications as Sensors Week 1▾

Hall Sensors — Real-World Applications

"When you are using a Hall sensor — you bias it by sending a current I through the sample and if you take the sample and place it in a magnetic field B₀, you will get a transverse voltage V_H. If you measure this ratio V_H/I you will immediately know how much is the magnetic field B₀ — and this is how you can use it as a sensor." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 8
"In automobiles it is used for measuring the level of the fuel. You suspend a magnet which floats on top of the fuel. As the level of the liquid goes down, the magnet also moves down, much further away from the sensor. B₀ becomes less, the Hall voltage becomes less, and therefore you can find out how much is the decrease in the fluid." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 8

Summary of Hall Sensor Applications

  • Automobile fuel level gauges (floating magnet on fuel)
  • Position sensing of rotating discs in industrial machinery
  • Aerospace orientation sensors
  • Mobile phone compasses and proximity sensors
  • Brushless DC motor position detection

"What Drude's theory says is that the longitudinal resistivity — the voltage drop along the direction of the flow of current — is independent of the magnetic field. L06 Need for Quantum Mechanics — Introduction to Sommerfeld's Model Week 2▾

Why Classical Statistics Fails for Electrons

Electrons are fermions — half-integer spin particles — obeying the Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons can share the same quantum state (same n, ℓ, m_ℓ, m_s). This forces electrons to fill energy levels from the bottom up, not spread classically. The result is a completely different velocity and energy distribution from the Maxwell–Boltzmann gas Drude assumed.

Fermi–Dirac Distribution

Fermi–Dirac Distribution (Central result of quantum statistical mechanics)
f(ε) = 1 / {exp[(ε − μ)/k_BT] + 1}

μ = chemical potential (= Fermi energy E_F at T=0). f = probability a state of energy ε is occupied. The +1 in the denominator encodes Pauli exclusion (unlike Bose–Einstein which has −1, or Maxwell–Boltzmann which has no ±1).

Boundary Conditions — Why Periodic BC?

"In your problem of just a single electron inside a box you consider that the wave function becomes zero at the two edges. But this has a problem because it gives rise to standing waves inside the solid — you want electrons to actually propagate through the solid. So what Sommerfeld considered was this very important periodic boundary condition: the wave function at x equals the wave function at x+L." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 13
Periodic (Born–von Kármán) BC → allowed k
ψ(x+L) = ψ(x) → e^{ikL} = 1 → k = 2πn/L (n = 0, ±1, ±2, ...) 3D: k_x = 2πn_x/L, k_y = 2πn_y/L, k_z = 2πn_z/L Plane wave: ψ_k(r) = (1/√V)e^{ik·r}, Energy: ε_k = ℏ²k²/(2m)
L07 Sommerfeld I — Quantum States in a Metal, k-Space & Fermi Sphere Week 2▾

Discrete k-States in k-Space

"If I look at the momentum of my electron in the solid, it has momenta k_x, k_y, k_z. You will have a discrete set of states — each spaced by 2π/L in each direction. The smallest volume in the momentum space is equal to (2π/L)³ — below this volume there is no state which is available." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 13

Sommerfeld Expansion

For properties that depend on integrals of the form ∫f(ε)H(ε)dε, the correction from T=0 result can be systematically calculated. The key result:

Sommerfeld expansion (leading correction in T²)
∫₋∞^∞ H(ε)f(ε)dε ≈ ∫₋∞^μ H(ε)dε + (π²/6)(k_BT)²H'(μ) + O(T⁴)
E_F at finite T
E_F(T) = E_F₀[1 − (π²/12)(k_BT/E_F₀)² + ...] Cu at 300K: correction ≈ (π²/12)(0.026/7)² ≈ 4×10⁻⁴ → negligible!
Internal energy at finite T
U(T) = U₀[1 + (5π²/12)(k_BT/E_F₀)² + ...]
L11 Fermi Sphere, Fermi Surface & DOS at E_F Week 2▾

The Fermi Surface

The Fermi surface is the surface in k-space separating occupied from empty states at T=0. For free electrons it is a perfect sphere of radius k_F. For real metals (in a periodic potential), the Fermi surface is distorted by the lattice and can have complex topology.

Key Fermi surface quantities
k_F = (3π²n)^{1/3}   (Fermi wavevector) E_F = ℏ²k_F²/(2m)   (Fermi energy) v_F = ℏk_F/m = √(2E_F/m)   (Fermi velocity) D(E_F) = 3n/(2E_F)   (DOS at Fermi energy)

All transport and thermodynamic properties of metals are governed by electrons near the Fermi surface — not by all N electrons. This is a fundamental principle of Fermi liquid theory.

L13 Electronic Specific Heat — Derivation of C_ve = γT Week 3▾

Qualitative Argument

In Drude: all N electrons absorb heat → C = (3/2)Nk_B. In Sommerfeld: only electrons within ~k_BT of E_F can be excited (need empty state above). Fraction active ≈ k_BT/E_F. Energy absorbed ≈ (fraction) × N × k_BT → U_thermal ∝ (k_BT/E_F)×Nk_BT = Nk_B²T²/E_F → C_ve ∝ T.

▸ Derivation: C_ve = γT (Sommerfeld)
From Sommerfeld expansion: U(T) = U₀ + (π²/6)D(E_F)(k_BT)² + O(T⁴)
Using D(E_F) = 3n/(2E_F): U(T) = U₀ + (π²/4)·n·k_B²T²/E_F
Differentiate: C_ve = dU/dT = (π²/2)·n·k_B²T/E_F = (π²/2)·nk_B·(k_BT/E_F)
C_ve = γT    γ = π²nk_B²/(2E_F) = π²k_B²D(E_F)/3   (Sommerfeld constant)
Ratio of Sommerfeld to Drude specific heat
C_ve(Sommerfeld) / C_ve(Drude) = (π²/3)·(k_BT/E_F) ≈ 0.01 at 300K

~100× smaller than Drude — matches experiment perfectly! The factor k_BT/E_F is the fraction of active electrons.

L15–L17 Thermal Conductivity of Metals — Drude vs Sommerfeld Week 3▾
"In Drude's theory the velocity was about 10⁵ m/s. But in Sommerfeld the velocity is the Fermi velocity — which is 10 times the Drude velocity. The specific heat from Sommerfeld is about 10⁻² times the Drude specific heat. These two factors cancel out — the kappa from Sommerfeld is approximately the same as from Drude! With a strange combination of higher velocity but lower specific heat, they completely cancel out at room temperature." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 23
▸ Derivation: Thermal Conductivity κ (Sommerfeld)
General kinetic theory: κ = (1/3)·v_F²·τ·C_ve_vol
v_F = Fermi velocity (T-independent), C_ve_vol = γT (volume-specific heat)
Also: σ = ne²τ/m, v_F² = 2E_F/m, C_ve_vol = (π²/3)D(E_F)k_B²T
Ratio κ/σ: (1/3)v_F²τ·C_ve_vol / (ne²τ/m) = mv_F²C_ve_vol/(3ne²) = 2E_F·C_ve_vol/(3ne²)
Substitute C_ve_vol = (π²/3)·(3n/2E_F)·k_B²T = π²nk_B²T/(2E_F):
κ/(σT) = π²k_B²/(3e²) = L₀ = 2.44×10⁻⁸ W·Ω·K⁻² ← Lorenz number ■
Wiedemann–Franz Law
κ = L₀·σ·T    L₀ = π²k_B²/(3e²) = 2.44×10⁻⁸ W·Ω·K⁻²

n and τ cancel → universal constant. Sommerfeld gives correct L₀. Drude gives L₀ = (3/2)(k_B/e)² ≈ 1.24×10⁻⁸ — wrong by factor ~2.

"The Wiedemann-Franz law is valid even within Sommerfeld's theory. But at intermediate temperatures the Wiedemann-Franz law is not validated — because there the ionic contribution (AT³ phonon term) to specific heat contributes and changes the balance between κ and σ." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 23
L18 Pauli Spin Paramagnetism & Limitations of Sommerfeld Model Week 3▾

Pauli Paramagnetism

In a magnetic field B, spin-up and spin-down electron sub-bands shift by ±μ_BB. More spin-down states fill → net magnetisation. Only electrons near E_F participate.

Pauli spin susceptibility
χ_P = μ₀μ_B²·D(E_F) = 3nμ₀μ_B²/(2E_F) = nμ₀μ_B²/(k_BT_F) Temperature-INDEPENDENT!   (contrast with Curie law χ ∝ 1/T)
Key Experimental Signature Classical Curie paramagnetism gives χ ∝ 1/T. Pauli paramagnetism is T-independent. Observation of T-independent χ in alkali metals (Li, Na, K) was one of the first direct experimental confirmations of Fermi-Dirac statistics in metals.

Limitations of Sommerfeld Model

"We have not considered that the electron is moving in a lattice of ions — we have not considered the interaction of the electrons with the ions inside the solid. The electron is moving through a periodic potential which is created by these ions, but we have neglected all of that. If you include all of that then the effect of these interactions actually acts like a drag on the electrons and it increases its effective mass." — Prof. Satyajit Banerjee, Lecture 23
  • Neglects periodic potential of ion cores → cannot explain band gaps, metals vs. insulators
  • Neglects electron-phonon interaction → explains m* enhancement (heavy fermions)
  • Neglects electron-electron interactions → important in correlated electron systems
  • Cannot explain positive Hall coefficients (needs holes concept)

L19 Crystal Structure I — Lattice, Basis, Unit Cell, Wigner-Seitz Cell Week 4▾
Fundamental Identity
Crystal = Lattice + Basis

Lattice: mathematical set of points with perfect translational symmetry. Basis: identical atomic group at each point. Lattice captures geometry; basis captures the physical/chemical content.

Definitions

  • Bravais lattice: Set of points R = n₁a + n₂b + n₃c (primitive vectors a,b,c; integers n₁,n₂,n₃). Every point has identical environment.
  • Primitive unit cell: Minimum-volume cell containing exactly 1 lattice point. Volume = |a·(b×c)|.
  • Conventional unit cell: Larger cell displaying crystal symmetry (may contain 2, 4, etc. lattice points). More intuitive but not primitive.
  • Wigner–Seitz cell: Region of space closer to a given lattice point than to any other. Constructed by drawing perpendicular bisector planes between a point and all its neighbours. Has full point-group symmetry of the lattice.

L22 Crystallographic Restriction Theorem — Only n = 1,2,3,4,6 Allowed Week 4▾
▸ Proof: Crystallographic Restriction Theorem
Setup: 2D lattice with spacing t. Points A and B are adjacent, distance t apart. Rotation by φ = 360°/n is a symmetry.
Rotate B about A by φ → new point C. Rotate A about B by −φ → new point D. Both C,D must be lattice points.
Distance CD: By geometry (both rotations are by φ from A,B separated by t):
CD = t − 2t·cos(π−φ) = t + 2t·cosφ = t(1 + 2cosφ)
Lattice constraint: CD must be an integer multiple of t: 1 + 2cosφ = m (integer)
Since |cosφ|≤1: −1 ≤ (m−1)/2 ≤ 1 → −1 ≤ m ≤ 3, so m ∈ {−1,0,1,2,3}
m=−1 → cosφ=−1 → φ=180° → n=2  |  m=0 → cosφ=−½ → φ=120° → n=3
m=1 → cosφ=0 → φ=90° → n=4  |  m=2 → cosφ=½ → φ=60° → n=6  |  m=3 → φ=0°, n=1
Only n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 are compatible with translational periodicity ■

n=5 forbidden: φ=72°, cos72°≈0.309 → m=1+2×0.309=1.618 — NOT an integer. Pentagons cannot tile a plane. Quasicrystals (Shechtman 1984, Nobel 2011) have 5-fold diffraction patterns because they are aperiodic (quasiperiodic) — they do not contradict this theorem.

L23 Miller Indices I — Procedure & Important Planes Week 4▾

Miller Index Procedure

  1. Find where the plane intercepts the three crystallographic axes. Express intercepts in units of lattice parameters: (p·a, q·b, r·c).
  2. Take reciprocals: (1/p, 1/q, 1/r).
  3. Multiply by LCM to get smallest integers: (h, k, l).
  4. Negative intercept → bar over index: (h̄kl). Parallel to axis → intercept ∞ → index 0.
Why Reciprocals? If a plane is parallel to an axis, it never intersects it (intercept = ∞). Taking 1/∞ = 0 is mathematically clean. Also: in orthogonal systems, (hkl) are proportional to components of the unit normal to the plane. The reciprocal lattice vector G_hkl = ha* + kb* + lc* is always normal to the (hkl) planes.

L47 Classical Dulong-Petit & Einstein Model of Specific Heat Week 8▾

Classical Dulong-Petit Law (1819)

Each atom has 3 KE + 3 PE degrees of freedom. Equipartition → ⟨E⟩ = 3k_BT per atom. Total: U = 3Nk_BT → C_V = 3Nk_B = 3R per mole ≈ 24.9 J/mol·K. Works at high T (above ~θ_D) but fails completely at low T: diamond at room T has C_V ~ 0.7 J/mol·K, not 24.9!

Einstein's Quantum Solution (1907)

Einstein quantised each oscillator using Planck's hypothesis. All N atoms treated as independent harmonic oscillators at the SAME frequency ω_E.

C_E = dU/dT: Let x = θ_E/T = ℏω_E/k_BT:

L62 Bloch's Theorem — Proof via Translation Operators Week 9▾

The Problem Sommerfeld Left

Sommerfeld set V=0 everywhere. Real crystals have a periodic Coulomb potential V(r) = V(r+R) for every lattice vector R. What form must the eigenstates take? Bloch's theorem (1928) gives the complete answer.

▸ Full Proof of Bloch's Theorem
Define translation operator T_R: T_R·f(r) = f(r+R). Key: V(r+R)=V(r) → [H, T_R]=0
Simultaneous eigenstates: Since [H,T_R]=0, ∃ states that are eigenstates of both: H|ψ⟩=E|ψ⟩ and T_R|ψ⟩=c_R|ψ⟩
Constraint on c_R: T_{R₁+R₂} = T_{R₁}T_{R₂} → c_{R₁+R₂} = c_{R₁}c_{R₂}. Also: ∫|ψ(r+R)|²d³r = ∫|ψ(r)|²d³r → |c_R|²=1 → |c_R|=1
Only solution: c_R = e^{ik·R} for some vector k (exponential is the only function satisfying f(R₁+R₂)=f(R₁)f(R₂) with |f|=1)
Write ψ(r) = e^{ik·r}u(r): Then ψ(r+R) = e^{ik·(r+R)}u(r+R) = e^{ik·R}·e^{ik·r}·u(r+R)
For this to equal c_R·ψ(r) = e^{ik·R}·e^{ik·r}·u(r): need u(r+R) = u(r) ✓
ψ_{nk}(r) = e^{ik·r}·u_{nk}(r) where u_{nk}(r+R) = u_{nk}(r) ← Bloch's Theorem ■
"In free electrons, ℏk gives the momentum of the electron. What about when the wave function is of Bloch form? The answer is: ℏk behaves exactly like the momentum of an electron in a periodic lattice. If I take d/dt of ℏk for an electron moving in a periodic system, this will be equal to the external force — the force applied from outside, for example an electric field. So it is in this way that it behaves like momentum." — Prof. Manoj K. Harbola, Lecture 60
Key consequences of Bloch's theorem
Crystal momentum: ℏk (k defined modulo reciprocal lattice → reduce to 1st BZ) No scattering from perfect lattice: ψ_{nk} is exact eigenstate → propagates freely Band structure: For each k ∈ 1st BZ, multiple eigenvalues E_n(k) labelled by band index n k and k+G are equivalent: all distinct states in 1st BZ only
L63 Bloch II — Why No Scattering from Perfect Lattice & Physical Picture Week 9▾
"We have shown that k within the First Brillouin Zone is enough to specify the wave function. The wave functions ψ_{k+G} and ψ_k are identical. k and k+G are equivalent. And this implies that k can be specified within the First Brillouin Zone." — Prof. Manoj K. Harbola, Lecture 60

Why Metals Have Long Mean Free Paths

A Bloch state is a stationary solution — it neither accelerates nor decelerates spontaneously. An electron in |ψ_{nk}⟩ will remain there forever if the Hamiltonian stays periodic. Only deviations from perfect periodicity scatter electrons:

  • Phonons (thermal vibrations): dominant at high T → ρ ∝ T
  • Impurities and defects: dominant at low T → residual resistivity (T-independent)
  • Electron-electron scattering: sub-dominant → ρ ∝ T² (Fermi liquid)

At 4K, pure Cu has λ ~ mm (not Å!). The Drude model thought λ ~ 25 Å because it didn't know about Bloch's theorem.

L80 Superconductivity II — BCS Theory & Cooper Pairs Week 12▾

Cooper Pairs — Physical Picture

An electron moving through the lattice polarises nearby ions, creating a slight local positive charge density that persists for ~10⁻¹³ s (phonon timescale). A second electron arriving later sees this polarisation and is attracted. This phonon-mediated attractive interaction overcomes the Coulomb repulsion for electrons near E_F, binding them into Cooper pairs: (k↑, −k↓).

BCS Universal Results (1957)
Energy gap: 2Δ(0) = 3.528 k_BT_C (universal ratio) Coherence length: ξ₀ = ℏv_F/(πΔ) ~ 100–1000 nm (Cooper pair size) Penetration depth: λ_L = √(m*/μ₀n_se²) ~ 50–200 nm T_C ~ 1.13·ω_D·exp(−1/N(0)V)   (N(0) = DOS at E_F, V = pairing potential)

Type I vs Type II

Type I: Single critical field H_C. Above H_C, superconductivity destroyed abruptly. κ = λ_L/ξ₀ < 1/√2. Examples: Hg, Pb, Sn. Type II: Two critical fields H_C1 and H_C2. For H_C1 < H < H_C2, flux penetrates in quantised vortices (Abrikosov lattice) while bulk SC survives. κ > 1/√2. Examples: Nb, all high-T_C materials. Practical applications (MRI magnets, maglev) use Type II.

100× smaller because only fraction ~k_BT/E_F ≈ 0.004 of electrons can be thermally excited (need empty state above). Drude assumed all N electrons absorb heat → factor of order k_BT/E_F too large.
State and derive the Wiedemann-Franz law. What is the correct Lorenz number?
κ = (1/3)v_F²τC_ve_vol; σ = ne²τ/m. Ratio: κ/(σT) = v_F²C_ve_vol/(3ne²/m) = 2E_F·(π²nk_B²/(2E_F))/(3ne²) = π²k_B²/(3e²) = L₀.
L₀ = π²k_B²/(3e²) = 2.44×10⁻⁸ W·Ω·K⁻². Universal (n, τ cancel). Drude gives (3/2)(k_B/e)² = 1.24×10⁻⁸ — factor 2 wrong.

II — Crystal Structure & Diffraction

Prove that only n=1,2,3,4,6-fold rotation axes are possible in a crystal lattice.
Rotate adjacent lattice point B (distance t from A) by φ=360°/n about A, and A about B. New points C,D must be lattice points → CD must be integer multiple of t: CD = t+2t cosφ = mt. So 2cosφ = m−1. Since |cosφ|≤1: m ∈{−1,0,1,2,3} → φ = 180°(n=2), 120°(n=3), 90°(n=4), 60°(n=6), 0°(n=1). n=5: cos72°≈0.309 → m≈1.618 not integer → forbidden.
Derive Bragg's law. What are systematic absences and what causes them?
Path difference between rays from adjacent planes = 2d sinθ. Constructive interference: 2d sinθ = nλ.
Systematic absences: structure factor F_hkl = Σf_j exp[2πi(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)] = 0 for certain (hkl). BCC: F = f[1+e^{πi(h+k+l)}] = 0 when h+k+l = odd → (100),(111),(210)... absent. FCC: absent when h,k,l mixed (some odd, some even) → (100),(110),(210)... absent, but (111),(200),(220)... present.
Calculate the packing fraction of FCC. Why is 74% the theoretical maximum for spheres?
FCC: atoms touch along face diagonal → 4r = a√2 → r = a√2/4. n=4. PF = 4×(4π/3)(a√2/4)³/a³ = π/(3√2) ≈ 74.05%.
74% is maximum because FCC/HCP achieve coordination number 12 — no sphere can be simultaneously tangent to more than 12 identical spheres of the same radius. Kepler conjectured this in 1611; proved by T. Hales (1998). Both FCC (ABCABC) and HCP (ABABAB) close-packed stackings achieve this.

III — Phonons & Specific Heat

Derive ω(k) for the 1D monatomic chain. Draw the dispersion and explain acoustic limit, zone boundary, and BZ.
EOM: Mü_n = K(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}−2u_n). Trial u_n = Ae^{i(nka−ωt)}: Mω² = 4K sin²(ka/2) → ω = 2√(K/M)|sin(ka/2)|.
k→0: ω≈a√(K/M)·k (linear, acoustic, v_s = a√(K/M)). k=π/a: ω_max=2√(K/M), v_g=0 (standing wave, Bragg reflection). BZ: ω periodic with period 2π/a → all distinct modes in k∈[−π/a,+π/a]. N unit cells → N k-values.
Derive the diatomic chain dispersion. Explain acoustic vs optical and the forbidden gap.
EOM for M,m atoms → trial waves → (2K−ω²M)A = 2Kcos(ka)B and 2Kcos(ka)A = (2K−ω²m)B. Det=0 → ω² = K(1/M+1/m) ± K√[(1/M+1/m)²−4sin²(ka)/Mm].
Acoustic (−): ω→0 as k→0, atoms move same direction (sound). Optical (+): ω=√[2K(1/M+1/m)] at k=0, atoms move opposite (IR coupling). Gap: √(2K/M) to √(2K/m) — no propagating modes; vanishes when M=m.
Derive the Debye T³ law. Why does it succeed where Einstein fails?
Debye DOS: g(ν)=9Nν²/ν_D³. E = 9Nk_BT(T/θ_D)³∫₀^{x_D} x³/(e^x−1)dx.
Low T (x_D→∞): ∫→π⁴/15. E = (3π⁴Nk_B/5)T⁴/θ_D³ → C_V = (12π⁴Nk_B/5)(T/θ_D)³ ∝ T³. ✓
Einstein fails because it has a single frequency ω_E: at T≪θ_E, no modes with energy ≲k_BT → exponential C_E∝e^{-θ_E/T}. Debye has g(ω)∝ω² → acoustic modes at all ω→0 always excitable at any T>0 → power-law C_V∝T³.

IV — Band Theory & Semiconductors

State and prove Bloch's theorem. What are its physical consequences?
Statement: ψ_{nk}(r) = e^{ik·r}u_{nk}(r) where u is lattice-periodic.
Proof: [H,T_R]=0 → simultaneous eigenstates. |c_R|=1 → c_R=e^{ik·R}. Writing ψ=e^{ik·r}u(r): ψ(r+R)=e^{ik·R}ψ(r) requires u(r+R)=u(r). ■
Consequences: (1) No scattering from perfect lattice (Bloch state is exact eigenstate). (2) Mean free path λ≫a at low T. (3) Crystal momentum ℏk defined (mod ℏG). (4) Energy bands E_n(k) labelled by n,k.
Derive the KP dispersion relation. Explain the three limiting cases and how energy gaps form.
Square-well potential → Schrödinger in each region + Bloch BC + 4 continuity conditions → determinant=0. Dirac comb limit (V₀→∞, b→0, P=mV₀ab/ℏ² finite): (P/αa)sin(αa)+cos(αa)=cos(ka).
Gaps form where |LHS|>1 (no real k). At αa=nπ: sin(nπ)=0 so LHS=cos(nπ)=±1 — right at boundary of gap. The ±1 band edges in reduced zone scheme correspond to Bragg reflection at BZ boundaries.
P=0: free electron; P→∞: atomic levels; general P: bands + gaps.
Define effective mass. Derive it and explain why m*<0 leads to the concept of holes. How does this explain the positive Hall coefficient?
v_g = (1/ℏ)dE/dk. F = ℏ(dk/dt). a = dv_g/dt = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)F. Comparing F=m*a: m* = ℏ²/(d²E/dk²).
Near band top: d²E/dk²<0 → m*<0 → electron decelerates under force. Reinterpret: hole (missing electron) has charge +e, mass m_h*=−m*>0, wavevector k_h=−k_e. Holes drift in direction of E field.
Positive Hall: In Be (2s²), 2s band nearly full → holes dominate → R_H = +1/(n_he) > 0. ✓
Derive the intrinsic carrier concentration n_i for a semiconductor.
n = N_C exp[−(E_C−E_F)/k_BT], p = N_V exp[−(E_F−E_V)/k_BT] (Boltzmann approx).
Mass action: np = N_CN_V exp(−E_g/k_BT) = n_i² (independent of E_F!).
Intrinsic (n=p): E_F ≈ (E_C+E_V)/2. n_i = √(N_CN_V)·exp(−E_g/2k_BT).
Si (E_g=1.1 eV, 300K): n_i ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

V — Magnetism

Derive Langevin's classical theory of paramagnetism and obtain the Curie law.
PE = −μH cosθ. Boltzmann: dn ∝ e^{a cosθ}sinθ dθ, a=μH/k_BT. ⟨cosθ⟩ = coth(a)−1/a = L(a) (Langevin function). M = nμL(a). For a≪1: L(a)≈a/3 → M = nμ²H/(3k_BT). χ = C/T, C = nμ²/(3k_B). ✓
Apply Hund's rules to Fe²⁺ (3d⁶). Find L, S, J. Calculate g_J and μ_eff.
3d⁶ filling (m_ℓ = +2,+1,0,−1,−2): ↑↑↑↑↑↓ (last electron pairs with m_ℓ=+2).
Rule 1: S = 5×½ − 1×½ = 2. Rule 2: L = |2+1+0+(−1)+(−2)+2| = 2. Rule 3: >half-filled → J = L+S = 4. Term: ⁵D₄.
g_J = 1 + [J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)]/(2J(J+1)) = 1+(20+6−6)/40 = 3/2.
μ_eff = (3/2)√[4×5]·μ_B = (3/2)√20·μ_B ≈ 6.71 μ_B. Experiment: ~5.1–5.5 μ_B (orbital quenching in crystal → spin-only ≈ 4.90 μ_B fits better).
Derive the Curie-Weiss law using Weiss molecular field theory. Find T_C.
H_eff = H + λM. Curie law: M = C(H+λM)/T → χ = M/H = C/(T−Cλ) = C/(T−T_C). ← Curie-Weiss. T_C = Cλ = nλg_J²J(J+1)μ_B²/(3k_B). Experimentally: plot 1/χ vs T → straight line; x-intercept = T_C; slope = 1/C.
Why can't magnetic dipole-dipole interactions explain ferromagnetism? What is the exchange interaction?
Dipolar energy E_dip ~ μ₀μ_B²/a³ ~ 10⁻²⁵ J ~ 10⁻⁶ eV → T_dip ~ 0.01 K. Observed T_C(Fe) = 1043 K → factor 10⁶ discrepancy. Required Weiss field ~10⁷ Gauss — impossible magnetically.
Exchange: from Coulomb repulsion + Pauli exclusion. Heitler-London: singlet energy = C+|J_ex|; triplet = C−|J_ex|. Exchange integral J_ex = ∫∫ψ_A*(1)ψ_B*(2)(e²/r₁₂)ψ_A(2)ψ_B(1)dV₁dV₂. Electrostatic origin, scale ~0.1 eV → T_C ~ 1000 K ✓. J_ex>0 → ferro; J_ex<0 → antiferro. H_ex = −2J_ex Σ_{⟨ij⟩}S_i·S_j.
