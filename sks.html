Lecture 1

Entropy

What is Entropy?

Entropy is a real physical quantity — as real as pressure, volume, or temperature — but unlike those it cannot be directly felt. It is denoted by S and has SI units of J/K (joules per kelvin).

🔑 Core Definition
Entropy is a single-valued state function — it depends only on the current state of the system, not on the history of how the system arrived at that state. This makes it a thermodynamic potential in the same class as internal energy U.

Entropy plays three fundamental roles in thermodynamics:

  • Distinguishes adiabatics: There are infinitely many adiabatic processes; entropy gives each one a unique label (its rank or level).
  • Direction of processes: In an isolated system, entropy never decreases (Second Law). It increases for irreversible processes and remains constant for reversible ones.
  • Unavailable energy: Part of the heat absorbed in any process becomes unavailable for conversion to work. Entropy quantifies this unavailability.

The Boltzmann Entropy Formula

Ludwig Boltzmann made the profound connection between entropy and the microscopic description of matter. He showed that entropy is a measure of the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) W consistent with the macroscopic state:

Boltzmann's Entropy (Microscopic Definition)
S = kB ln W
kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann constant) W = thermodynamic probability (number of accessible microstates)
💡 Intuition: Order vs Disorder
A highly ordered state has very few microstates (W is small → S is small). A disordered state has an enormous number of microstates (W is huge → S is large). When entropy increases, the system moves from a less probable (more ordered) to a more probable (more disordered) macrostate. This is why disorder spontaneously increases — it is overwhelmingly more likely. There are vastly more disordered arrangements than ordered ones.

Entropy in a Carnot Cycle — The Origin of the State-Function Property

Consider the P-V diagram showing multiple Carnot cycles operating between different isotherms. SKS Sir's notes (Lec-1) show two Carnot engines operating between temperatures T₁, T₂, T₃ on the same P-V diagram, with heat exchanges Q₁, Q₂, Q₃ along the adiabatics.

Fig 1.1 — Multiple Carnot cycles on a P-V diagram. Each adiabatic line carries a fixed Q. For any Carnot engine: Q/T = const along each adiabatic.

For a Carnot engine between T₁ and T₂, the efficiency gives:

Carnot Heat Ratios
Q₁/Q₂ = T₁/T₂ ⟹ Q₁/T₁ = Q₂/T₂ = Q₃/T₃ = ... = const
The ratio Q/T is the same along any adiabatic line in a Carnot diagram
🔑 Entropy as Unavailable Energy
For a reversible cycle: Σ(Qᵢ/Tᵢ) = 0 and Σδᵢ = 0. This means the ratio dQ/T is an exact differential — its integral is path-independent. This is the entropy function S, defined by dS = dQ_rev / T.

If the adiabatics are closed (form a complete cycle), the condition is:

∮ dQ/T = 0 (reversible cycle) ∮ dS = 0 ⟹ dS = dQ_rev / T is an exact differential

Entropy as Unavailable Energy — Physical Explanation

Consider heat Q₁ absorbed at temperature T₁. The work extractable from a Carnot engine between T₁ and T₂ (the sink) is:

📐 Derivation — Entropy and Unavailable Energy
1
Q₁/T₁ = Q₂/T₂ ⟹ Q₂ = Q₁(T₂/T₁) Carnot relation. Q₂ is the heat rejected to the sink at T₂.
2
W = Q₁ − Q₂ = Q₁(1 − T₂/T₁) Net work = heat absorbed minus heat rejected
3
W = Q₁ − (Q₁/T₁)·T₂ Rewriting: the unavailable part is (Q₁/T₁)·T₂ = S·T₂
4
Unavailable energy = T₂ · ΔS As entropy ΔS increases (more disorder), more energy becomes unavailable for work. This is the thermodynamic price of irreversibility.
⚠ Key Insight: Why Entropy Increases Spontaneously
In any real (irreversible) process, the entropy of an isolated system increases. This is the Second Law. As entropy increases, more and more energy gets "locked up" as unavailable thermal energy. The Universe tends toward states of higher entropy because there are astronomically more disordered microstates than ordered ones — and random processes naturally sample the most probable configurations.
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Entropy
Why is entropy called a "state function" and what are the consequences?
A state function is any thermodynamic quantity whose value depends only on the current state of the system — not on the path taken to reach that state. Entropy is a state function because dS = dQ_rev/T is an exact differential (its cyclic integral is zero for any reversible cycle).

Consequences: (1) ΔS between two states is uniquely defined regardless of the process. (2) We can calculate ΔS along any convenient reversible path, even if the actual process is irreversible. (3) For a spontaneous irreversible process, ΔStotal > 0, but we can still compute ΔS of each subsystem separately and add them.
MNEMONIC: "STATE = SAME THING ALWAYS, TRAJECTORY ERASED" — only endpoint matters
What is the physical meaning of S = k ln W?
W is the thermodynamic probability — the number of distinct microscopic arrangements (microstates) that correspond to the same macroscopic state. Taking the logarithm makes entropy additive: for two independent systems A and B, Wtotal = WA × WB, so Stotal = k ln(WAWB) = k ln WA + k ln WB = SA + SB.

This connects the macroscopic (thermodynamic) and microscopic (statistical) descriptions of matter: entropy is fundamentally about how many ways a system can be arranged at the molecular level consistent with what we observe macroscopically.
MNEMONIC: "BOLTZMANN'S GRAVE" — his tombstone literally has S = k log W inscribed on it
Why is entropy constant in an adiabatic reversible process?
By definition, an adiabatic process has dQ = 0 (no heat exchange with surroundings). For a reversible process, dS = dQ_rev/T = 0/T = 0. Therefore S is constant — the process is isentropic. This is why reversible adiabatics are the same as isentropes, and in an S-T diagram they appear as vertical lines. For an irreversible adiabatic, dS > 0 (entropy increases even though no heat enters).
MNEMONIC: "REVERSIBLE ADIABATIC = ISENTROPIC = VERTICAL ON S-T GRAPH"

Entropy of a Perfect Gas

We derive the entropy change of a perfect gas from first principles using the combined first and second laws. This gives a formula in terms of measurable quantities: temperature and volume (or temperature and pressure).

Derivation from First Principles

📐 Full Derivation — Entropy of a Perfect Gas
1
dQ = dU + P dV (First Law) The heat absorbed equals the increase in internal energy plus work done by the gas. This is the First Law of Thermodynamics for a quasi-static process.
2
dS = dQ_rev / T (Second Law definition of entropy) For a reversible process, the entropy change is dQ/T. For irreversible, dS > dQ/T.
3
dS = dU/T + P dV/T Substituting the First Law: dS = (dU + PdV)/T
4
For perfect gas: dU = Cᵥ dT and P = nRT/V For a perfect gas, internal energy depends only on T (Joule's law), so dU = CᵥdT. The equation of state is PV = nRT.
5
dS = Cᵥ dT/T + (nR/V) dV Substituting dU and PdV = nRT·dV/V into step 3
6
Integrating: S₂ − S₁ = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁) Direct integration from state 1 to state 2. This is the entropy change between two equilibrium states of a perfect gas.
7
Equivalently: ΔS = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + Cₚ ln(V₂/V₁) Using Mayer's relation: Cₚ − Cᵥ = nR (or R for one mole). This form shows entropy depends on two independent variables for a perfect gas.
Entropy Change of a Perfect Gas
S₂ − S₁ = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁) = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + Cₚ ln(V₂/V₁)
Valid for n moles of a perfect gas undergoing any process (reversible or not!) between equilibrium states. The path doesn't matter — S is a state function.
💡 Special Cases
Isothermal (T = const, dT = 0): ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = −nR ln(P₂/P₁). Expansion (V₂ > V₁) increases entropy.

Isochoric (V = const, dV = 0): ΔS = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁). Heating increases entropy.

Isobaric (P = const): Using PV = nRT, V∝T at const P, so dV/V = dT/T → ΔS = (Cᵥ+nR)ln(T₂/T₁) = Cₚ ln(T₂/T₁).

Adiabatic reversible: ΔS = 0 (isentropic). From the formula: Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) = −nR ln(V₂/V₁), which recovers the adiabatic relation T Vᵞ⁻¹ = const.
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Perfect Gas Entropy
Show that entropy is zero for a reversible adiabatic process using the formula.
For a reversible adiabatic: PVᵞ = const, and TV^(γ-1) = const. From the latter: ln(T₂/T₁) = −(γ−1)ln(V₂/V₁) = −(nR/Cᵥ)ln(V₂/V₁). Substituting into ΔS = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁): ΔS = Cᵥ·(−nR/Cᵥ)ln(V₂/V₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁) = −nR ln(V₂/V₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁) = 0 ✓
MNEMONIC: "ADIABATIC REVERSIBLE = S DOESN'T CHANGE" — think of it as the 'zero path' in entropy
Why can we use the entropy formula even for irreversible processes?
Because entropy is a state function. The formula ΔS = Cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁) is derived for reversible processes, but since S depends only on the state (T, V) and not on the path, we can use any convenient reversible path connecting the same initial and final states to compute ΔS. The answer is the same regardless. For an irreversible process, we simply identify the initial and final equilibrium states and compute ΔS between them using the formula — even though the actual process was irreversible.

Thermodynamic Functions: U, H, F, G

The four thermodynamic potentials — Internal Energy U, Enthalpy H, Helmholtz Free Energy F, and Gibbs Free Energy G — are all state functions. Each is most naturally suited to a different set of constraints (variables held fixed).

🔑 Combined First and Second Law
The fundamental thermodynamic identity ties entropy, internal energy, pressure, and volume together:

TdS = dU + PdV  ⟹  dU = TdS − PdV

This single equation is the foundation from which all four potentials are derived using Legendre transforms.

Definitions and Natural Variables

PotentialDefinitionDifferentialNatural VariablesUse Case
U — Internal Energy Fundamental dU = TdS − PdV S, V Isolated system (S, V = const)
H — Enthalpy H = U + PV dH = TdS + VdP S, P Constant pressure (chemical reactions)
F — Helmholtz Free Energy F = U − TS dF = −PdV − SdT T, V Constant T, V (statistical mechanics)
G — Gibbs Free Energy G = U − TS + PV dG = −SdT + VdP T, P Const T, P (spontaneity, equilibrium)

Deriving the Differentials

📐 Enthalpy H = U + PV
1
H = U + PV (definition) Enthalpy is internal energy plus the "flow work" PV.
2
dH = dU + PdV + VdP Product rule: d(PV) = PdV + VdP
3
dU = TdS − PdV (substituting the fundamental relation) Use the combined first+second law
4
dH = TdS − PdV + PdV + VdP = TdS + VdP ✓ The PdV terms cancel. Result: dH = TdS + VdP. Natural variables of H are (S, P).
📐 Helmholtz Free Energy F = U − TS
1
F = U − TS (definition) F represents the maximum work extractable from a system at constant T,V. The "TS" term is the unavailable energy.
2
dF = dU − TdS − SdT Product rule: d(TS) = TdS + SdT
3
dF = (TdS − PdV) − TdS − SdT = −PdV − SdT ✓ Substituting dU = TdS − PdV; the TdS terms cancel. Natural variables: (T, V)
📐 Gibbs Free Energy G = U − TS + PV = H − TS = F + PV
1
G = U − TS + PV (definition) G is the Legendre transform of U with respect to both T and P. It is most useful for processes at constant T and P (e.g., chemical reactions in the lab).
2
dG = dU − TdS − SdT + PdV + VdP Full differential of G = U − TS + PV
3
dG = (TdS − PdV) − TdS − SdT + PdV + VdP Substituting dU = TdS − PdV
4
dG = −SdT + VdP ✓ TdS and PdV terms both cancel. Natural variables of G are (T, P) — the most experimentally convenient!

Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

🌟 The Most Important Results of Thermodynamics
At constant T and P (the conditions of almost every chemical and biological process):

ΔG < 0: Process is spontaneous (proceeds without external input)
ΔG > 0: Process is non-spontaneous (requires external work)
ΔG = 0: System is at equilibrium (no net driving force)

G also measures the maximum non-PV work extractable from a system at constant T, P (e.g., electrical work in a battery).
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Thermodynamic Functions
What is the physical significance of each thermodynamic potential?
U (Internal Energy): Total energy stored in the system. Decrease in U equals work done + heat lost at constant S, V. Natural for isolated systems.

H (Enthalpy): U + PV. At constant pressure, ΔH = Q (heat absorbed). This is why enthalpy is used in calorimetry and chemistry. H also equals the heat absorbed in a reversible constant-pressure process.

F (Helmholtz Free Energy): Maximum work obtainable from the system at constant T, V. The "free" energy that can actually be used for work. ΔF = W_max (isothermal).

G (Gibbs Free Energy): Maximum non-PV work at constant T, P. Most useful for chemistry and biology since experiments are usually done at constant T (room temperature) and P (atmospheric pressure).
MNEMONIC: "UGLY HOUNDS FREQUENTLY GROWL" → U, H, F, G in order
Derive dH = dU + PdV + VdP and state when ΔH = Q.
H = U + PV → dH = dU + d(PV) = dU + PdV + VdP. From First Law: dQ = dU + PdV. Therefore dH = dQ + VdP. At constant pressure (dP = 0): dH = dQ → ΔH = Q. So the heat absorbed at constant pressure equals the enthalpy change — this is why ΔH is so fundamental to chemistry: most reactions happen open to the atmosphere (constant P).

Maxwell's Relations

Maxwell's relations are a set of four partial-derivative equalities derived from the exactness of the differentials of the four thermodynamic potentials. They are among the most powerful tools in thermodynamics, allowing us to relate quantities that are hard to measure (like entropy changes with volume) to easily measurable quantities (like pressure changes with temperature).

The Exactness Condition (Mathematical Basis)

If dx = M dy + N dz is an exact differential (meaning x is a state function), then by Clairaut's theorem (equality of mixed partial derivatives):

Condition for Exactness (Euler Criterion)
dx = M dy + N dz is exact ⟺ (∂M/∂z)_y = (∂N/∂y)_z
This follows because if x = x(y,z), then M = (∂x/∂y)_z and N = (∂x/∂z)_y, and since mixed partials commute: ∂²x/∂z∂y = ∂²x/∂y∂z

Deriving All Four Maxwell Relations

📐 Maxwell's Relations from the Four Exact Differentials
I
dU = TdS − PdV ⟹ (∂T/∂V)_S = −(∂P/∂S)_V From dU: M=T (coefficient of dS), N=−P (coefficient of dV). Applying exactness: (∂T/∂V)_S = ∂(−P)/∂S)_V → Maxwell I
II
dH = TdS + VdP ⟹ (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P From dH: M=T (coeff of dS), N=V (coeff of dP). Exactness: (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P → Maxwell II
III
dF = −PdV − SdT ⟹ (∂P/∂T)_V = (∂S/∂V)_T From dF: M=−P (coeff of dV), N=−S (coeff of dT). Exactness: ∂(−P)/∂T)_V = ∂(−S)/∂V)_T → (∂P/∂T)_V = (∂S/∂V)_T → Maxwell III
IV
dG = −SdT + VdP ⟹ −(∂S/∂P)_T = (∂V/∂T)_P From dG: M=−S (coeff of dT), N=V (coeff of dP). Exactness: ∂(−S)/∂P)_T = (∂V/∂T)_P → Maxwell IV
The Four Maxwell Relations (Summary)
(∂T/∂V)_S = −(∂P/∂S)_V ... (I) from dU (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P ... (II) from dH (∂P/∂T)_V = (∂S/∂V)_T ... (III) from dF ← most used −(∂S/∂P)_T = (∂V/∂T)_P ... (IV) from dG ← most used
Relations III and IV are the most practically useful — they relate entropy derivatives to P-V-T quantities that can be measured directly.

Applying Maxwell III: Isothermal Expansion and Entropy

Maxwell III — (∂P/∂T)_V = (∂S/∂V)_T — is one of the most useful relations. It allows us to compute how entropy changes with volume at constant temperature (impossible to measure directly) from the pressure-temperature dependence (easily measured).

💡 Physical Meaning of Maxwell III
Multiplying both sides by T: T(∂P/∂T)_V = T(∂S/∂V)_T = (∂Q/∂V)_T. This says: in isothermal expansion, the heat absorbed per unit volume increase equals T times the pressure coefficient (∂P/∂T)_V. For an ideal gas, P = RT/V, so ∂P/∂T = R/V, and T(∂P/∂T)_V = RT/V = P → (∂U/∂V)_T = 0 (Joule's law!).
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Maxwell's Relations
State all four Maxwell relations and specify which potential each comes from.
(I) from U: (∂T/∂V)_S = −(∂P/∂S)_V
(II) from H: (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P
(III) from F: (∂P/∂T)_V = (∂S/∂V)_T ← Most commonly used
(IV) from G: −(∂S/∂P)_T = (∂V/∂T)_P ← Most commonly used
MNEMONIC: "GOOD PHYSICISTS HAVE STUDIED UNDER VERY FINE TEACHERS" → G(dG), P(-S,V), H(dH), S(T,V), U(dU), V(-P), F(dF), T(-P,-S)
How do Maxwell's relations help measure entropy changes experimentally?
Entropy cannot be measured directly — there is no "entropymeter." But Maxwell's relations equate entropy derivatives to P-V-T derivatives, which CAN be measured. For example: (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V (Maxwell III). The right side can be found from the equation of state: just measure pressure at two temperatures with volume held constant. Similarly, (∂S/∂P)_T = −(∂V/∂T)_P (Maxwell IV) gives the entropy change with pressure from the thermal expansion coefficient. This is how calorimetric and P-V-T data are combined to construct complete entropy tables for real substances.
MNEMONIC: "MAXWELL CONVERTS UNMEASURABLE TO MEASURABLE"

The Three TdS Equations

The TdS equations (also called the "specific heat equations") express TdS in terms of the different pairs of independent variables: (T,V), (T,P), and (P,V). They are extremely useful for calculating heat exchanges in terms of measurable quantities.

The key idea: entropy S is a state function. We can write S as a function of any two independent variables and expand dS as a total differential, then multiply through by T.

First TdS Equation — S = S(T, V)

📐 Derivation of First TdS Equation
1
S = S(T, V) → dS = (∂S/∂T)_V dT + (∂S/∂V)_T dV Total differential of S treating T and V as independent variables.
2
TdS = T(∂S/∂T)_V dT + T(∂S/∂V)_T dV Multiply through by T.
3
Cᵥ = T(∂S/∂T)_V (definition of heat capacity at constant V) At constant V (dV=0): TdS = dQ = CᵥdT → Cᵥ = T(∂S/∂T)_V
4
T(∂S/∂V)_T = T(∂P/∂T)_V (by Maxwell III) Maxwell relation: (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V
5
TdS = Cᵥ dT + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV ← FIRST TdS EQUATION ✓ Final result. Physically: the heat absorbed = Cᵥ×temperature change + work done against intermolecular forces during expansion.

Second TdS Equation — S = S(T, P)

📐 Derivation of Second TdS Equation
1
S = S(T, P) → dS = (∂S/∂T)_P dT + (∂S/∂P)_T dP Total differential treating T and P as independent variables.
2
Cₚ = T(∂S/∂T)_P (heat capacity at constant P) At constant P: TdS = dQ = CₚdT → Cₚ = T(∂S/∂T)_P
3
T(∂S/∂P)_T = −T(∂V/∂T)_P (by Maxwell IV) Maxwell IV: (∂S/∂P)_T = −(∂V/∂T)_P
4
TdS = Cₚ dT − T(∂V/∂T)_P dP ← SECOND TdS EQUATION ✓ Physically: heat absorbed = Cₚ×temp change − T×(thermal expansion coeff)×pressure change

Third TdS Equation — S = S(P, V)

📐 Derivation of Third TdS Equation
1
S = S(P, V) → dS = (∂S/∂P)_V dP + (∂S/∂V)_P dV Total differential treating P and V as independent variables.
2
TdS = T(∂S/∂P)_V dP + T(∂S/∂V)_P dV Multiply by T
3
(∂S/∂P)_V = (∂S/∂T)_V (∂T/∂P)_V = (Cᵥ/T)(∂T/∂P)_V Chain rule: (∂S/∂P)_V = (∂S/∂T)_V × (∂T/∂P)_V. Using Cᵥ/T = (∂S/∂T)_V
4
(∂S/∂V)_P = (∂S/∂T)_P (∂T/∂V)_P = (Cₚ/T)(∂T/∂V)_P Similarly using Cₚ/T = (∂S/∂T)_P
5
TdS = Cᵥ(∂T/∂P)_V dP + Cₚ(∂T/∂V)_P dV ← THIRD TdS EQUATION ✓ This form is useful when P and V are the natural control variables.
Summary — Three TdS Equations
TdS = Cᵥ dT + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV ... (1st) [S = S(T,V)] TdS = Cₚ dT − T(∂V/∂T)_P dP ... (2nd) [S = S(T,P)] TdS = Cᵥ(∂T/∂P)_V dP + Cₚ(∂T/∂V)_P dV ... (3rd) [S = S(P,V)]
All three are exact — they represent the same physical quantity (heat exchange) in different variable choices.
📝 Important Questions & Answers — TdS Equations
Derive the 1st TdS equation and explain the physical meaning of each term.
Derivation: Write S = S(T,V) → dS = (∂S/∂T)_V dT + (∂S/∂V)_T dV. Multiply by T: TdS = Cᵥ dT + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV (using Cᵥ = T(∂S/∂T)_V and Maxwell III).

Physical meanings:
• Cᵥ dT: Heat required to raise the temperature by dT at constant volume. This is the familiar "sensible heat."
• T(∂P/∂T)_V dV: Heat absorbed when the gas expands by dV at constant temperature. For an ideal gas, this term equals P dV — the work done. For a real gas, it has an extra contribution from changing intermolecular forces.
MNEMONIC: "TdS ONE = Cv CHANGES T, MAXWELL CHANGES V"
Using the 2nd TdS equation, find how the temperature of a gas changes during an adiabatic process.
For an adiabatic process: TdS = dQ = 0 (no heat exchange). Applying the 2nd TdS equation: 0 = Cₚ dT − T(∂V/∂T)_P dP. Therefore: Cₚ dT = T(∂V/∂T)_P dP → (dT/dP)_S = T(∂V/∂T)_P / Cₚ. For an ideal gas: (∂V/∂T)_P = nR/P, so (dT/dP)_S = nRT/(PsCₚ) = V/Cₚ. This is the "adiabatic lapse rate" — how temperature drops as pressure decreases (e.g., rising air in the atmosphere). It shows that temperature and pressure always increase/decrease together in an adiabatic process.

Variation of Cᵥ and Cₚ, and Cₚ − Cᵥ

Variation of Cᵥ with Volume

From the 1st TdS equation, since dS is an exact differential, applying the Euler criterion:

📐 Variation of Cᵥ with Volume
1
1st TdS: dS = (Cᵥ/T)dT + (∂P/∂T)_V dV Dividing the 1st TdS equation by T
2
Since dS is exact: ∂/∂V(Cᵥ/T)_T = ∂/∂T(∂P/∂T)_V)_V Euler's condition for exact differentials: cross-partial derivatives must be equal
3
(1/T)(∂Cᵥ/∂V)_T = ∂²P/∂T²)_V T is constant in the left derivative, so it comes out
4
(∂Cᵥ/∂V)_T = T(∂²P/∂T²)_V ← KEY RESULT Cᵥ changes with volume only if pressure has a non-zero second derivative with temperature.
5
For perfect gas: P = nRT/V → ∂P/∂T = nR/V → ∂²P/∂T² = 0 ∴ (∂Cᵥ/∂V)_T = 0 ✓ (Cᵥ independent of volume for perfect gas) Perfect gas: all derivatives of nR/V with respect to T are zero, confirming Joule's law
6
For van der Waals gas: P = RT/(v−b) − a/v² ∂²P/∂T² = 0 also! → (∂Cᵥ/∂V)_T = 0 Even for van der Waals gas, ∂P/∂T = R/(v−b) is independent of T, so ∂²P/∂T² = 0. Cᵥ is independent of volume for both perfect and van der Waals gases.
🌟 Important Result: Cᵥ is Volume-Independent for Both Perfect and Van der Waals Gases
This is a non-trivial result! It means Cᵥ depends only on temperature for these gases — not on how compressed or expanded they are. The physical reason: in the van der Waals equation, the intermolecular forces (represented by 'a') affect the internal energy but do so in a way that doesn't alter how Cᵥ changes with volume.

Variation of Cₚ with Pressure

📐 Variation of Cₚ with Pressure
1
2nd TdS: dS = (Cₚ/T)dT − (∂V/∂T)_P dP Dividing the 2nd TdS equation by T
2
Exactness: ∂/∂P(Cₚ/T)_T = −∂/∂T(∂V/∂T)_P)_P Euler criterion applied to the exact differential dS
3
(∂Cₚ/∂P)_T = −T(∂²V/∂T²)_P ← KEY RESULT Cₚ changes with pressure if the volume has a non-zero second derivative with temperature at constant pressure.
4
For perfect gas: PV = RT → V = RT/P → ∂V/∂T = R/P → ∂²V/∂T² = 0 ∴ (∂Cₚ/∂P)_T = 0 (Cₚ independent of P for perfect gas) Confirms perfect gas result — Cₚ depends only on T, not P.
5
For van der Waals gas: (∂Cₚ/∂P)_T = −T(∂²V/∂T²)_P = +2aT/RT² × something ≠ 0 For a real gas, Cₚ does vary with pressure — this is measurable and important for high-pressure engineering.

Cₚ − Cᵥ: General Derivation

The difference between the heat capacities at constant pressure and constant volume can be derived from the TdS equations. This is one of the most important results in thermodynamics.

📐 Derivation of Cₚ − Cᵥ
1
From 1st TdS (eq. I): TdS = Cᵥ dT + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV First TdS equation
2
From 2nd TdS (eq. II): TdS = Cₚ dT − T(∂V/∂T)_P dP Second TdS equation
3
Subtract I from II: 0 = (Cₚ−Cᵥ)dT − T{(∂V/∂T)_P dP + (∂P/∂T)_V dV} Equating the two expressions for TdS and rearranging. Note TdS cancels.
4
At constant pressure (dP = 0): (Cₚ−Cᵥ)dT = T(∂V/∂T)_P dP + T(∂P/∂T)_V dV Setting dP = 0 in the equation above isolates the Cₚ−Cᵥ term
5
At constant P: set dP = 0, so: (Cₚ−Cᵥ)dT = T(∂P/∂T)_V dV|_P = T(∂P/∂T)_V (∂V/∂T)_P dT Since at constant P, dV = (∂V/∂T)_P dT
6
Cₚ − Cᵥ = T(∂V/∂T)_P (∂P/∂T)_V ← GENERAL RESULT ✓ This is the exact general result for any substance. It relates Cₚ−Cᵥ to measurable P-V-T properties.
7
For perfect gas: (∂V/∂T)_P = R/P, (∂P/∂T)_V = R/V Cₚ − Cᵥ = T·(R/P)·(R/V) = R·T/(PV) = R·(RT)/(RT) = R Using PV = RT (per mole). Famous Mayer's relation: Cₚ − Cᵥ = R ≈ 8.314 J/mol·K
8
For van der Waals gas: Cₚ − Cᵥ = R(1 + 2a/(vRT)) Since v−b ≈ v for small b, the correction comes from the 'a' term (intermolecular attractions). The difference is always greater than R for a real gas below the Boyle temperature.
Summary — Cₚ − Cᵥ
General: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T(∂V/∂T)_P · (∂P/∂T)_V Perfect gas: Cₚ − Cᵥ = R (Mayer's relation) VdW gas: Cₚ − Cᵥ = R(1 + 2a/vRT) ≥ R Note: Cₚ > Cᵥ always (since T, (∂V/∂T)_P², (−∂V/∂P)_T > 0)

Variation of Internal Energy U with Volume

📐 (∂U/∂V)_T — Internal Pressure
1
From: dU = TdS − PdV → (∂U/∂V)_T = T(∂S/∂V)_T − P Differentiate dU with respect to V at constant T
2
By Maxwell III: (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V Maxwell relation from dF
3
(∂U/∂V)_T = T(∂P/∂T)_V − P ← INTERNAL PRESSURE ✓ This is called the "internal pressure" — it measures how the internal energy changes with volume at constant temperature, reflecting intermolecular forces.
4
Perfect gas: P=RT/V → T(∂P/∂T)_V = T·R/V = P → (∂U/∂V)_T = P − P = 0 ✓ Joule's law: internal energy of a perfect gas is independent of volume (no intermolecular forces)
5
VdW gas: P = RT/(v−b) − a/v² → T(∂P/∂T)_V = RT/(v−b) (∂U/∂V)_T = RT/(v−b) − [RT/(v−b) − a/v²] = a/v² The internal pressure for a van der Waals gas is a/v² — positive, representing the attractive interactions between molecules.
🌟 Physical Meaning: (∂U/∂V)_T = a/v² for Van der Waals Gas
As a van der Waals gas expands isothermally (at constant T), the molecules move apart against intermolecular attractions (represented by the constant 'a'). Work must be done against these attractions, so internal energy U increases. The rate of increase is a/v² per unit volume increase. This is positive (energy input required) confirming molecules attract each other. For a perfect gas, a = 0 and no energy is needed for isothermal expansion — U stays constant (Joule's law).
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Heat Capacities
Why is Cₚ always greater than Cᵥ? Give a physical explanation.
Mathematical: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T(∂V/∂T)_P(∂P/∂T)_V. All factors are positive for any stable system: T > 0 (absolute temperature), (∂V/∂T)_P > 0 (most substances expand when heated at constant P), and (∂P/∂T)_V > 0 (pressure increases when heated at constant V). Hence Cₚ − Cᵥ > 0 always.

Physical: When you heat gas at constant pressure, you supply heat for two purposes: (1) raising the temperature (same as Cᵥ), and (2) doing work PdV to push back the atmosphere as the gas expands. At constant volume, you only supply heat for purpose (1). So Cₚ always needs more heat input per degree of temperature rise than Cᵥ.
MNEMONIC: "PRESSURE NEEDS EXTRA HEAT FOR EXPANSION WORK"
Derive Mayer's relation Cₚ − Cᵥ = R from first principles for a perfect gas.
Use the general result: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T(∂V/∂T)_P(∂P/∂T)_V. For a perfect gas PV = RT (per mole):
(∂V/∂T)_P = R/P and (∂P/∂T)_V = R/V
Therefore: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T × (R/P) × (R/V) = TR²/(PV) = TR²/(RT) = R ✓
R = 8.314 J/mol·K is the universal gas constant. This is Mayer's relation, derived in 1842, which historically provided one of the first determinations of the mechanical equivalent of heat.
MNEMONIC: "MAYER'S EQUATION: Cp MINUS Cv EQUALS R" — the R reminds you it's the gas constant and a perfect gas result

First-Order Phase Transitions & Clausius–Clapeyron Equation

A first-order phase transition is a change of phase (solid↔liquid↔gas) occurring at constant temperature and pressure. Examples: melting, boiling, sublimation. The key feature: there is a latent heat — heat is absorbed or released at the transition without any temperature change.

Condition for Phase Equilibrium — Equal Gibbs Potentials

Consider a system of mass m₁ of liquid in equilibrium with mass m₂ of vapour. The total Gibbs free energy is:

📐 Deriving the Phase Equilibrium Condition
1
G_total = m₁g₁ + m₂g₂ (where g₁, g₂ are specific Gibbs potentials per unit mass) Total Gibbs free energy is additive for the two phases
2
For 1st order transition at const T, P: G = const → dG = 0 At constant T and P, G is minimised at equilibrium (dG = 0)
3
dG = dm₁g₁ + dm₂g₂ = 0 In equilibrium: dm₁ = −dm₂ (conservation of mass) → g₁ = g₂ ← Phase Equilibrium Condition If mass transfers from phase 1 to phase 2 (dm₂ = −dm₁), then G is stationary only when g₁ = g₂
🔑 Condition for Phase Equilibrium
At a first-order phase transition, the specific Gibbs free energy is equal in both phases: g₁ = g₂. Equivalently, the chemical potential μ₁ = μ₂. This is the thermodynamic condition for two phases to coexist in equilibrium.

Clausius–Clapeyron Equation — Full Derivation

Fig 7.1 — Phase diagram with triple point. The fusion, vaporisation, and sublimation curves meet at the triple point. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation gives the slope dP/dT of each curve. For water, the fusion curve has negative slope (dP/dT < 0) because ice is less dense than water (V₂ < V₁).
📐 Full Derivation — Clausius–Clapeyron Equation
1
At T: g_liquid = g_vapour (phase equilibrium) The Gibbs potential per unit mass is equal in both phases at temperature T and pressure P
2
At T+dT: (g_l + dg_l) = (g_g + dg_g) → dg_l = dg_g At a slightly different temperature T+dT (with corresponding pressure P+dP), equilibrium must still hold
3
dg = dG/m = d(U−TS+PV)/m = −s dT + v dP For specific quantities (per unit mass): dg = −s dT + v dP. Here s = specific entropy, v = specific volume.
4
−s_l dT + v_l dP = −s_g dT + v_g dP Equating dg_l = dg_g: writing out both differentials
5
(s_g − s_l) dT = (v_g − v_l) dP Rearranging: collecting entropy terms on left, volume terms on right
6
dP/dT = (s_g − s_l)/(v_g − v_l) The slope of the coexistence curve in the P-T diagram
7
Latent heat: L = T(s_g − s_l) [since dQ = TdS = L at phase transition] The latent heat L is the heat absorbed per unit mass during the phase change at constant T. It equals T times the entropy difference.
8
dP/dT = L / [T(v_g − v_l)] ← CLAUSIUS–CLAPEYRON EQUATION ✓ The slope of the P-T coexistence curve equals the latent heat divided by T times the volume change at the transition.
Clausius–Clapeyron Equation
dP/dT = L / [T(V_g − V_l)] L = latent heat per unit mass T = temperature of the transition V_g = specific volume of vapour (gas) V_l = specific volume of liquid
This equation gives the slope of the P-T coexistence curve (phase boundary). It determines how the boiling/melting point changes with pressure.

Application: Melting of Ice — Why Ice Has Negative Slope

For the ice-water transition: V_ice < V_water (water expands upon freezing — ice is less dense). Therefore:

V_g = V_ice < V_l = V_water V_g − V_l < 0 dP/dT = L/[T(V_g−V_l)] < 0 (negative slope)
The melting point of ice DECREASES when pressure increases. Ice under high pressure melts at a lower temperature. This is why ice skating is possible (though pressure melting is a small effect; friction is the main mechanism).
💡 Physical Meaning of Clausius–Clapeyron
The equation tells us: the slope of the P-T coexistence curve is determined by the ratio of latent heat to the volume change at the transition.

• Large L (large latent heat) → steep curve → boiling point changes rapidly with pressure. Example: water has large L_vap = 2260 J/g, so boiling point changes quickly with altitude.
• Small ΔV → steeper curve → harder to change the transition pressure with temperature.
• For an ideal gas approximation (V_g ≫ V_l, V_g ≈ RT/P): d(ln P)/d(1/T) = −L/R — this is the Clausius-Clapeyron equation in integrated form.
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Clausius–Clapeyron
Explain the triple point using the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.
The triple point is the unique temperature and pressure at which all three phases (solid, liquid, gas) coexist in equilibrium. At this point, all three coexistence curves (fusion, vaporisation, sublimation) intersect.

The existence of the triple point of water (273.16 K, 611.73 Pa) can be understood from the fact that as pressure decreases, the boiling point decreases (from Clausius-Clapeyron: dP/dT > 0 for vaporisation curve) while the melting point increases (dP/dT < 0 for fusion). At sufficiently low pressure, the boiling and melting points coincide — this is the triple point. At any lower pressure, liquid water cannot exist at all.
MNEMONIC: "TRIPLE POINT = THREE CURVES MEET, THREE PHASES GREET"
Water boils at 100°C at 1 atm but at ~70°C at altitude. Explain using Clausius–Clapeyron.
At high altitude, atmospheric pressure P is lower. For the liquid-vapour coexistence curve, dP/dT = L/[T(V_g−V_l)] > 0 (positive slope). A lower P corresponds to a lower T on the coexistence curve — so the boiling point decreases. Using the integrated form: ln(P₂/P₁) = (L/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). At ~5000m altitude, P ≈ 0.54 atm, giving T_boil ≈ 83°C. At Everest (P ≈ 0.33 atm), water boils at ~70°C — insufficient to cook many foods properly.

Joule–Thomson Effect (Adiabatic Throttling)

The Joule–Thomson effect describes what happens when a gas is forced through a porous plug (or throttle valve) from high pressure to low pressure, under thermally insulated (adiabatic) conditions. This is the physical basis of most industrial refrigeration and liquefaction of gases.

The Apparatus and Observations

Fig 8.1 — Joule–Thomson apparatus. Gas is pushed through a porous plug from high pressure P₁ to low pressure P₂ by pistons. The system is thermally insulated. Key result: Enthalpy H is conserved (isenthalpic process).

J-T is an Isenthalpic Process — Proof

📐 Proof that H = const in J-T Process
1
Work done ON gas on left: W₁ = P₁V₁ (piston pushes volume V₁ at pressure P₁) The left piston does work P₁V₁ on the gas, pushing it through the plug.
2
Work done BY gas on right: W₂ = P₂V₂ (gas pushes piston by volume V₂ at P₂) The gas does work P₂V₂ on the right piston as it expands.
3
Net work done ON gas: W_net = P₁V₁ − P₂V₂ Total work = work received − work done
4
Adiabatic: dQ = 0 → ΔU = W_net = P₁V₁ − P₂V₂ By First Law: ΔU = Q + W. With Q=0: U₂ − U₁ = P₁V₁ − P₂V₂
5
U₂ + P₂V₂ = U₁ + P₁V₁ → H₂ = H₁ → H = const ✓ Since H = U + PV, we have H₂ = H₁. The Joule-Thomson process is ISENTHALPIC.

The Joule–Thomson Coefficient — Full Derivation

📐 Deriving the J-T Coefficient μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H
1
Since H = const: dH = 0 dH = TdS + VdP = Cₚ dT − T(∂V/∂T)_P dP + VdP Start from dH = TdS + VdP and substitute the 2nd TdS equation for TdS
2
dH = Cₚ dT − {T(∂V/∂T)_P − V} dP Collecting terms
3
Setting dH = 0: Cₚ dT = {T(∂V/∂T)_P − V} dP For the isenthalpic process, dH = 0
4
μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H = (1/Cₚ){T(∂V/∂T)_P − V} ← J-T COEFFICIENT ✓ The J-T coefficient gives the temperature change per unit pressure drop. Positive μ → cooling; negative μ → heating.
Joule–Thomson Coefficient
μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H = (1/Cₚ){T(∂V/∂T)_P − V} For perfect gas: (∂V/∂T)_P = nR/P = V/T → T(∂V/∂T)_P = V ∴ μ_JT = (1/Cₚ){V − V} = 0 (no J-T effect for perfect gas) For real gas (VdW): μ_JT = (1/Cₚ){2a/RT − b} (approx.)
• μ_JT > 0: cooling upon expansion (T decreases as P decreases) — refrigeration! • μ_JT < 0: heating upon expansion — occurs at high T • μ_JT = 0: no temperature change — only for perfect gas, or at the inversion temperature

Inversion Temperature — Where J-T Changes Sign

📐 Inversion Temperature for Van der Waals Gas
1
At inversion: μ_JT = 0 → T(∂V/∂T)_P = V The J-T coefficient changes sign at the inversion temperature Tᵢ
2
For VdW gas: 0 = (1/Cₚ){2a/RT − b} → 2a/RT_i = b Setting μ_JT = 0 and solving for T
3
T_i = 2a/(bR) ← INVERSION TEMPERATURE ✓ Above T_i: J-T effect is heating. Below T_i: J-T effect is cooling. For N₂: T_i ≈ 621 K; for H₂: T_i ≈ 202 K; for He: T_i ≈ 40 K.
⚠ The Anomaly of H₂ and He at Room Temperature
At room temperature (≈300K): Hydrogen (T_i ≈ 202K) is already ABOVE its inversion temperature, so it HEATS UP when throttled — the opposite of most gases! Helium (T_i ≈ 40K) also heats up at room temperature. This is why H₂ and He must first be pre-cooled below their inversion temperatures before throttling can be used to liquefy them. Most other gases (N₂, O₂, air) have inversion temperatures well above room temperature and cool upon throttling.

J-T Effect as Deviation from Boyle's and Joule's Laws

From SKS Sir's notes (Lec-3): The J-T effect arises from the combination of deviations from both Boyle's law and Joule's law:

J-T Coefficient in terms of deviations
Cₚ(∂T/∂P)_H = T(∂V/∂T)_P − V = −T(∂S/∂P)_T − V [by Maxwell IV] = −[T(∂S/∂P)_T + V] = −[(∂U/∂P)_T + P(∂V/∂P)_T + V] ← this involves deviations from BOTH laws: • (∂U/∂P)_T ≠ 0 → deviation from Joule's law (real intermolecular forces) • ∂(PV)/∂P ≠ 0 → deviation from Boyle's law (real gas equation of state)
The J-T effect is zero for a perfect gas precisely because a perfect gas obeys both Joule's law and Boyle's law exactly.
📝 Important Questions & Answers — Joule–Thomson Effect
Prove that the Joule-Thomson expansion is isenthalpic. What is the significance?
Proof: The apparatus has adiabatic walls (dQ = 0). Work done ON gas by left piston = P₁V₁. Work done BY gas on right piston = P₂V₂. By the First Law: ΔU = U₂ − U₁ = P₁V₁ − P₂V₂. Rearranging: U₂ + P₂V₂ = U₁ + P₁V₁ → H₂ = H₁ → H = const.

Significance: The isenthalpic nature means the process is NOT isentropic (entropy increases for a real gas — the process is irreversible). It also means the temperature change depends entirely on how H relates to T at constant H — this is measured by the J-T coefficient. For industrial liquefaction, this is exploited: throttle a gas below its inversion temperature, and it cools; repeat in a cycle and eventually it liquefies.
MNEMONIC: "JT = JUST THROTTLE, HOLDS H CONSTANT"
Why is the J-T coefficient zero for a perfect gas?
μ_JT = (1/Cₚ){T(∂V/∂T)_P − V}. For a perfect gas: PV = nRT → V = nRT/P → (∂V/∂T)_P = nR/P → T(∂V/∂T)_P = nRT/P = V. Therefore μ_JT = (1/Cₚ){V−V} = 0.

Physical reason: A perfect gas has no intermolecular forces. When it expands, no work is done against attractive forces, so no energy is needed from the kinetic energy of the molecules — temperature doesn't change. The J-T effect is entirely due to the real gas deviations (the 'a' and 'b' constants in van der Waals equation).
What is the inversion temperature and why is it important for gas liquefaction?
The inversion temperature T_i = 2a/(bR) is the temperature above which throttling causes heating (μ_JT < 0) and below which it causes cooling (μ_JT > 0).

For liquefaction: gas must be cooled BELOW T_i before throttling can be used to liquefy it. Most industrial gases (N₂, O₂, air) have T_i well above room temperature, so they can be directly throttled to liquefy. H₂ (T_i ≈ 202K) and He (T_i ≈ 40K) must first be pre-cooled by other means — e.g., H₂ is pre-cooled using liquid N₂, and He is pre-cooled using liquid H₂ — before J-T throttling cools them further to liquefaction.
MNEMONIC: "ABOVE INVERSION = HEATING, BELOW = COOLING" — think of it as an 'inversion' of behavior

Phase Transitions — Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

Gibbs Free Energy Criterion for Spontaneity

Recall: G = H − TS or equivalently G = U + PV − TS. At constant T and P:

🌟 The Gibbs Criterion
ΔG < 0: Process is spontaneous (thermodynamically favorable)
ΔG > 0: Process is non-spontaneous (requires external work)
ΔG = 0: System is at thermodynamic equilibrium

Physical meaning: G = H − TS. A process is spontaneous if it decreases enthalpy (exothermic, ΔH < 0) OR increases entropy (TΔS > 0), or both. If one opposes the other, the sign of ΔG determines which effect wins. At high T, the TS term dominates → entropy-driven processes. At low T, the H term dominates → energy-driven processes.

Total Differential of G and Its Meaning

Gibbs Free Energy — Natural Variables (T, P)
G = H − TS = U + PV − TS dG = −SdT + VdP ⟹ S = −(∂G/∂T)_P ← entropy from G ⟹ V = (∂G/∂P)_T ← volume from G
These relations show G is a "generating function" — all thermodynamic properties can be derived from it once G(T,P) is known.

G and Maximum Work

🔑 G as Maximum Non-PV Work
At constant T and P: dW_max = −dG. This means: the maximum useful (non-expansion) work that a system can do in a process at constant T, P equals the decrease in Gibbs free energy. In electrochemistry: ΔG = −nFE, where n = moles of electrons, F = Faraday constant, E = cell EMF. The battery voltage is directly related to the Gibbs free energy change of the electrochemical reaction.

Phase Stability and the Common-Tangent Rule

Fig 9.1 — Gibbs free energy G vs temperature T at constant P for the three phases. Since dG = −SdT + VdP, the slope (∂G/∂T)_P = −S. The phase with lowest G at each T is the stable phase. Transitions occur where two G curves cross: at T_melt (solid-liquid) and T_boil (liquid-gas). The gas has the steepest negative slope because S_gas is largest.
💡 Reading the G-T Phase Diagram
Slope: (∂G/∂T)_P = −S. Since entropy increases from solid → liquid → gas, the G curves become progressively steeper (more negative slope) in that order.

Stable phase: At any temperature, the stable phase is the one with the lowest G. This is because systems at constant T, P minimize their Gibbs free energy.

Phase transitions: Occur where two G curves intersect — at that T, both phases have equal G (phase coexistence). Below T_melt, solid is stable; between T_melt and T_boil, liquid is stable; above T_boil, gas is stable.

📝 Important Questions & Answers — Phase Transitions
Explain why the slope of the G-T curve is steepest for the gas phase.
The slope (∂G/∂T)_P = −S. Since entropy increases in the order S_solid < S_liquid < S_gas, the magnitude of the slope is greatest for the gas phase. In a G vs T plot, this means the gas G curve has the steepest downward slope. At high temperatures, the gas G curve eventually falls below both liquid and solid G curves — the gas becomes the thermodynamically stable phase. This is the fundamental thermodynamic reason why all substances become gases at sufficiently high temperatures.
MNEMONIC: "GAS HAS GREATEST SLOPE = GREATEST SLOPE MEANS GREATEST ENTROPY"
What is the significance of ΔG = 0 for a phase transition?
ΔG = 0 (i.e., g₁ = g₂) is the condition for two phases to coexist in equilibrium. This occurs along the coexistence curves in the P-T diagram (the melting curve, boiling curve, sublimation curve). At these curves, neither phase is more stable than the other — they are in dynamic equilibrium, with molecules continuously exchanging between phases. This is also the condition from which the Clausius-Clapeyron equation is derived. Away from these curves, one phase has lower G and is the thermodynamically stable phase.
Show that Cₚ − Cᵥ = R for a perfect gas using only the general formula and the ideal gas equation.
General: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T(∂V/∂T)_P·(∂P/∂T)_V. For one mole of ideal gas: PV = RT. From PV = RT at constant P: V = RT/P → (∂V/∂T)_P = R/P. From PV = RT at constant V: P = RT/V → (∂P/∂T)_V = R/V. Substituting: Cₚ − Cᵥ = T·(R/P)·(R/V) = TR²/(PV) = TR²/(RT) = R ✓. This elegant result shows that Cₚ − Cᵥ = R exactly for any ideal gas, regardless of whether it is monatomic, diatomic, etc. The difference is always exactly the universal gas constant R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).
MNEMONIC: "PERFECT GAS ALWAYS GIVES R — no more, no less"
