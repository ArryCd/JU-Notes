Conductivity Range

1Free Electron Gas Model – Drude (Classical)

Drude (1900) and Lorentz treated the conduction electrons in a metal as a classical ideal gas obeying Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics. Electrons collide with stationary ion cores.

Packing Fractions (Context: Electron Density)

Free Electron Density — Example: Copper

Number of free electrons per cm³ in Cu
Atomic weight of Cu = 63.546 g/mol
Density of Cu = 8.96 g/cc

n = (6.023 × 10²³ / 63.546) × 8.96 ≈ 8.5 × 10²² electrons/cc

→ ~1000× denser than classical ideal gas at STP

Drude Equation of Motion

When electric field E is applied, force on electron = eE. But there's a resistive force proportional to instantaneous velocity (mimicking collisions):

m dv/dt = eE − λv

At steady state (dv/dt = 0):
eE = λv_d → λ = eE/v_d

Full solution:
v(t) = v_d (1 − e^{−t/τ}) ← τ = relaxation/mean free time

As t → ∞, v → v_d (drift velocity)

Electrical Conductivity Derivation

Current density J = nevA/A = nev_d (n = electrons/vol, e = charge, v_d = drift vel.)
From eE = λv_d and λ = eE/v_d → v_d = eEτ/m
J = ne(eEτ/m) = (ne²τ/m)E
Since J = σE:
σ = ne²τ / m ← Drude formula

Plugging in: n ~ 10²² /cc, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg, τ ~ 10⁻¹⁴ s
→ σ ~ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹ ✓ matches experiment
Success ✓ Correctly gives room temperature conductivity
2Thermal Conductivity (Drude)

Heat flows from hot to cold. In 1D, electron at position x carries energy E[T(x)]. Electrons travel ±vτ before colliding, bringing energy from their last collision point.

Thermal current density
j_q = −k dT/dx (Fourier's Law)

From Drude: k = (nv²τ/3) · C_v^e

With C_v^e = (3/2)k_B (classical) and ⟨v²⟩ = 8k_BT/πm:

k = (4/π) k_B² (n/m) τ T

Wiedemann–Franz Law

k / (σT) = (4/π)(k_B/e)² ≈ constant (experimentally!)

This is the Lorenz number L = k/(σT)
Success ✓ Drude correctly predicts W-F law

Drawbacks of Drude Model

1. Low temperature failure: As T→0, ρ→∞ (τ→0), so 1/τ (scattering rate) → ∞. But experimentally, at very low T, mean free path λ becomes thousands of Å — Drude can't explain this.
2. Hall effect anomaly: For divalent/trivalent metals (Be, Al), RH is positive (Drude predicts always negative). Drude fails for multivalent metals.
3. Electronic specific heat: Drude predicts C_v = (3/2)Nk_B, but experiment gives ~100× less. Biggest failure.
4. Cannot explain insulators: At T→0, conductivity should → ∞ by Drude, but insulators have zero conductivity.
3Hall Effect

When current flows in y-direction and magnetic field B is applied in z-direction, a transverse voltage (Hall voltage VH) develops in the x-direction.

Microscopic Derivation (Drude Framework)

Drude equation: m dv/dt = e[E + v×B] − mv/τ
Steady-state (dv/dt = 0): p/τ = e[E + v×B]
Using J = nev → v = J/ne, substitute into above
Separate x and y components of E
E⃗ = −(1/ne)(J⃗ × B⃗) + (m/ne²τ)J⃗

x-component: E_x = −(1/ne)JB₀ ≡ ρ_H · J ← Hall field
y-component: E_y = (m/ne²τ)J ≡ ρ · J ← Ohmic field

Hall resistivity: ρ_H = −(1/ne)B₀
Hall coefficient: R_H = ρ_H/B₀ = −1/ne

Experimental Results (R_H · ne)

MetalValenceR_H · ne (theory = −1)Interpretation
Cu1−1.5Drude OK
Ag1−1.5Drude OK
Be2+0.2Positive! Holes dominate
Al3+0.3Positive! Band structure needed
Key insight: Positive RH means charge carriers behave as holes (positive). This is a quantum band-structure effect — Drude treating carriers as electrons always predicts RH < 0.
4Quantum Free Electron Model — Sommerfeld (1928)

Electrons are quantum particles (fermions) — they obey Fermi-Dirac statistics, NOT Maxwell-Boltzmann. Each state can hold at most 2 electrons (spin ↑ and ↓) by Pauli's exclusion principle.

1D Particle in a Box → Stationary Wave BC

SE: −(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ (V=0 inside)

Solution: ψ(x) = A cos(kx) + B sin(kx)
BC: ψ(0) = 0 → A = 0; ψ(L) = 0 → kL = nπ

k = nπ/L, n = 1, 2, 3, ...

ψ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)

Energy: E = ℏ²k²/2m = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²) → E ∝ n²

Progressive Wave (Periodic BC) — More Physical for Solid

Instead of a box with hard walls, treat the crystal as a ring (no boundary) — electron travels continuously:

ψ(x) = ψ(x+L) → e^{ikL} = 1 → kL = 2πn

k = 2πn/L, n = 0, ±1, ±2, ...

ψ(x,y,z) = (1/√V) exp(i k⃗·r⃗) (3D plane wave)

Energy: E(k⃗) = ℏ²k²/2m = ℏ²(k_x² + k_y² + k_z²)/2m

Density of States (3D) — D(E)

D(E)dE = number of states with energy in [E, E+dE]

Volume of spherical shell in k-space: 4πk²dk
Number of states = 2 × (4πk²dk) / (2π/L)³ [factor 2 for spin]
= (V/π²) k² dk

Using E = ℏ²k²/2m → k = √(2mE)/ℏ, dk = (m/ℏ²k)dE:

D(E) = V/(2π²) (2m/ℏ²)^{3/2} E^{1/2} = C · E^{1/2}

where C = V/(2π²) (2m/ℏ²)^{3/2}

Fermi-Dirac Distribution

f_D(E) = 1 / [1 + exp((E − E_F)/k_BT)]

At T = 0: f_D = 1 for E < E_F; f_D = 0 for E > E_F
At T ≠ 0: smearing over ~k_BT around E_F (≈ 26 meV at 300K)
5Sommerfeld Theory — Key Results

Fermi Energy at T = 0

Total number of electrons N = ∫₀^{E_F₀} D(E) · 1 · dE (f=1 at T=0)

N = C ∫₀^{E_{F0}} E^{1/2} dE = (2/3)C E_{F0}^{3/2}

Solving for E_{F0}:

E_{F0} = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)^{2/3} where n = N/V

Typical values (in eV): Li=4.72, Na=3.12, K=2.14, Cu=7.04, Ag=5.19
Classical paradox: E_{F0} ~ 5 eV ≡ temperature ~58,000 K! But classically, T=0 means zero energy. The electrons have this energy purely due to Pauli exclusion — quantum zero-point pressure.

Average Energy at T = 0

Ē₀ = (1/N) ∫₀^{E_{F0}} E · D(E) dE = (3/5) E_{F0}

← Average energy is 3/5 of Fermi energy, not zero!

Temperature Dependence of Fermi Energy

E_F(T) = E_{F0} [1 − (π²/12)(kT/E_F)²]

Correction is ~10⁻⁴ eV at 300 K — Fermi energy barely changes with T.

Average Energy at Temperature T

ū(T) = U₀ [1 + (5π²/12)(kT/E_F)²]

where U₀ = (3/5)N·E_{F0}

Electronic Specific Heat (CRITICAL)

This resolves the biggest failure of Drude:

C_v^e = ∂U/∂T = U₀ · (5π²/12) · 2kT/E_F² · k_B

= (π²/2) nk_B · (k_BT/E_F)

C_v^e / vol = γT where γ = (π²/2) nk_B²/E_F

→ C_v ∝ T (NOT constant as Drude predicted!)

Why Drude Overestimated C_v by 100×

In Drude: all N electrons absorb thermal energy → C_v = (3/2)Nk_B.
In Sommerfeld: only electrons near E_F (within ~k_BT) can be excited. This is only ~(k_BT/E_F) fraction of electrons, so C_v is reduced by this factor → C_v ∝ T.

Electrical Conductivity (Sommerfeld)

σ = ne²τ(E_F)/m

Mean free path: λ = v_F · τ(E_F)

σ = e²λm² / (12π³ℏ³) · S_F

where S_F = 4πk_F² is the Fermi surface area

Key difference: τ evaluated at E_F, not at average energy

Work Function

The potential well confining electrons has finite depth E_s (vacuum level).

φ = E_s − E_F

Work function = energy needed to pull an electron from E_F to vacuum
6Pauli Spin Paramagnetism

Paramagnetism = magnetic property that appears only when external magnetic field B is present. Pauli Spin Paramagnetism (PSP) is due to conduction electrons, not ions.

Magnetic Moment of Electron

μ_B = eℏ/(2mc) ← Bohr magneton

For spin-½ electrons: spin +½ (↑): μ_z = −μ_B (moment anti-parallel to B → energy = +μ_BB) spin −½ (↓): μ_z = +μ_B (moment parallel to B → energy = −μ_BB)

Pauli Susceptibility

χ_P = nμ_B² / k_BT (classical → wrong!)

Quantum result: only electrons near E_F contribute

χ_P = (3/2) nμ_B²/E_F = nμ_B²/k_BT_F

→ Temperature INDEPENDENT (unlike classical paramagnets which go as 1/T)
Key experimental signature: Classical Curie paramagnetism χ ∝ 1/T, but metals like Na, K, Li show χ ≈ constant with temperature. This is Pauli paramagnetism — a quantum effect.
7Boltzmann Transport Equation

Goes beyond Drude and Sommerfeld by tracking how the distribution function f(r⃗, p⃗, t) evolves under fields and collisions.

What is f(r⃗, p⃗, t)?

f(x, y, z, p_x, p_y, p_z, t) d³r d³p = number of particles in phase-space element d³r·d³p at time t. This is a 7-variable function (Cartesian + momentum + time).

Derivation of BTE

Particle at (r⃗, p⃗, t) moves to (r⃗+v⃗δt, p⃗+F⃗δt, t+δt) under external force F⃗ = −e(E⃗ + v⃗×B⃗)
By Liouville's theorem: phase-space volume is conserved → f(r⃗+v⃗δt, ..., t+δt) = f(r⃗, p⃗, t) (without collisions)
Taylor expand and divide by δt → total time derivative = 0 without collisions
Add collision term (∂f/∂t)|_coll to account for scattering
∂f/∂t|_field = −v⃗·∇_r f − F⃗·∇_p f

BTE: ∂f/∂t|_field + ∂f/∂t|_coll = 0

Relaxation time approximation:
∂f/∂t|_coll = −(f − f₀)/τ

Steady state (∂f/∂t = 0):
(f − f₀)/τ = −∂f/∂t|_field = −v⃗·∇_r f + eE⃗·∇_p f

For E field in x-direction:
f − f₀ = eE_x τ(E) v_x ∂f₀/∂E

Conductivity from BTE

J = −(2e/h³) ∫∫∫ v_x (f − f₀) dp_x dp_y dp_z

After integration (replacing v_x² → v²/3, converting to energy):

J = (e²n τ(E_F)/m) E_x → σ = ne²τ(E_F)/m

Same form as Drude, but τ evaluated at Fermi energy — physically correct!
Sommerfeld vs Drude σ: Identical formula, but Sommerfeld uses Fermi velocity v_F (not thermal velocity) and τ(E_F). This resolves the low-temperature behaviour correctly.

BTE — Collision Term (Detailed)

∂f/∂t|_coll = ∫ d³p' · P(p⃗,p⃗') · [f(p⃗')(1−f(p⃗)) − f(p⃗)(1−f(p⃗'))]

Three factors determining scattering rate:
1. f(p⃗) = occupancy at initial state (source)
2. P(p⃗,p⃗') = transition probability p⃗→p⃗'
3. (1−f(p⃗')) = vacancy at final state (Pauli blocking)
★Important Questions for Class Test

Derivation-Based Questions

Q1 — Drude Model
Derive the expression for electrical conductivity using the Drude free electron gas model. Show that σ = ne²τ/m. What are the assumptions made?
Hint: Start from equation of motion m dv/dt = eE − λv. Steady state → drift velocity. Use J = nev_d = σE.
Q2 — Hall Effect
Starting from the Drude equation dp⃗/dt = fext − p⃗/τ with both E⃗ and B⃗ fields, derive the Hall coefficient R_H = −1/ne. Why is R_H positive for Be and Al?
Hint: Apply steady state condition. Separate x and y components. E_x = ρ_H · J (Hall). R_H = ρ_H/B₀.
Q3 — Fermi Energy
Derive the expression for Fermi energy E_{F0} = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)^{2/3}. Calculate it for copper and comment on its physical significance.
Hint: Use D(E) = C·E^½ for 3D. Integrate N = ∫₀^{E_F} D(E)dE at T=0. Solve for E_F.
Q4 — Electronic Specific Heat
Show that the electronic contribution to specific heat is C_v^e = γT where γ = π²nk_B²/(2E_F). How does this resolve the Drude model failure?
Hint: Differentiate Ū(T) = U₀[1 + (5π²/12)(kT/E_F)²] with respect to T.
Q5 — Density of States
Derive the density of states D(E) for a free electron gas in 3D. Sketch D(E) vs E for 1D, 2D, and 3D systems and comment on the differences.
Hint: 3D: D(k)dk = 4πk²dk/(2π/L)³ · 2 (spin). Convert using E = ℏ²k²/2m.
Q6 — Thermal Conductivity
Derive thermal conductivity k from the Drude model and hence prove the Wiedemann-Franz law k/σT = constant. State the Lorenz number.
Hint: Use 1D energy transport argument. Net thermal current = (n/2)v[E(T(x−vτ)) − E(T(x+vτ))]. Taylor expand T.
Q7 — BTE
Write the Boltzmann transport equation. Using the relaxation time approximation, show how it gives σ = ne²τ(E_F)/m.
Hint: ∂f/∂t|field + ∂f/∂t|coll = 0. Substitution f − f₀ = eEτv_x ∂f₀/∂E. Integrate to get J.

Conceptual Questions

Q8
Discuss the successes and failures of the Drude model. Why does the Sommerfeld model succeed where Drude fails?
Successes: σ, Wiedemann-Franz. Failures: specific heat (100× off), low-T behaviour, positive Hall coefficient.
Q9
What is Pauli spin paramagnetism? Why is the susceptibility temperature-independent, unlike classical paramagnets?
Only electrons near E_F can flip spins. Their number ∝ D(E_F)·k_BT, but T cancels in χ. Classical: all electrons contribute → χ ∝ 1/T (Curie).
Q10
Explain the difference between stationary wave BC and periodic (Born-von Kármán) BC for electrons in a solid. Which is more physical and why?
Stationary: ψ=0 at walls, standing waves, k=nπ/L. Periodic: travelling waves, k=2πn/L, each state doubly degenerate (±k). Periodic is more physical for bulk solids.
Q11
Define mean free path in the Drude model. Calculate it for Cu at room temperature and at ~2K. Why can't Drude explain the low-temperature value?
λ = vτ. At 300K: λ = 10⁵×10⁻¹⁴ ≈ 10Å. At 2K: τ~10⁻¹³s → λ ~ 1000Å. Drude assumed λ = lattice spacing — contradicted at low T.
Q12
What is the physical significance of the Fermi energy? Show that the average energy of electrons at T=0 is Ē = (3/5)E_F, not zero.
Fermi energy = highest occupied energy at T=0. Non-zero because Pauli exclusion forces electrons to fill up states. Ē = ∫₀^{E_F} E·D(E)dE / N.

Numerical/Short Answer

Q13
Given n = 8.5×10²² /cc, τ = 4×10⁻¹⁴ s for Cu, calculate: (a) σ, (b) relaxation time at 2K given σ increases 10⁴×, (c) mean free path at 2K.
Q14
Sketch f_D(E) at T=0 and T=300K for a metal with E_F = 5eV. Explain why the distribution is essentially unchanged at room temperature.
k_BT = 26meV ≪ E_F = 5000meV. Only ~0.5% of electrons near E_F are affected.
Q15
For a free electron gas, show the ratio γ_expt/γ_theory = m*/m_e (effective mass ratio). What are heavy fermions? Give examples.
γ ∝ D(E_F) ∝ m. In periodic potential, m replaces m_e. CeAl₃, CeCu₂Si₂ have m* ~ 1000m_e.
