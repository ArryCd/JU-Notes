Introduction to Spectroscopy What is a spectrum? Atomic vs Molecular

A spectrum is a plot of intensity vs. wavelength, frequency, or wavenumber for radiation emitted/absorbed by matter. When electromagnetic radiation interacts with matter, the result — absorption, emission, or scattering — is spectroscopy.

Emission vs. Absorption

  • Emission spectrum: atoms/molecules lose energy and emit photons. Seen as bright lines on a dark background. Comes from the inner part of the sun.
  • Absorption spectrum (Dark lines / Fraunhofer lines): radiation passes through a cool gas and is absorbed at specific frequencies. Seen as dark lines on a continuous background. Acts as a fingerprint of the absorbing element.
Historical Significance
Elements Cs and Rb were discovered through spectroscopy — unknown lines seen in flames that didn't match any known element. Spectroscopy enables analysis of distant stars and galaxies (composition, velocity, temperature) without touching them.

Applications of Spectroscopy

  • Forensic science — compositional analysis of trace samples
  • Food safety — detecting adulterants and contaminants
  • Material science — probing electronic structure and bonding
  • Astronomy — studying extra-terrestrial objects remotely
  • Medical diagnostics — IR/NMR/fluorescence spectroscopy

Atomic vs. Molecular Spectroscopy

FeatureAtomicMolecular
Line shapeSharp, delta-function-like linesBroad bands (overlapping lines)
OriginElectronic transitions only, $E_2 - E_1 = h\nu$Electronic + vibrational + rotational transitions
ComplexitySimpler to interpretComplex — band spectra due to multiple levels
InteractionEM radiation with electron cloudEM wave interacts with oscillating dipole
Why Molecular Spectroscopy is Complex
In a molecule, the EM wave interacts with an oscillating dipole. The molecule can have rotational and vibrational motion that modulates this dipole. This leads to many more energy levels, hence overlapping spectral lines forming bands instead of sharp lines.

The range $10^4$–$10^5$ cm$^{-1}$ is easy to plot and covers most interesting spectroscopy.

The Born-Oppenheimer Approximation Decoupling electronic, vibrational and rotational motion

For a molecule like H₂ (2 nuclei, 2 electrons), the full Schrödinger equation involves all particles simultaneously — it cannot be solved analytically because of the large number of interaction terms. The Born-Oppenheimer approximation is the key trick that makes molecular quantum mechanics tractable.

The Physical Idea

Electrons are about 1836 times lighter than protons and move roughly 1836 times faster. When an electron makes one orbit, the nucleus has barely moved. So from the electron's perspective, the nucleus is essentially stationary. Conversely, when the nucleus vibrates, the electrons instantaneously rearrange.

Key Insight
Because the nuclear motion is so much slower than electronic motion, we can decouple them. Solve for electrons first (with fixed nuclei), then solve for nuclei in the resulting potential. This is the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.

Mathematical Formulation

The total molecular wavefunction factorises into independent parts:

Born-Oppenheimer Factorisation $$\Psi_\text{mol} = \Psi_e \cdot \Psi_v \cdot \Psi_r$$

where $\Psi_e$ = electronic, $\Psi_v$ = vibrational, $\Psi_r$ = rotational wavefunction

Similarly, the total Hamiltonian separates:

Separated Hamiltonian $$\hat{H}_\text{mol} = \hat{H}_e + \hat{H}_v + \hat{H}_r$$

Since each $\hat{H}_i$ operates only on its own $\Psi_i$:

Energy Addition $$\hat{H}_\text{mol}\Psi_\text{mol} = (E_e + E_v + E_r)\Psi_\text{mol}$$ $$E_\text{total} = E_e + E_v + E_r$$

This is why "zooming in" on an electronic absorption band reveals vibrational structure, and zooming further reveals rotational structure — the energy scales are all different and separable.

Why Can Translational Motion Be Ignored?
  • A nucleus has spin motion (spin angular momentum) and vibrational motion between bonds.
  • The whole nucleus also has translational motion (center-of-mass motion).
  • Translational motion only gains energy when an external electric field is applied (i.e., it couples to electrostatics). In a field-free spectroscopy experiment, it's negligible — except it appears as Doppler broadening.
  • So: $\Psi_\text{nuc} = \Psi_t \cdot \Psi_v \cdot \Psi_r$, and since $\Psi_t$ doesn't appear in spectroscopy (only Doppler shift), we drop it.

Classification of Molecules as Rotors Moments of inertia and molecular topology

Rotational spectroscopy depends critically on the moment of inertia of the molecule. For rotation about 3 principal axes A, B, C, we define $I_A$, $I_B$, $I_C$ (with convention $I_A \leq I_B \leq I_C$).

Moment of Inertia (general) $$I = \sum_i m_i r_i^2$$

where $r_i$ is the perpendicular distance of mass $m_i$ from the rotation axis.

Five Types of Tops (Molecular Rotors)

1. Linear Molecule

All atoms along one axis. Rotation about the molecular axis has negligible inertia since all mass is on the axis.

$$I_A = I_B, \quad I_C \approx 0$$

Examples: CO, HCl, CO₂, HCN, N₂

2. Symmetric Top

Two of the three moments are equal.

$$I_A = I_B \neq I_C$$

Sub-types:

  • Prolate symmetric top ($I_A = I_B > I_C$): shaped like a rugby ball / cigar. Example: CH₃Cl, NH₃. The unique axis C has smaller inertia (spins fastest).
  • Oblate symmetric top ($I_A = I_B < I_C$): shaped like a frisbee/disc. Example: BCl₃, benzene. The unique axis C has larger inertia.

3. Spherical Top

$$I_A = I_B = I_C$$

Rotation is equivalent in all directions. No net change in dipole moment with rotation → no pure rotational spectrum. Examples: CH₄, SF₆, CCl₄ (hydrocarbons).

4. Asymmetric Top

$$I_A \neq I_B \neq I_C$$

The most common and most complicated case. No two moments are equal. Examples: H₂O, SO₂, most complex organic molecules. No simple closed-form energy expression.

Mnemonic — Prolate vs Oblate
Prolate = Pointer — thin and pointy like a pen/cigar (unique axis is the long symmetry axis, smallest I).
Oblate = Obese/disc — fat like a discus (unique axis perpendicular to the plane, largest I).
Remember: for prolate $I_C < I_A = I_B$ and for oblate $I_C > I_A = I_B$.

Rotational Spectroscopy — Rigid Diatomic Rotor Quantum energy levels, wavefunctions, and the microwave spectrum

The Rigid Rotor Model

Model: a diatomic molecule is two masses $m_1$, $m_2$ separated by a fixed bond length $r_e$ (no vibration). The system reduces to a single particle of reduced mass $\mu$ rotating at distance $r_e$:

Reduced Mass $$\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

The moment of inertia:

$$I = \mu r_e^2$$

Since the bond length is fixed, there is no potential energy (no vibration), so the Hamiltonian is purely kinetic:

Rigid Rotor Hamiltonian $$\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{L}^2}{2I}$$

Solving the Schrödinger Equation

The SE in 3D using spherical polar coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$:

Rigid Rotor SE $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2I}\left[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right]\Psi = E\Psi$$

Note: the $r$ term vanishes since $r$ is fixed for a rigid rotor. $\nabla^2$ reduces to just the angular part.

Step-by-Step Derivation of Energy Levels
  1. Assume separable solution: $\Psi(\theta,\phi) = \Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi)$
  2. Substitute into SE and divide by $\Theta\Phi$. The $\phi$-dependent part separates: $$\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial\phi^2} = -m^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Phi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{im\phi}$$ where $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$ is the magnetic quantum number.
  3. The $\theta$-equation becomes the associated Legendre equation: $$\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\theta}\right)\Theta = m^2\Theta$$ Solutions are Associated Legendre Polynomials $P_J^{|m|}(\cos\theta)$.
  4. For physical (normalizable, single-valued) solutions, we require the rotational quantum number $J = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ with $|m| \leq J$.
  5. The combined solution $\Psi = \Theta\Phi$ are the spherical harmonics $Y_J^m(\theta,\phi)$.
  6. Substituting back gives the energy eigenvalue: $$E_J = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I}J(J+1)$$
Rigid Rotor Energy Levels $$\boxed{E_J = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I}J(J+1), \qquad J = 0, 1, 2, \ldots}$$

In spectroscopy, we convert to wavenumbers (divide by $hc$):

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_J = \frac{E_J}{hc} = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 Ic}J(J+1) = B\,J(J+1) \quad \text{cm}^{-1}}$$
Rotational Constant B $$\boxed{B = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 Ic} = \frac{\hbar}{4\pi Ic} \quad \text{cm}^{-1}}$$

$B$ contains $I = \mu r_e^2$. Measuring $B$ from spectroscopy gives us the bond length $r_e$. This is why rotational spectroscopy is so powerful for structural chemistry.

Degeneracy of Rotational Levels

For each value of $J$, the magnetic quantum number $m$ (or $m_J$) can take $2J+1$ values: $m = -J, -J+1, \ldots, 0, \ldots, J-1, J$. Each $(J, m)$ pair is a distinct state with the same energy (in field-free space). Therefore:

Degeneracy of Level J $$g_J = 2J + 1$$

This degeneracy is crucial for understanding the intensity pattern of the rotational spectrum.

The Microwave Absorption Spectrum

The transition frequency for $J \to J+1$ absorption:

Transition Wavenumber (Rigid Rotor) $$\tilde{\nu}_{J \to J+1} = \varepsilon_{J+1} - \varepsilon_J = B(J+2)(J+1) - BJ(J+1) = 2B(J+1)$$ $$\boxed{\tilde{\nu}_{J \to J+1} = 2B(J+1)}$$
Key Result: Equally Spaced Spectrum
For $J = 0 \to 1$: $\tilde{\nu} = 2B$; for $J = 1 \to 2$: $\tilde{\nu} = 4B$; for $J = 2 \to 3$: $\tilde{\nu} = 6B$, etc. The spectrum consists of equally spaced lines at $2B, 4B, 6B, \ldots$ with spacing $2B$ between consecutive lines. Measuring this spacing gives $B$, and hence the bond length!

Intensity and Population — Maxwell-Boltzmann Distribution

The intensity of a spectral line depends on the population of the initial state. At temperature $T$, population of level $J$ is:

Population of Level J $$N_J = N_0 \cdot (2J+1) \cdot e^{-Bhc\,J(J+1)/kT}$$

The $(2J+1)$ factor is the degeneracy; it increases population for low $J$. The exponential Boltzmann factor suppresses high $J$. Their competition creates a maximum.

The level with maximum population is found by differentiating $N_J$ with respect to $J$ and setting to zero:

Most Populated Level $$\boxed{J_\text{max} = \sqrt{\frac{kT}{2Bhc}} - \frac{1}{2}}$$
Why Intensity Peaks Then Falls
At low $J$: few states (small degeneracy) but high population per state → some intensity. At high $J$: many states but Boltzmann factor kills them. In between, the product $(2J+1)e^{-E_J/kT}$ has a maximum at $J_\text{max}$, giving the characteristic bell-shaped envelope of the rotational spectrum.
Q1 HCl has $B = 10.6$ cm$^{-1}$. Where will the first absorption line appear and what is the bond length?

First line: $J = 0 \to 1$, so $\tilde{\nu} = 2B = 21.2$ cm$^{-1}$ (microwave region).

Solution

Bond length from $B$: $B = h/(8\pi^2 Ic)$ so $I = h/(8\pi^2 Bc)$

$I = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{8\pi^2 \times 10.6 \times 100 \times 2.998\times10^{10}} = 2.65\times10^{-47}$ kg·m²

Reduced mass of HCl: $\mu = \frac{1\times35}{36}\times1.66\times10^{-27} = 1.63\times10^{-27}$ kg

$r_e = \sqrt{I/\mu} = \sqrt{2.65\times10^{-47}/1.63\times10^{-27}} = 1.27$ Å ✓ (known value ~1.274 Å)

Selection Rules for Rotational Spectroscopy Which transitions are allowed? The physical and mathematical basis.

Physical Prerequisite: Permanent Dipole Moment

For a molecule to have a pure rotational spectrum, it must possess a permanent electric dipole moment. The interaction energy between a dipole $\vec{\mu}$ and an external electric field $\vec{E}$ is:

$$U = -\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{E} = -\mu E \cos\theta$$

When the molecule rotates, the direction of $\vec{\mu}$ changes with time → the oscillating dipole interacts with the oscillating EM field. When the dipole's rotation frequency matches the field frequency: resonance → absorption/emission.

Condition for Pure Rotational Spectrum
The molecule must have a permanent dipole moment $\mu \neq 0$. Molecules like O₂, N₂, CO₂, H₂ (homonuclear diatomics or symmetric molecules) have $\mu = 0$ and show no pure rotational spectrum. However, they can still show vibrational and electronic spectra.
MoleculeDipole?Pure Rotational Spectrum?Why
HCl, CO, HFYesYesHeteronuclear → asymmetric charge
O₂, N₂, H₂NoNoHomonuclear → symmetric charge dist.
CO₂NoNoLinear, symmetric → dipoles cancel
H₂O, SO₂YesYesBent molecule → net dipole
CH₄, CCl₄NoNoSpherical top → all dipoles cancel

Mathematical Derivation of the Selection Rule

A transition from state $\Psi_{J',m'}$ to state $\Psi_{J,m}$ occurs only if the transition dipole moment is non-zero:

Transition Dipole Moment (must be non-zero) $$\langle\Psi_{J'}^{m'} | \hat{\mu}_z | \Psi_J^m\rangle = \int\int Y_{J'}^{m'*}(\theta,\phi)\,\mu\cos\theta\,Y_J^m(\theta,\phi)\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi \neq 0$$

Here $\mu_z = \mu\cos\theta$ because the $z$-component of the dipole in the lab frame is $\mu$ (fixed body) times $\cos\theta$ (projection). The wavefunctions are spherical harmonics $Y_J^m$.

Deriving $\Delta m = 0$ Condition (Step by Step)
  1. Separate the integral into $\theta$-part and $\phi$-part: $$= N'N\mu \int_0^{2\pi} e^{-im'\phi}e^{im\phi}\,d\phi \;\times\; \int_0^\pi P_{J'}^{|m'|}(\cos\theta)\,\cos\theta\,P_J^{|m|}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta$$
  2. Evaluate the $\phi$ integral: $$\int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m-m')\phi}\,d\phi = \begin{cases} 2\pi & \text{if } m = m' \\ 0 & \text{if } m \neq m' \end{cases}$$
  3. Therefore: the integral is zero unless $m = m'$, i.e., $\Delta m = m' - m = 0$.
  4. This gives the first selection rule: $\Delta m = 0$.
Deriving $\Delta J = \pm 1$ Condition (Step by Step)
  1. With $\Delta m = 0$, the integral reduces to: $$2\pi N'N\mu \int_{-1}^{+1} P_{J'}^{|m|}(x)\cdot x \cdot P_J^{|m|}(x)\,dx \quad \text{(where } x = \cos\theta\text{)}$$
  2. Use the recurrence identity for Associated Legendre polynomials: $$(2J+1)\,x\,P_J^{|m|}(x) = (J-|m|+1)\,P_{J+1}^{|m|}(x) + (J+|m|)\,P_{J-1}^{|m|}(x)$$
  3. Substitute this identity into the integral. The orthogonality of Legendre polynomials says: $$\int_{-1}^{1} P_{J'}^{|m|}(x)\,P_J^{|m|}(x)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{unless } J' = J$$
  4. After substitution, the integral is non-zero only if $J' = J+1$ or $J' = J-1$, i.e., $\Delta J = \pm 1$.
  5. $\Delta J = +1$: absorption (molecule goes to higher $J$). $\Delta J = -1$: emission.
Selection Rules for Pure Rotational Spectroscopy $$\boxed{\Delta J = \pm 1, \quad \Delta m = 0}$$

$\Delta J = +1$ for absorption, $\Delta J = -1$ for emission. Only adjacent $J$ levels can be connected.

Mnemonic — Selection Rules
"One Step at a Time" — the rotational quantum number can only change by one ($\Delta J = \pm 1$). Think of it like climbing stairs — you can only go up or down one step, never jump two. The dipole "projects" onto the lab $z$-axis via $\cos\theta$, so $m$ cannot change ($\Delta m = 0$).
Q2 Why does CO₂ show no pure rotational spectrum even though it has C=O bonds which are individually polar?
Answer

CO₂ is a linear, symmetric molecule: O=C=O. The two C=O dipole moments point in opposite directions along the molecular axis and are equal in magnitude. They exactly cancel, giving a net permanent dipole $\mu = 0$. With no permanent dipole, no oscillating dipole is created by rotation, so no interaction with the EM field is possible → no rotational spectrum. CO₂ can still show vibrational (IR) and electronic (UV-Vis) spectra.

Non-Rigid Rotor & Centrifugal Distortion Why the spectrum isn't perfectly equally spaced — the most misunderstood correction

The Problem with the Rigid Rotor
The rigid rotor predicts perfectly equally-spaced spectral lines at $2B, 4B, 6B, \ldots$. But in reality, the spectrum lines get slightly closer together at higher $J$. The rigid rotor model is wrong because real bonds stretch when the molecule rotates fast (like a spinning ball on a rubber string).

Physical Mechanism of Centrifugal Distortion

Consider the molecule spinning. The centrifugal force $\mu\omega^2 r$ acts outward, trying to stretch the bond. The restoring force of the bond (treated as a spring with force constant $k$) acts inward. In equilibrium:

Force Balance for Non-Rigid Rotor $$k(r - r_e) = \mu\omega^2 r$$

Solving for $r$ in terms of $r_e$:

Stretched Bond Length $$r = \frac{r_e}{1 - \mu\omega^2/k}$$
Why Does $B$ Change?
The rotational constant $B \propto 1/r^2$. When the molecule rotates faster (higher $J$), the centrifugal force stretches the bond: $r > r_e$. So $r^2 > r_e^2$ and $B = \hbar/(4\pi\mu r^2 c) < B_e$. $B$ decreases as rotation speed increases.

Also: $\langle 1/r^2\rangle \neq 1/r_e^2$ for the average over vibrational motion. So $B$ is not exactly $B_e$ even in the vibrational ground state.

Energy of the Non-Rigid Rotor — Detailed Derivation

We need to compute the total energy including the potential energy of the stretched spring, not just kinetic energy.

Full Derivation (KD Sir's approach)
  1. Kinetic energy: $\text{KE} = L^2/2I$ where $I = \mu r^2$ (bond is now stretched to $r$, not $r_e$)
  2. Potential energy: $\text{PE} = \frac{1}{2}k(r-r_e)^2 = \frac{1}{2}k\left(\frac{\mu\omega^2 r}{k}\right)^2 = \frac{\mu^2\omega^2r^2}{2k}$
  3. Now, $L = I\omega = \mu r^2\omega$, so $\omega = L/\mu r^2$. Thus $\mu\omega^2 r^2 = L^2/\mu r^2$, and: $$\text{PE} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{L^4}{\mu^2r^4 \cdot k} \cdot \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2} = \frac{L^4\mu}{2kI^3}$$
  4. Also: KE with stretched $r$: $$\text{KE} = \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{L^2\omega^2}{r_e^2 k} = \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{L^2\omega^2\mu}{k}$$ After careful expansion keeping first-order correction in $\mu\omega^2/k$: $$\text{KE} = \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{L^2\omega^2\mu}{k} \approx \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{L^4\mu}{I^2 r_e^2 k}$$
  5. Total energy: $E = \text{KE} + \text{PE}$: $$E = \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{L^4\mu}{I^2 r_e^2 k} + \frac{L^4\mu}{2kI^3}$$ Combining terms (using $I = \mu r_e^2$): $$\boxed{E = \frac{L^2}{2I} - \frac{\mu L^4}{2kI^3}}$$
  6. Quantise: $L^2 \to J(J+1)\hbar^2$. Convert to wavenumbers: $$\varepsilon_J = B\,J(J+1) - D\,J^2(J+1)^2$$
Non-Rigid Rotor Energy $$\boxed{\varepsilon_J = B\,J(J+1) - D\,J^2(J+1)^2 \quad \text{cm}^{-1}}$$
Centrifugal Distortion Constant D $$D = \frac{4B^3}{\tilde{\omega}_e^2}$$

where $\tilde{\omega}_e$ is the vibrational frequency of the bond (in cm$^{-1}$). Note: $D \approx 10^{-3}$ cm$^{-1}$ while $B \approx 10$ cm$^{-1}$, so $D \ll B$. The correction is tiny at low $J$ but becomes significant at high $J$ because of the $J^2(J+1)^2$ dependence.

Why D is Always Positive and Small
$D > 0$ always, because centrifugal force always stretches the bond (never compresses it). The $-D\,J^2(J+1)^2$ term lowers the energy levels compared to the rigid rotor. At high $J$, where $J^2(J+1)^2$ is large, the levels are pulled closer together, making spectral lines converge slightly. Typical values: $B \sim 1$–$20$ cm$^{-1}$, $D \sim 10^{-3}$–$10^{-4}$ cm$^{-1}$.

Transition Frequencies for the Non-Rigid Rotor

Calculate $\tilde{\nu}_{J \to J+1} = \varepsilon_{J+1} - \varepsilon_J$:

Derivation of Transition Frequency
  1. $\varepsilon_{J+1} = B(J+1)(J+2) - D(J+1)^2(J+2)^2$
  2. $\varepsilon_J = BJ(J+1) - DJ^2(J+1)^2$
  3. $\tilde{\nu} = \varepsilon_{J+1} - \varepsilon_J = B(J+1)[(J+2)-(J)] - D(J+1)^2[(J+2)^2 - J^2]$
  4. $= 2B(J+1) - D(J+1)^2[(J+2+J)(J+2-J)]$
  5. $= 2B(J+1) - D(J+1)^2[(2J+2)(2)]$
  6. $= 2B(J+1) - 4D(J+1)^3$
Non-Rigid Rotor Transition Frequency $$\boxed{\tilde{\nu}_{J \to J+1} = 2B(J+1) - 4D(J+1)^3}$$
Physical Interpretation
The $-4D(J+1)^3$ term means lines are no longer equally spaced — they converge slightly at high $J$. Rewrite: $$\tilde{\nu} = 2(J+1)\left[B - 2D(J+1)^2\right]$$ So the effective $B$ decreases with $J$. At low $J$, the $D$ term is negligible. At high $J$ (high excited states), the $J^3$ term dominates and the spectrum visibly converges.

Extracting Both B and D from the Spectrum

If both $B$ and $D$ are unknown, rewrite the transition formula:

$$\frac{\tilde{\nu}}{(J+1)} = 2B - 4D(J+1)^2$$

Plot $\tilde{\nu}/(J+1)$ vs $(J+1)^2$: this gives a straight line with intercept $2B$ and slope $-4D$. Both constants can be extracted!

Q3 Explain physically why centrifugal distortion makes spectral lines converge at higher J.
Answer

At higher $J$, the molecule rotates faster. The centrifugal force $\mu\omega^2 r$ is larger. This stretches the bond further: $r > r_e$. Since $I = \mu r^2$ increases, and $B \propto 1/I$, the effective $B$ decreases. The transition energy $2B(J+1)$ is therefore smaller than the rigid rotor prediction. The $-4D(J+1)^3$ correction captures this: it grows rapidly with $J$, pulling successive lines closer together. Think of it as the molecule becoming "lazier to rotate" as it expands.

Mnemonic — Centrifugal Distortion
"Spin fast, stretch far, slow down":
Fast spin → centrifugal stretch → bigger $I$ → smaller $B$ → smaller transition frequency → lines converge.
$D$ = Distortion. $D > 0$ always. The $-DJ^2(J+1)^2$ term always lowers energy levels relative to rigid rotor.

Symmetric Top Rotational Spectroscopy Two quantum numbers J and k — the projection quantum number

Energy Levels of the Symmetric Top

For a symmetric top, two axes have equal moments ($I_A = I_B$), but the third ($I_C$) is different. The classical energy is:

$$E = \frac{L_A^2}{2I_A} + \frac{L_B^2}{2I_B} + \frac{L_C^2}{2I_C} = \frac{L^2 - L_C^2}{2I_A} + \frac{L_C^2}{2I_C}$$

Quantising: $L^2 \to J(J+1)\hbar^2$. The component along the symmetry axis $C$ is quantised separately — we need a new quantum number $k$ (the projection of $J$ on the molecular symmetry axis).

The k Quantum Number
$k$ represents the component of angular momentum along the molecule's own symmetry axis (body-fixed axis). It takes integer values: $k = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots, \pm J$. This is analogous to $m$ (which is projection on the space-fixed axis). The molecule's rotation can be described as: the angular momentum vector precesses around the space-fixed axis (characterised by $m$) and around the body-fixed symmetry axis (characterised by $k$).
Symmetric Top Energy Levels $$\boxed{\varepsilon_{J,k} = B\,J(J+1) + (A-B)\,k^2}$$

where $A = \hbar/(4\pi I_C hc)$ (rotational constant for unique axis) and $B = \hbar/(4\pi I_A hc)$. For prolate: $I_A > I_C$ so $A > B$ → $(A-B) > 0$ → energy increases with $|k|$. For oblate: $I_A < I_C$ so $A < B$ → $(A-B) < 0$ → energy decreases with $|k|$.

Selection Rules for the Symmetric Top

Symmetric Top Selection Rules $$\Delta J = \pm 1, \quad \Delta k = 0$$

Since $\Delta k = 0$, transitions occur between levels with the same $k$. The transition frequency:

Symmetric Top Transition Frequency $$\tilde{\nu}_{J,k \to J+1,k} = 2B(J+1)$$
Key Insight: Why $\Delta k = 0$?
$k$ represents rotation about the symmetry axis. The dipole moment lies along the symmetry axis for a symmetric top. Rotation about the symmetry axis doesn't change the orientation of the dipole in space — the field "sees" the same dipole regardless of $k$. Therefore, $k$ cannot change via electric dipole interaction.
Important Consequence
Even though the energy depends on $k$ ($\varepsilon_{J,k} \neq \varepsilon_{J,k'}$ for $k \neq k'$), the transition frequency $2B(J+1)$ is independent of $k$! So the spectrum looks the same as for a linear molecule (equally spaced), but with $k$-degeneracy (more states contributing to each line). The $A$ constant is hidden from pure rotational spectra — you need other techniques to find $I_C$.

Degeneracy of Symmetric Top Levels

For each $(J,k)$: $k$ can be $\pm k$ (same energy due to $(A-B)k^2$, so twofold degeneracy for $k \neq 0$) and $m$ can be $2J+1$ values. Total degeneracy:

$$g_{J,k} = \begin{cases} 2J+1 & k = 0 \\ 2(2J+1) & k \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

Centrifugal Distortion in the Symmetric Top

Including distortion, the energy of the symmetric top becomes:

Distorted Symmetric Top Energy $$\varepsilon_{J,k} = BJ(J+1) + (A-B)k^2 - D_J J^2(J+1)^2 - D_{JK}J(J+1)k^2 - D_K k^4$$

There are now three centrifugal distortion constants: $D_J$ (purely rotational distortion), $D_{JK}$ (coupled rotation–$k$ distortion), and $D_K$ (purely $k$-axis distortion). The transition frequency becomes:

$$\tilde{\nu}_{J\to J+1} = 2B(J+1) - 4D_J(J+1)^3 - 2D_{JK}(J+1)k^2$$

Now the spectral lines for different $k$ are split slightly — the $D_{JK}$ term gives $k$-dependence to the line positions, allowing $D_{JK}$ to be measured.

Vibrational Spectroscopy — Harmonic Oscillator Infrared absorption, selection rules, and the quantum harmonic oscillator

The Harmonic Oscillator Model

Model the bond as a spring obeying Hooke's Law. Define the vibrational coordinate $q = r - r_e$ (displacement from equilibrium).

Restoring Force $$F = -k(r - r_e) = -kq$$

The Schrödinger equation with the harmonic potential $V = \frac{1}{2}kq^2$ leads to Hermite polynomial solutions. The energy eigenvalues are equally spaced:

Harmonic Oscillator Energy $$\boxed{E_v = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega_\text{osc}, \qquad v = 0, 1, 2, \ldots}$$

where $\omega_\text{osc} = \sqrt{k/\mu}$ is the classical angular frequency of the oscillator, and $v$ is the vibrational quantum number.

In Wavenumbers $$\tilde{\omega}_e = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} \quad \text{cm}^{-1}$$ $$\varepsilon_v = \left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)\tilde{\omega}_e$$
Zero-Point Energy
Even in the ground state ($v = 0$), the molecule has energy $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$. This is the zero-point energy (ZPE). It's a purely quantum mechanical effect — the molecule can never be completely at rest, consistent with the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

Selection Rule for Harmonic Oscillator

Using the transition dipole moment matrix element with harmonic oscillator wavefunctions (Hermite polynomials), the selection rule is:

$$\boxed{\Delta v = \pm 1}$$

All transitions jump exactly one vibrational level. The transition frequency:

$$\tilde{\nu}_{v \to v+1} = \varepsilon_{v+1} - \varepsilon_v = \tilde{\omega}_e \quad \text{(independent of } v\text{!)}$$
Key Result: One Line
In the ideal harmonic case, all vibrational transitions ($0\to1$, $1\to2$, $2\to3$, ...) have the same frequency $\tilde{\omega}_e$. The IR spectrum shows exactly one absorption line at the fundamental frequency!

Condition for Infrared Activity

The molecule must have a changing dipole moment during vibration. Dipole operator: $\mu(q) \approx \mu_0 + (\partial\mu/\partial q)q + \ldots$. The transition integral involves $(\partial\mu/\partial q)$. Non-zero only if the dipole changes during the vibration.

IR Active vs Inactive
A vibration is IR active if $\partial\mu/\partial q \neq 0$: the dipole moment must change during the vibration. For CO₂, the symmetric stretch doesn't change $\mu$ (IR inactive), but the asymmetric stretch and bending modes do (IR active). Compare with Raman: complementary selection rules (Raman active if polarisability changes).

Anharmonic Oscillator & the Morse Potential Real bonds, overtones, dissociation, and the hot band

Why Anharmonicity?

The harmonic model predicts equally spaced levels continuing to infinity. But real molecules:

  • Have bonds that can break (dissociation) → energy levels must be bounded
  • Show higher-order transitions ($\Delta v = \pm 2, \pm 3$...) — called overtones
  • Have unequally spaced levels that converge near dissociation

The Morse Potential

A better potential energy curve proposed by P. Morse:

Morse Potential $$U(q) = D_e\left(1 - e^{-aq}\right)^2$$

where $D_e$ = dissociation energy (from bottom of well), $a = \sqrt{k/2D_e}$ controls well width, and $q = r - r_e$.

Solving the SE with the Morse potential gives:

Anharmonic Oscillator Energy $$\boxed{\varepsilon_v = \left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)\tilde{\omega}_e - \left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 x_e\tilde{\omega}_e}$$

where $x_e$ is the anharmonicity constant (dimensionless, $x_e \ll 1$). The first term is the harmonic result; the second term is the anharmonic correction that lowers the energy levels.

Unequally Spaced Levels and Dissociation

The gap between consecutive levels:

$$\Delta\varepsilon_v = \varepsilon_{v+1} - \varepsilon_v = \tilde{\omega}_e - 2x_e\tilde{\omega}_e(v+1) = \tilde{\omega}_e[1 - 2x_e(v+1)]$$

The gap decreases as $v$ increases (levels converge). Setting $\Delta\varepsilon_v = 0$ gives the last bound state level:

Last Bound Vibrational Level $$v_F = \frac{1}{2x_e} - 1 \quad (\text{or equivalently, } v_\text{max} \approx \frac{\tilde{\omega}_e}{2x_e\tilde{\omega}_e} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2x_e} - \frac{1}{2})$$

Dissociation Energies $D_0$ and $D_e$

Spectroscopic vs. Thermodynamic Dissociation Energy $$D_0 = \varepsilon_v(v=v_F) - \varepsilon_v(v=0)$$ $$D_e = D_0 + \frac{1}{2}\tilde{\omega}_e \quad \text{(adding back ZPE)}$$
$D_e$ vs $D_0$
$D_e$ is measured from the bottom of the potential well. $D_0$ is the actual bond dissociation energy measured from $v=0$ (the real lowest state). Since ZPE $= \frac{1}{2}\tilde{\omega}_e$ lifts the ground state above the bottom: $D_e = D_0 + \frac{1}{2}\tilde{\omega}_e$. Always $D_e > D_0$.

Overtones and Combination Bands

For anharmonic oscillator, the selection rule relaxes: $\Delta v = \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \ldots$ (though intensity decreases rapidly for $|\Delta v| > 1$).

Fundamental and Overtone Frequencies $$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to1} = \tilde{\omega}_e - 2x_e\tilde{\omega}_e = \tilde{\omega}_e(1-2x_e) \quad\text{(fundamental)}$$ $$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to2} = 2\tilde{\omega}_e - 6x_e\tilde{\omega}_e = 2\tilde{\omega}_e(1-3x_e) \quad\text{(first overtone)}$$ $$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to3} = 3\tilde{\omega}_e(1-4x_e) \quad\text{(second overtone)}$$

The "Hot Band"

The $v=1 \to v=2$ transition gives:

$$\tilde{\nu}_{1\to2} = \tilde{\omega}_e(1-4x_e)$$

This is slightly red-shifted compared to the fundamental $\tilde{\omega}_e(1-2x_e)$. It's called a hot band because it's only visible when $v=1$ is thermally populated (at higher temperatures). At room temperature, $v=1$ has very low population (Maxwell-Boltzmann), so hot bands are usually weak and only visible at high $T$.

Q4 HCl shows a fundamental band at 2886 cm⁻¹ and first overtone at 5668 cm⁻¹. Find $\tilde{\omega}_e$ and $x_e$.
Solution

$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to1} = \tilde{\omega}_e(1-2x_e) = 2886$ cm$^{-1}$ ... (i)

$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to2} = 2\tilde{\omega}_e(1-3x_e) = 5668$ cm$^{-1}$ ... (ii)

From (ii): $\tilde{\omega}_e(1-3x_e) = 2834$ cm$^{-1}$ ... (ii')

Subtract (ii') from (i): $\tilde{\omega}_e x_e = 52$ cm$^{-1}$

From (i): $\tilde{\omega}_e - 2(52) = 2886 \Rightarrow \tilde{\omega}_e = 2990$ cm$^{-1}$

$x_e = 52/2990 = 0.0174$

The Vibrating Rotor (Rotation-Vibration Coupling) P, Q, R branches and the combination band structure

Combined Energy: Vibration + Rotation

A real molecule vibrates and rotates simultaneously. In the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, the energy is additive:

Combined Energy $$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\text{vib} + \varepsilon_\text{rot} = BJ(J+1) - DJ^2(J+1)^2 + \left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)\tilde{\omega}_e - \left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 x_e\tilde{\omega}_e$$

However, there is a subtlety: the rotational constant $B$ depends on the vibrational state $v$, because the average bond length $\langle r^2 \rangle$ changes with $v$:

$$B_v = B_e - \alpha_e\left(v+\frac{1}{2}\right)$$

where $\alpha_e$ is the vibration-rotation coupling constant. Higher vibrational states have slightly longer average bond lengths → slightly smaller $B_v$.

Selection Rules for the Vibrating Rotor

$$\Delta v = \pm 1 \quad \text{(vibrational)} \qquad \Delta J = 0, \pm 1 \quad \text{(rotational)}$$

Note: for a diatomic, $\Delta J = 0$ is forbidden (no Q branch). For polyatomic molecules, $\Delta J = 0$ (Q branch) is allowed when the vibration changes the moment of inertia.

P, Q, R Branches

For $v = 0 \to 1$ transition (fundamental band), considering both vibrational and rotational changes:

R branch ($\Delta J = +1$) $$\tilde{\nu}_R = \tilde{\omega}_e + (B_1+B_0)(J+1) + (B_1-B_0)(J+1)^2 \approx \tilde{\omega}_e + 2B(J+1) \quad (J=0,1,2\ldots)$$
P branch ($\Delta J = -1$) $$\tilde{\nu}_P = \tilde{\omega}_e - (B_1+B_0)J + (B_1-B_0)J^2 \approx \tilde{\omega}_e - 2BJ \quad (J=1,2,3\ldots)$$
Q branch ($\Delta J = 0$, if allowed) $$\tilde{\nu}_Q = \tilde{\omega}_e + (B_1-B_0)J(J+1) \approx \tilde{\omega}_e \quad \text{(all collapse near } \tilde{\omega}_e\text{)}$$
Mnemonic — P Q R Branches
P = Previous (lower $J$, lower frequency, to the left of center)
Q = Quiet/central (same $J$, frequency ≈ $\tilde{\omega}_e$, central line, missing for diatomics)
R = Rising/right (higher $J$, higher frequency, to the right of center)

Introduction to Laser Physics Einstein coefficients, stimulated emission, population inversion

What is a Laser?

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. It produces a highly coherent, monochromatic, collimated beam of light through the process of stimulated emission.

Three Radiative Processes — Einstein's Analysis

Consider a two-level system with states $|1\rangle$ (lower) and $|2\rangle$ (upper), with populations $N_1$ and $N_2$. There are exactly three ways the system can interact with radiation:

Einstein's Detailed Derivation of the A and B Coefficients

Einstein's brilliant insight was to derive the relationship between $A$ and $B$ from thermodynamic equilibrium — without knowing quantum mechanics in detail!

Full Derivation of Einstein Relations (JC Sir, Lecture 1)
  1. At thermal equilibrium, the rate of upward transitions = rate of downward transitions: $$B_{12}\,u(\omega)\,N_1 = B_{21}\,u(\omega)\,N_2 + A_{21}\,N_2$$ where $u(\omega)$ is the radiation energy density per unit frequency.
  2. Solving for $u(\omega)$: $$u(\omega) = \frac{A_{21}/B_{21}}{\frac{B_{12}}{B_{21}}\frac{N_1}{N_2} - 1}$$
  3. From Boltzmann statistics at temperature $T$: $N_1/N_2 = e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}$ (since $E_2 > E_1$). Also, for non-degenerate levels, $B_{12} = B_{21}$ (detailed balance).
  4. So: $u(\omega) = \frac{A_{21}/B_{21}}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1}$
  5. But we know the Planck blackbody distribution: $u(\omega) = \frac{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3}{\pi^2 c^3}\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1}$
  6. Comparing: $\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \frac{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3}{\pi^2 c^3}$ and $B_{12} = B_{21}$
Einstein Relations (Key Results) $$\boxed{\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \frac{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3}{\pi^2 c^3}}$$ $$\boxed{B_{12} = B_{21} \equiv B}$$ $$\boxed{B_{21} = \frac{\pi^2 c^3}{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3 \tau_{sp}}}$$

where $\tau_{sp} = 1/A_{21}$ is the spontaneous emission lifetime. The ratio $A/B \propto \omega^3$ — at higher frequencies (X-rays), spontaneous emission strongly dominates over stimulated; at lower frequencies (microwaves), stimulated emission dominates.

Physical Meaning of Einstein Relations
  • $B_{12} = B_{21}$: Absorption and stimulated emission are equally probable per photon per atom. This is a deep result — the quantum mechanics "doesn't care" which direction the transition goes.
  • $A/B \propto \omega^3$: At optical frequencies, $A \gg B\cdot u(\omega)$ in thermal light. Spontaneous emission completely dominates. To get stimulated emission to dominate, we need an intense coherent field (laser cavity) and population inversion.

Laser Theory — Rate Equations, Gain, and Lasing Condition The complete mathematical treatment of laser amplification

The Line Shape Function $g(\omega)$

Not all atoms interact equally with all photon frequencies. The line shape function $g(\omega)$ describes the probability that an atom interacts with radiation at frequency $\omega$. It's normalised:

$$\int_\omega g(\omega)\,d\omega = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad g(\omega) \sim \frac{1}{\Delta\omega}$$

A narrower line width ($\Delta\omega$ small) means $g(\omega)$ is taller and more peaked — stronger interaction at the resonance frequency. This is why narrower transitions are better for lasers: higher gain at line center.

Transition Rate with Line Shape

Absorption Rate (corrected for line shape) $$\Gamma_{12} = \int B_{12}\,u(\omega)\,N_1\,g(\omega)\,d\omega = \frac{\pi^2 c^3}{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3 \tau_{sp}}\cdot\frac{N_1\,u(\omega)}{g(\omega)^{-1}}$$

For nearly monochromatic radiation where $u(\omega)$ is essentially constant over the linewidth of $g(\omega)$:

$$\boxed{\Gamma_{12} = \frac{\pi^2 c^3 N_1 u(\omega)}{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3 \tau_{sp}}}$$

Beer-Lambert Law from First Principles

Consider a beam propagating along $z$ through a medium. In a thin slice $dz$ with area $S$:

Derivation of Beer-Lambert / Gain Equation (JC Sir, L2)
  1. Number of upward transitions per unit time (absorption) in slice $S\,dz$: $\Gamma_{12}\,\hbar\omega\,S\,dz$
  2. Number of downward transitions (stimulated emission) in slice: $\Gamma_{21}\,\hbar\omega\,S\,dz$
  3. Net energy absorbed by matter from field per unit time = $(\Gamma_{12} - \Gamma_{21})\hbar\omega\,S\,dz$
  4. This equals the intensity change: $[I(z) - I(z+dz)]\,S = -\frac{dI}{dz}\,S\,dz$
  5. Using $I = u(\omega)\cdot c/n_0 = u\cdot c/n_0$ and $\Gamma_{21} = \Gamma_{12}\cdot N_2/N_1$ (since $B_{12}=B_{21}$): $$-\frac{dI}{dz} = \frac{\pi^2 c^2 g(\omega)(N_1-N_2)}{n_0^2\,\omega^2\,\tau_{sp}} \cdot I$$
  6. This gives the key equation: $$\frac{dI}{dz} = -\alpha\,I$$ where the gain/absorption coefficient is: $$\alpha = \frac{\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega)\,(N_1 - N_2)}{n_0^2\,\omega^2\,\tau_{sp}}$$
  7. Solution: $I(z) = I(0)\,e^{-\alpha z}$ — Beer-Lambert Law.
Beer-Lambert Law $$\boxed{I(z) = I(0)\,e^{-\alpha z}}$$ $$\alpha = \frac{\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega)\,(N_1 - N_2)}{n_0^2\,\omega^2\,\tau_{sp}}$$
The Magic of Population Inversion
Normally, $N_1 > N_2$ (Boltzmann). Then $\alpha > 0$ and $I(z) = I(0)e^{-\alpha z}$ — intensity decays (attenuation/absorption).
But if we can achieve $N_2 > N_1$ (population inversion), then $N_1 - N_2 < 0$, so $\alpha < 0$! Then $I(z) = I(0)e^{|\alpha|z}$ — intensity grows exponentially. This is optical amplification / laser action!

Rate Equations for the Two-Level System

Rate Equations $$\frac{dN_2}{dt} = W_{12}(N_1 - N_2) - \Gamma_{21}N_2 = W_{12}(N_1-N_2) - (S_{21}+A_{21})N_2$$ $$\frac{dN_1}{dt} = -W_{12}(N_1-N_2) + \Gamma_{21}N_2$$

where $W_{12}$ = stimulated transition rate per atom (both $B_{12}$ and $B_{21}$ are equal), $S_{21}$ = non-radiative decay, $A_{21}$ = spontaneous emission. Note $N_1 + N_2 = N$ (constant).

Steady-State Solution and Population Inversion Impossibility in Two-Level Systems

Setting $dN_2/dt = 0$ for steady state, and assuming ideal laser (no non-radiative decay):

Steady-State Population Ratio $$\frac{N_2}{N_1} = \frac{W_{12}}{A_{21} + W_{12}}$$ $$\Rightarrow \frac{N_2}{N_1} < 1 \quad \text{always!}$$
Why Two-Level Systems Cannot Lase!
The maximum achievable in a two-level system is $N_2 = N_1$ (when $W_{12} \to \infty$, i.e., pumping infinitely hard). You can never get $N_2 > N_1$ in a two-level system at steady state. This is because every photon that pumps the system up also stimulates it back down with equal probability ($B_{12} = B_{21}$). The system bleaches — it reaches transparency but never gain.

Saturation of Population Inversion

Define the inversion density $\Delta N = N_2 - N_1$ and the normalised line shape function $\tilde{g}(\omega) = g(\omega)/g(\omega_0)$. The population inversion in steady state:

Saturation of Inversion $$\frac{\Delta N}{N} = \frac{-1}{1 + \frac{2W_{12}}{A_{21}}} = \frac{-1}{1 + I/I_s\,\tilde{g}(\omega)}$$
Saturation Intensity $$\boxed{I_s = \frac{\hbar\omega_0^3 n_0^2}{2\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega_0)}}$$

$I_s$ is the intensity at which the inversion is reduced to half its small-signal value. At $I \ll I_s$: $\Delta N/N \approx -1$ (full inversion). At $I \gg I_s$: $\Delta N/N \to 0$ (saturation/bleaching).

The Lasing Threshold Condition

For a laser cavity with gain medium of length $d$, and mirrors of reflectivity $R_1$, $R_2$: a round trip must give net gain ≥ 1.

Derivation of Lasing Condition (JC Sir's Cavity Analysis)
  1. Intensity after traversing gain medium of length $d$ (one way): $I = I_0 e^{-\alpha d}$
  2. Reflection from mirrors $R_1$ and $R_2$: intensity becomes $I_0 e^{-\alpha d} \cdot R_1 \cdot e^{-\alpha d} \cdot R_2 = I_0 R_1 R_2 e^{-2\alpha d}$
  3. Including extra passive losses (scattering, diffraction) $\alpha_1$ per pass: $I_0 R_1 R_2 e^{-2(\alpha+\alpha_1)d}$
  4. For sustained oscillation (threshold), the round-trip intensity must equal the starting intensity: $$R_1 R_2\,e^{-2(\alpha+\alpha_1)d} \geq 1$$
  5. Taking logarithm: $\ln R_1 R_2 \geq 2(\alpha+\alpha_1)d$, i.e., $-\alpha \geq \alpha_1 - \frac{\ln R_1 R_2}{2d}$
  6. Passive cavity lifetime: $1/\tau_c = c\alpha_1/n_0 - c\ln R_1 R_2/(2dn_0)$, giving $n_0/c\tau_c = \alpha_1 - \ln R_1 R_2/(2d)$
  7. Threshold condition: $|\alpha| \geq n_0/(c\tau_c)$, i.e.: $$\frac{\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega)\,(N_2-N_1)}{n_0^2\,\omega^2\,\tau_{sp}} \geq \frac{n_0}{c\tau_c}$$
  8. Rearranging: $$\boxed{(N_2-N_1) \geq \frac{n_0^3\omega^2\tau_{sp}}{\pi^2 c^3\,g(\omega)\,\tau_c}}$$
What the Lasing Condition Tells Us
To lower the threshold (make lasing easier):
  • Low $\omega^2$: lower frequency lasers have lower threshold (less spontaneous emission). This is why CO₂ (IR) lasers have low threshold.
  • Long $\tau_c$ (high cavity Q): better mirrors (high $R_1, R_2$), lower losses → more time for photons to build up.
  • Short $\tau_{sp}$: faster spontaneous decay rate (stronger transition) → more coupling.
  • Large $g(\omega)$ at line center: narrow linewidth → stronger interaction with resonant photons.

Line Broadening Mechanisms in Lasers

Homogeneous Broadening

All atoms interact identically with the radiation field. The line shape is Lorentzian (derived below).

Derivation of Lorentzian Line Shape (JC Sir)
  1. Model the radiating atom as a damped harmonic oscillator. The electric field decays exponentially: $\mathcal{E}(t) = \mathcal{E}_0\,e^{i\omega_0 t}\,e^{-t/2\tau_{sp}}$
  2. The spectrum is the Fourier transform: $\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\omega) = \int_0^\infty \mathcal{E}(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt$
  3. $\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\omega) = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{\frac{1}{2\tau_{sp}} - i(\omega_0 - \omega)}$
  4. The power spectrum (line shape) $g(\omega) \propto |\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\omega)|^2$: $$g(\omega) = \frac{k}{\frac{1}{4\tau_{sp}^2} + (\omega-\omega_0)^2}$$
  5. Normalising ($\int g\,d\omega = 1$): $k = 1/(2\pi\tau_{sp})$, giving: $$\boxed{g(\omega) = \frac{2\tau_{sp}/\pi}{1 + 4\tau_{sp}^2(\omega-\omega_0)^2}} \quad \leftarrow \text{Lorentzian}$$
  6. The FWHM is $\Delta\omega = 1/\tau_{sp}$. Narrower linewidth → larger $g(\omega_0)$ → lower lasing threshold.

Inhomogeneous Broadening (Doppler)

Different atoms have different velocities → different Doppler shifts → each atom "sees" a different resonance frequency. The aggregate line shape is Gaussian. Atoms in a gas laser (like He-Ne) experience primarily Doppler broadening. Solid-state lasers typically have homogeneous broadening.

Q5 Why can population inversion not be achieved with a two-level system? What is the minimum system needed?
Answer

In a two-level system: $B_{12} = B_{21}$, so every photon pumping the system up has the same probability of stimulating a transition back down. In steady state, the maximum achievable is $N_2 = N_1$ (transparency), never inversion ($N_2 > N_1$). You would need to pump infinitely fast to just reach equality, not surpass it.

The minimum is a three-level system (like Ruby laser). The pump excites atoms to level 3. Atoms rapidly decay non-radiatively to long-lived level 2 (metastable, $\tau \sim 10^{-3}$ s for Ruby). This accumulates population in level 2 faster than stimulated emission depletes it → inversion between levels 2 and 1.

A four-level system (like Nd:YAG) is even better: the lower lasing level (level 1') is above the ground state, so it's nearly empty at all times. Much easier to invert. This is why 4-level lasers have lower threshold than 3-level lasers.

  • Rapid non-radiative decay ($10^{-13}$ s) to metastable E₂
  • Population inversion between E₂ and E₁
  • Lasing at 694 nm (red)
  • Pulsed operation (since E₁ is ground state → needs more than 50% of atoms in E₂)

  • Ne achieves population inversion between $3s$ and $2p$ levels
  • Three laser lines: $3s \to 3p$ (3.39 μm IR), $2s \to 2p$ (1.15 μm IR), $3s \to 2p$ (632.8 nm visible red)
  • Continuous wave (CW) operation

  • Lasing entity: CO₂ molecule. Auxiliary gases: N₂ (pumped by discharge, transfers energy to CO₂ asymmetric stretch mode) and He (cools gas)

Lambert-Beer Law in Absorption Spectroscopy

For a sample of concentration $c$ (mol/L) and path length $l$ (cm):

Lambert-Beer Law $$\ln\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right) = \alpha_\nu c l \quad \Rightarrow \quad \log\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right) = \varepsilon_\nu c l$$

where $\varepsilon_\nu = \alpha_\nu/2.303$ is the molar absorption coefficient (extinction coefficient) in units of L·mol$^{-1}$·cm$^{-1}$. The quantity $\log(I_0/I) = $ Optical Density (OD) or Absorbance $A$.

Practical Use
The OD (Optical Density) is dimensionless and additive. Beer-Lambert law is linear in concentration — the basis of quantitative spectrophotometry (measuring unknown concentrations). It fails at high concentrations (aggregation, scattering, saturation effects).
Q6 What is population inversion and why is it thermodynamically unusual?
Answer

In thermal equilibrium, the Boltzmann distribution dictates that the ground state has more population than excited states: $N_1 > N_2$ (assuming $E_1 < E_2$). Population inversion ($N_2 > N_1$) corresponds to a negative temperature in the Boltzmann distribution — physically impossible in true equilibrium.

Therefore, population inversion is a non-equilibrium state that must be actively maintained by continuous pumping (energy input). When the pumping stops, the system relaxes back to equilibrium (Boltzmann distribution) with $N_1 > N_2$. This is why lasers need continuous pump power to maintain CW operation.

Harmonic Oscillator Energy
$\varepsilon_v = (v+\tfrac{1}{2})\tilde{\omega}_e$
$\tilde{\omega}_e = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{k/\mu}$; $\Delta v = \pm 1$
Anharmonic Energy
$\varepsilon_v = (v+\tfrac{1}{2})\tilde{\omega}_e - (v+\tfrac{1}{2})^2 x_e\tilde{\omega}_e$
$x_e$ = anharmonicity constant ($\ll 1$)
Level Spacing (Anharmonic)
$\Delta\varepsilon_v = \tilde{\omega}_e[1-2x_e(v+1)]$
Decreasing spacing → converges at dissociation
Dissociation Energy
$D_e = D_0 + \dfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\omega}_e$
$D_e$ from potential minimum; $D_0$ from $v=0$
Fundamental
$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to1} = \tilde{\omega}_e(1-2x_e)$
Main IR absorption band
First Overtone
$\tilde{\nu}_{0\to2} = 2\tilde{\omega}_e(1-3x_e)$
$\Delta v = 2$; weak intensity
Last Vibrational Level
$v_F = \dfrac{1}{2x_e} - 1$
From $\Delta\varepsilon_v = 0$
Vibrating Rotor $B_v$
$B_v = B_e - \alpha_e(v+\tfrac{1}{2})$
$B$ decreases with vibrational excitation

Laser Physics

Einstein A/B Ratio
$\dfrac{A_{21}}{B_{21}} = \dfrac{\hbar\omega^3 n_0^3}{\pi^2 c^3}$
From Planck distribution comparison
Symmetry of B Coefficients
$B_{12} = B_{21}$
Non-degenerate levels; absorption = stimulated emission rate per atom
Beer-Lambert Law
$I(z) = I(0)\,e^{-\alpha z}$
$\alpha < 0$ for inversion → amplification
Gain/Loss Coefficient
$\alpha = \dfrac{\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega)\,(N_1-N_2)}{n_0^2\,\omega^2\,\tau_{sp}}$
$\alpha > 0$: absorption; $\alpha < 0$: gain (inversion)
Lasing Threshold
$(N_2-N_1) \geq \dfrac{n_0^3\omega^2\tau_{sp}}{\pi^2 c^3\,g(\omega)\,\tau_c}$
$\tau_c$ = passive cavity lifetime
Saturation Intensity
$I_s = \dfrac{\hbar\omega_0^3 n_0^2}{2\pi^2 c^2\,g(\omega_0)}$
Intensity at which inversion is halved
Lorentzian Line Shape
$g(\omega) = \dfrac{2\tau_{sp}/\pi}{1+4\tau_{sp}^2(\omega-\omega_0)^2}$
FWHM $= 1/\tau_{sp}$; homogeneous broadening
Lambert-Beer (Practical)
$A = \log(I_0/I) = \varepsilon_\nu c l$
$A$ = absorbance/OD; $\varepsilon$ in L·mol$^{-1}$·cm$^{-1}$
Two-Level Steady State
$\dfrac{N_2}{N_1} = \dfrac{W_{12}}{A_{21}+W_{12}} < 1$
Max achievable: $N_2 = N_1$ (never inversion)
Quantum Yield
$\Theta = \dfrac{\Gamma}{\Gamma + K_{nr}}$
$\Gamma$ = radiative rate; $K_{nr}$ = non-radiative constant
