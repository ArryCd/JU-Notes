Free Electron Gas & the Drude Model

05 / 02 / 2026 — 12 / 02 / 2026  ·  Kittel / Dekker

Why Solid State Physics?

Solids are distinguished by an extremely high density of matter. This high density leads to very different types of chemical bonding — ionic (NaCl), covalent (diamond), metallic, and van der Waals. The entities that carry current inside a solid are electrons, holes, and ions. Solid state physics is classified as low energy physics, since the probing energies lie between meV and keV — the regime of optical and X-ray radiation.

Energy Scale Context
At room temperature (T = 300 K), the thermal energy kBT ≈ 26 meV. This is the energy scale relevant for electron transport. Optical/EM radiation spans meV to keV, so it couples directly to electron excitations. X-rays (λ ≈ 1 Å, hν ≈ keV) are used to probe crystal structure. In low energy physics, the nucleus is treated as a single entity. Moving to intermediate energy (keV–MeV), nucleons become relevant; at high energy (MeV–GeV), quarks and gluons appear.

Conductivity — The Central Observable

The electrical conductivity σ (or its inverse, resistivity ρ) spans about 26 decades:

Materialσ (Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹)Example
Metal~10⁶Cu, Al, Au
Semiconductor10⁻⁴ – 10²Si, Ge
Insulator~10⁻²⁰Sn-coated In₂O₃ (ITO, special case)

Ohm's law in microscopic form is J = σE. Starting from V = IR and R = ρℓ/A:

V = Iρℓ/A → (V/ℓ)σ = I/A → J = σE

Packing Fractions — Setting the Scene

Before treating electrons as a gas, it helps to understand how tightly atoms are packed. For simple cubic (SC), the lattice parameter a = 2r, so:

PFSC = (4/3)πr³ / a³ = π/6 ≈ 52%

For FCC: a√2 = 4r → r = a√2/4, with 4 atoms/cell:

PFFCC = 4 × (4/3)π(a√2/4)³ / a³ = π√2/6 ≈ 74%

For BCC: PF ≈ 68%. The free electrons live in the remaining void space.

The Free Electron Gas Density

Take Cu as an example: atomic weight 63.546 g/mol, density 8.96 g/cc. Then:

n = (6.023 × 10²³ / 63.546) × 8.96 ≈ 8.5 × 10²² electrons / cc

This is about 1000× denser than a classical ideal gas at STP (2.7 × 10¹⁹/cc), which is why quantum effects become critical.

The Drude Model (1900)

Core Assumptions
1. Free electrons behave as a classical ideal gas (Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics).
2. Electrons undergo instantaneous scattering off ion cores (not off other electrons).
3. Between collisions, electrons travel freely (no interaction with lattice).
4. After a collision, electrons emerge with a thermal velocity characteristic of the local temperature — they have no memory of their velocity before the collision.

Derivation of the Equation of Motion & Drift Velocity

When an electric field E is applied, each electron feels a force F = eE (taking e as positive magnitude, force is in −x for E in +x). Without scattering, this gives constant acceleration — which is unphysical. Drude adds a damping (resistive) force proportional to the instantaneous velocity:

m dv/dt = eE − γv

The damping coefficient γ has dimensions of momentum/length. At steady state (dv/dt = 0), eE = γvd, so γ = eE/vd. Substituting back:

m dv/dt = eE(1 − v/vd)
▶ Full integration: v(t) from rest

Separating variables: ∫ dv/(1 − v/vd) = ∫ (eE/m) dt

−vd ln(1 − v/vd) = (eE/m)t + C

At t = 0, v = 0 (field just switched on, no prior drift): C = 0.

ln(1 − v/vd) = −(eE/mvd)t = −t/τ

where we identify τ = mvd/eE as the relaxation time (mean free time).

v(t) = vd(1 − e−t/τ)

As t → ∞, v → vd (the drift velocity). The relaxation time τ is the characteristic time for the system to reach steady state after the field is turned on — typically τ ~ 10⁻¹⁴ s for metals at room temperature.

Conductivity from the Drude Model

The current density J = nevd. With the steady-state condition eE = mvd/τ, i.e., vd = eEτ/m:

J = ne · (eEτ/m) = (ne²τ/m) E = σE
σ = ne²τ/m    — Drude Conductivity Success: gives σ ~ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹ for metals at room temp

Numerical check for Cu:

n = 8.5×10²² /cc, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg, τCu = 4×10⁻¹⁴ s → σ ~ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹. ✓ (experimentally verified)

The Mean Free Path Problem

From the equipartition theorem, thermal speed vth = √(3kBT/m) ≈ 10⁵ m/s at 300 K. The mean free path is λ = vthτ:

λ = 10⁵ m/s × 10⁻¹⁴ s = 10⁻⁹ m = 10 Å at room temp.

Drude proposed λ ~ 1–10 Å, roughly the interatomic spacing. This seemed reasonable in 1900.

Drude's Fatal Drawback — Low Temperature
At 1–2 K, τ ~ 10⁻¹³ s → λ = 10⁶ × 10⁻¹³ = 10⁻⁷ m = 1000 Å. At low T, the electron should travel thousands of Ångströms without scattering. But crystal lattice spacings are only ~2–3 Å! Why doesn't the electron scatter off every atom? The Drude model gives no answer — this is the biggest failure. The resolution requires quantum mechanics (Bloch waves propagate undisturbed through a perfect periodic lattice).

Check Your Understanding

Why does the Drude model work at room temperature but fail at low temperature?
At room temperature, thermal vibrations (phonons) of the lattice disrupt the perfect periodicity of the crystal. Electrons scatter off these phonons, giving τ ~ 10⁻¹⁴ s and a mean free path of ~10 Å — coincidentally matching what Drude assumed. As temperature falls, phonon density drops, so τ increases and λ grows to ~1000 Å. The Drude model has no mechanism to explain why an electron can travel 1000 Å without scattering in an almost perfectly ordered lattice. The answer (from quantum mechanics) is that an electron's Bloch wave propagates through a perfectly periodic potential without scattering at all — scattering only arises from deviations from periodicity (phonons, impurities, defects).

Hall Effect — Probing Charge Carriers

12 / 02 / 2026  ·  Ashcroft & Mermin

Setup and Physical Picture

Apply electric field E along ŷ (current flows in ŷ) and magnetic field B = B₀ẑ. Electrons moving in −ŷ direction experience a Lorentz force:

F = −e(v × B) = −e[v(−ŷ) × B₀ẑ] = ev B₀ x̂

Electrons accumulate on one face (say −x side), leaving positive ions exposed on the +x side. This charge separation creates a transverse (Hall) electric field EH in x̂. At equilibrium, the Hall force balances the Lorentz force.

Derivation via Drude Equation of Motion

The full Drude equation with both E and B:

m dv/dt = e[E + v×B] − mv/τ

In steady state (dv/dt = 0), and using J = nev → v = J/ne:

eE = p/τ − e(v×B)

Writing out components with B = B₀ẑ:

Ex = −(1/ne)J B₀ + (m/ne²τ)Jx = ρH Jy + ρ Jx
Ey = (m/ne²τ)Jy = ρ Jy
Hall Coefficient
Comparing with Ex = ρH Jy → ρH = −B₀/ne → RH = ρH/B₀ = −1/ne.

The negative sign appears because electrons are the charge carriers. Experimentally, RHne = −1 for monovalent metals.
Failure for Divalent/Trivalent Metals
For Be, Al → RH is positive, meaning positive charge carriers dominate. Drude treating e⁻ as sole carriers predicts RHne = −1 always. But experimentally: Cu → −1.5, Ag → −1.5, Be → +0.2, Al → +0.3. Positive Hall coefficient signals holes as the dominant charge carrier — only explainable via band theory. This is one of the key failures of the Drude model.

Thermal Conductivity & the Wiedemann–Franz Law

12 / 02 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 6

Why Metals Are Good Thermal Conductors

For metals, both electrical conductivity σ ~ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹ and thermal conductivity k are high. This is no coincidence — the same free electrons responsible for electrical conduction also carry thermal energy. In insulators, σ ~ 10⁻²² Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹ and thermal conduction is by phonons only, making them poor electrical conductors but they can still have moderate thermal conductivity.

Fourier's Law and Thermal Current Density

Jq = −k dT/dx    (Fourier's Law)

where k is the thermal conductivity (W/m·K). We treat heat transport using the same kinetic picture as Drude — free electrons carry thermal energy as they diffuse from hot to cold regions.

Drude Derivation of Thermal Conductivity

Consider a 1D rod with temperature gradient dT/dx. An electron at position x had its last collision at x − vτ (coming from the hot side) or x + vτ (from the cold side). The thermal energy it carries is E[T(x ∓ vτ)].

▶ Derivation of k from kinetic theory

Thermal current density from hot→cold: Jhot→cold = (n/2) v E[T(x − vτ)]

Thermal current from cold→hot: Jcold→hot = (n/2) v E[T(x + vτ)]

Net: Jnet = (n/2) v [E(T(x−vτ)) − E(T(x+vτ))]

Taylor expand T(x ± vτ) = T(x) ± vτ(dT/dx):

Jnet = (n/2) v [E(T − ΔT) − E(T + ΔT)] = −nv²τ (dE/dT)(dT/dx)

In 3D, ⟨vx²⟩ = v²/3 (isotropy), so:

k = (1/3) n ⟨v²⟩ τ Cve

where Cve = dE/dT = (3/2)kB per electron (classical).

k = (nv²τ/3)(3kB/2) ... → k = (4/π) kB² (n/m) τ T

(using ⟨v²⟩ = 8kBT/πm from Maxwell speed distribution)

The Wiedemann–Franz Law

Dividing k by σ·T:

k / (σT) = [4/π · kB² n τ T / m] / [ne²τ/m · T] = (4/π)(kB/e)² ≈ constant Wiedemann-Franz Law — 1853 (empirical), 1900 (Drude explanation)
Lorenz Number
L = k/(σT) = (4/π)(kB/e)²

Experimentally, L ≈ 2.45 × 10⁻⁸ W·Ω/K² (Sommerfeld value). Drude gives L = (4/π)(kB/e)² ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻⁸ — within a factor of 2. This agreement is a major success of the Drude model.
Why Drude "Gets Lucky" on Wiedemann–Franz
The classical model overestimates both k (because Cve is overestimated by 3/2 kB) and σ (because ⟨v²⟩ is overestimated by classical vth instead of quantum Fermi velocity). These two errors cancel in the ratio k/σT, giving a result coincidentally close to the correct quantum (Sommerfeld) value. This is a famous case of two wrongs making a right.

Sommerfeld Model — Quantum Free Electrons

19 / 02 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 6

Why We Need Quantum Mechanics

Key Insight
Electrons are quantum particles. They obey Fermi–Dirac statistics, not Maxwell–Boltzmann. The electron gas in a metal is 1000× denser than a classical ideal gas — quantum effects are not a correction, they are dominant. Two electrons with the same quantum numbers (kx, ky, kz, ms) cannot occupy the same state (Pauli Exclusion). This completely changes the picture.

Particle-in-a-Box: 1D to 3D

Electrons are confined within the metal (treated as a box of side L). The Schrödinger equation with V = 0 inside:

−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ

With boundary conditions ψ(0) = ψ(L) = 0 (stationary wave), the solution is:

ψn(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L),    k = nπ/L,    n = 1, 2, 3, ...
E = ℏ²k²/2m = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²)    → E ∝ n²

For n = −1: ψ(x) = −ψ(−x) — linearly dependent on n = +1. Same energy as n = 1, but not a new state → non-degenerate in 1D.

Progressive Wave (Periodic Boundary Conditions)

For a crystal — electrons don't bounce off walls, they circulate. Apply periodic BCs: ψ(x) = ψ(x + L):

ψ(x) = (1/√V) eikx,    k = 2πn/L,    n = 0, ±1, ±2, ...

Each state (k, ms) is doubly degenerate in ±k (opposite momenta). In 3D:

ψ(r) = (1/√V) eik·r,    E = ℏ²k²/2m = ℏ²(kx²+ky²+kz²)/2m

The allowed k-values form a cubic lattice in k-space with spacing 2π/L. Each k-point has volume (2π/L)³.

Density of States in k-space

D(k)dk is the number of states with wavevector magnitude between k and k+dk. The volume of a spherical shell in k-space is 4πk²dk. Dividing by the volume per k-state and including spin:

D(k)dk = 2 × 4πk²dk / (2π/L)³ = Vk²dk/π²

Density of States in Energy D(E)

Using E = ℏ²k²/2m → k = (2mE)^(1/2)/ℏ, 2k dk = 2m dE/ℏ², so kdk = m dE/ℏ²:

D(E)dE = C E1/2 dE    (characteristic 3D parabola) C = (V/2π²)(2m/ℏ²)^(3/2)

This √E shape of D(E) is characteristic of free electrons in 3D — it rises steeply from 0 and forms a parabola.

Fermi–Dirac Distribution & Fermi Energy

19 / 02 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 6

The Fermi–Dirac Distribution

Since electrons are fermions (spin-1/2), they obey Pauli's exclusion principle. The probability that a state of energy E is occupied at temperature T is given by the Fermi–Dirac function:

fD(E) = 1 / [1 + e(E−EF)/kBT] Fermi-Dirac distribution (quantum statistics)

Note: EF here is the chemical potential (Fermi level), which depends weakly on T.

Limiting cases:

At T = 0: fD(E) = 1 for E < EF₀ and fD(E) = 0 for E > EF₀. Perfect step function. All states below EF₀ are filled, all above are empty.
At T ≠ 0: The step softens. States within ~kBT of EF get thermally excited. At room temperature, kBT ≈ 26 meV while EF ≈ 5 eV — so only about 0.5% of electrons near EF are affected. This is why many electronic properties are temperature-independent.
When E − EF ≫ kBT: fD(E) ≈ e−(E−EF)/kBT → recovers Maxwell–Boltzmann. This is the classical limit (dilute systems).

The Fermi Energy EF₀

At T = 0, we fill states up to EF₀. The total number of electrons is:

N = ∫₀EF₀ D(E) dE = C ∫₀EF₀ E1/2 dE = (2/3) C EF₀3/2
EF₀ = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3 For Cu: n = 8.5×10²² /cc → EF₀ ≈ 7 eV. TF = EF/kB ≈ 80,000 K!
The Fermi Temperature is Enormous
The Fermi temperature TF = EF₀/kB ≈ 80,000 K for Cu, compared to room temperature of 300 K. This means the electron gas is always highly degenerate — T ≪ TF at any reasonable temperature. The electrons are, in a sense, at nearly absolute zero all the time. This is why the specific heat of electrons is far smaller than the classical prediction (3/2 kB per electron) — only the fraction T/TF of electrons near EF can actually absorb thermal energy.

Number of Electrons per Energy Interval: N(E)dE

The actual electron count between E and E+dE is D(E) × fD(E) dE:

N(E)dE = D(E) fD(E) dE

At T = 0, this is just C√E for E < EF₀ and 0 above. This product — the filled DOS — is what determines almost all electronic properties of metals.

Sources of Permanent Magnetic Moments

The permanent magnetic moment of an atom has two contributions: orbital angular momentum and spin angular momentum of the electrons.

Orbital Magnetic Moment
An electron revolving in a circular orbit is a current loop. The magnetic moment is:

μ = iA/c = −(e/2mec) × (angular momentum) = −(e/2mec) × ℏ√ℓ(ℓ+1)

The z-component when a field is applied: (μℓ)z = −μB mℓ

where μB = eℏ/2mec is the Bohr magneton (= 9.274 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T).
Spin Magnetic Moment
The electron's intrinsic spin also has a magnetic moment, but with a different (anomalous) g-factor: gs ≈ 2.0023:

(μs)z = −gs(e/2mec) msℏ = ∓μB (for ms = ±1/2)

The spin moment is double the orbital moment per unit of angular momentum — this is the g-factor anomaly explained by Dirac's relativistic quantum mechanics.

Total Angular Momentum and the Landé g-factor

For a multi-electron atom, both L and S contribute. The total angular momentum J = L + S (LS coupling). The total magnetic moment along the z-axis is:

μJ = −g (e/2mec) √J(J+1) ℏ
g = 1 + [J(J+1) + S(S+1) − L(L+1)] / [2J(J+1)] Landé g-factor — derived from projecting μ onto J

Special cases: pure orbital (S=0) → g = 1; pure spin (L=0) → g = 2; general → interpolates between 1 and 2.

Diamagnetism — Classical Theory

16 / 04 / 2026  ·  Kittel

Physical Origin

Diamagnetism is the magnetic analog of Lenz's law. When an external magnetic field H is applied along ẑ, the orbital angular momentum Ma of each electron precesses about ẑ (Larmor precession) with frequency ωL = eH/2mec. This precession creates an induced magnetic moment that opposes the applied field.

Larmor Precession
The equation of motion for the angular momentum precessing in a field:

dMa/dt = ωL × Ma,    where ωL = (eH/2mec) ẑ

ωL ≈ 10¹¹ s⁻¹ for H ~ 10 Tesla. The angular momentum precesses (like a top) without changing its magnitude.

Langevin Diamagnetic Susceptibility

The induced diamagnetic moment per atom (after averaging over precessing orbits):

μdia = −(e/2mec) × meωL⟨ρ²⟩

where ⟨ρ²⟩ = ⟨x²⟩ + ⟨y²⟩ is the mean square of the projected radius onto the x-y plane. By isotropy, ⟨x²⟩ = ⟨y²⟩ = ⟨z²⟩ → ⟨r²⟩ = 3⟨z²⟩, and ⟨ρ²⟩ = 2⟨r²⟩/3. For Z electrons per atom:

Mdia = −NZ (e²/6mec²) H ⟨r₀²⟩
χdia = −NZ e² ⟨r₀²⟩ / (6mec²) ~ −10⁻⁶ Langevin diamagnetic susceptibility — negative, T-independent
Key Features of Diamagnetism
• χ < 0 (always, for all materials — diamagnetism is universal)
• χ is independent of temperature (no thermal fluctuations involved)
• χ ~ −10⁻⁶ (very small, often masked by para or ferro effects)
• Scales with the mean square atomic radius ⟨r²⟩ — heavier atoms (larger) have larger |χdia|
• In orbital frequency: field ν ~ 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ Hz → magnitude of induced diamagnetic term involves frequencies much faster than the Larmor frequency

Classical Paramagnetism — Langevin Theory

16 / 04 / 2026

Physical Picture

Each atom has a permanent magnetic moment μ (from unpaired electrons). At room temperature, thermal agitation randomizes the orientations → net M = 0 with no applied field. When H is applied, the moments try to align with H (energy −μ·H = −μHcosθ), but thermal energy opposes complete alignment. The result is partial alignment described by the Langevin function.

Derivation of the Langevin Result

The potential energy of a dipole at angle θ to H is: PE = −μHcosθ. In classical theory, θ can take any value continuously (unlike quantum theory). The probability of a dipole being at angle θ follows the Boltzmann distribution:

dP ∝ eμHcosθ/kBT sin θ dθ
▶ Full integration — deriving M = nμL(a)

Let α = μH/kBT and x = cosθ → −sinθ dθ = dx. Limits: θ: 0→π, x: 1→−1.

The normalization constant k = an/(4π sinh a) (from integrating the distribution).

Total magnetization M = ∫dM = ∫μcosθ dn:

M = nμ(coth a − 1/a) = nμ L(a)

where L(a) = coth(a) − 1/a is the Langevin function and a = μH/kBT.

Two Limiting Cases

Case 1: High T or Low H (a ≪ 1) — Curie Law
Taylor expand: L(a) ≈ a/3 for small a:

M = nμ(a/3) = nμ²H/(3kBT)

χpara = M/H = nμ²/(3kBT) = C/T    (Curie Law)

where C = nμ²/(3kB) is the Curie constant. χ ∝ 1/T — susceptibility decreases with temperature.
Case 2: High H or Low T (a → large) — Saturation
L(a) → 1 as a → ∞ → M → nμ = Ms (saturation magnetization). All dipoles are perfectly aligned with the field. In practice, achievable only at very low T with very high H (~ 1 Tesla). Saturation for Gd₂(SO₄)₃·8H₂O was first measured and classically predicted reasonably well, but the classical prediction for the saturation value differs slightly from experiment — requiring quantum mechanics.

Quantum Theory of Paramagnetism & Hund's Rules

26 / 04 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 14

Hund's Rules — Finding the Ground State Term

For a free atom with a partially-filled shell, the ground state term is determined by three rules (in order of priority):

Maximize S. Fill electrons with parallel spins first (Pauli: no two electrons with same quantum numbers). Σms = S gives maximum spin.
Maximize L. Given maximum S, maximize total orbital angular momentum L = Σmℓ.
J = |L − S| if the shell is less than half-filled; J = L + S if the shell is more than half-filled; J = 0 if exactly half-filled.
Example: Fe²⁺ (3d⁶)
Configuration: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fill the d-subshell (ℓ = 2, mℓ = 2,1,0,−1,−2):
↑↓ in mℓ=2, ↑ in mℓ=1,0,−1,−2

i) Σms = S = 2 (4 unpaired spins × 1/2 each)
ii) L = Σmℓ = 2 + 1 + 0 + (−1) + (−2) + 2 = 2
iii) More than half-filled → J = L + S = 4
Term: ²ˢ⁺¹LJ = ⁵D₄
Example: Fe³⁺ (3d⁵)
Half-filled d-shell: all 5 mℓ with ↑ spin
S = 5/2, L = 0 (Σmℓ = 2+1+0−1−2 = 0), J = |L−S| = 5/2
Term: ⁶S5/2. Note: L = 0 means purely spin contribution to moment!

Quantum Theory of Paramagnetism

The key difference from classical Langevin theory: mJ is quantized (takes values −J, −J+1, ..., +J), not continuous. The potential energy when B is applied: E = g μB mJ H.

The total magnetization M is a thermal average over these (2J+1) discrete orientations:

▶ Derivation of quantum paramagnetism — Brillouin function

Let x = gμBH/kBT. The partition sum:

Z = ΣmJ=−J+J emJx = eJx[1 + e−x + ... + e−2Jx] = sinh[(J+½)x] / sinh(x/2)

The magnetization M = ng μB J (d/dx) ln Z:

M = ngμB J BJ(a)    where a = gJμBH/kBT

and the Brillouin function:

BJ(a) = [(2J+1)/2J] coth[(2J+1)a/2J] − [1/2J] coth(a/2J)
Brillouin Function Limits
High T / Low H (a → 0): BJ(a) → (J+1)a/3J → Curie Law:
χ = nμB² peff² / (3kBT)   where peff = g√J(J+1) is the effective Bohr magneton number

High H / Low T (a → ∞): BJ(a) → 1 → M → Ms = ngJμB (saturation)

Special J = ½: B½(a) = tanh(a) — simplest case, two-level system!

Orbital Quenching in Transition Metal Ions

Why L = 0 for 3d Transition Metals?
For rare earth (4f) ions, the theory with g√J(J+1) works perfectly because 4f electrons are shielded by 5s and 5p shells from crystal field effects.

For 3d transition metal ions, the outermost d orbitals are directly exposed to the electric crystal field of neighboring ions. This electric field interaction is much stronger than the magnetic interaction. It "locks" the orbital angular momentum direction to the crystal axes, so L precesses rapidly and its time-average is zero. This is called orbital quenching (crystal field quenching).

Consequence: For 3d metals, use only spin → peff = gs√S(S+1) ≈ 2√S(S+1), and theory matches experiment!

Ferromagnetism — Weiss Theory & Exchange Interaction

03 / 05 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 15

What is Ferromagnetism?

Ferromagnets exhibit spontaneous magnetization — a net magnetic moment exists even in zero applied field (below the Curie temperature θf). Once magnetized to saturation, they remain magnetic even after removing the field (permanent magnets). Above θf, the spontaneous order disappears and the material becomes paramagnetic.

The Weiss Molecular Field Theory (1907)

Weiss proposed that ferromagnetism arises from an enormous internal molecular field Heff that aligns all moments parallel. This effective field is proportional to the magnetization itself:

Heff = Hext + λM

where λ is the Weiss constant (molecular field constant). Note: this is a mean-field theory — each spin sees the average magnetization of all other spins, not their actual fluctuating values.

Self-Consistent Equations for Spontaneous Magnetization

Using the quantum paramagnetism result M = Ms BJ(a) with a = gJμBHeff/kBT and Heff = λM (no external field):

M/Ms = BJ(a)    and    a = gJμBλM / kBT = (Ms kBT a) / (ngJ²μB² λ J)

These are two simultaneous equations in M and a, solved graphically:

Graphical Solution
Plot both equations: (1) M/Ms = BJ(a) — the Brillouin S-curve, and (2) M/Ms = (kBT/ngJ²μB²λJ) × a — a straight line through the origin.

• If T > θf: line slope > initial slope of BJ → only intersection at origin → M = 0 (paramagnetic)
• If T = θf: line is tangent to BJ at origin (critical point)
• If T < θf: line intersects BJ at a non-zero value → spontaneous M ≠ 0 (ferromagnetic)

The Curie Temperature from Weiss Theory

At T = θf, the straight line is tangent to BJ(a) at a = 0. The initial slope of BJ(a) is (J+1)/3J. Setting equal:

kBθf / ngJ²μB²λJ = J(J+1)/3J → θf = nλg²μB² J(J+1) / (3kB)
θf = nλμ² / (3kB)    (with μ = effective magnetic moment) Curie Temperature proportional to Weiss constant λ

The Curie–Weiss Law (Above θf)

For T > θf, using the small-a limit of BJ and Heff = H + λM:

χ = M/H = C / (T − θ)    (Curie–Weiss Law)

where θ is the paramagnetic Curie temperature (close to but not exactly equal to θf). Plotting 1/χ vs T gives a straight line with slope 1/C, x-intercept at θ. This allows experimental determination of both C and θ.

Origin of the Molecular Field — Exchange Interaction

The Mystery of the Molecular Field
Weiss's molecular field λM at saturation corresponds to H ~ μB H/kB ≈ kBθf, giving H ~ 10⁷ Gauss for θf ~ 1000 K. But the actual magnetic dipole-dipole interaction between neighboring atoms only gives ~10² Gauss — five orders of magnitude too small! The origin of the huge molecular field cannot be magnetic; it must be quantum mechanical.

The answer came from Heisenberg (1928): the molecular field arises from the exchange interaction, a purely quantum mechanical effect arising from the combination of the Pauli exclusion principle and Coulomb repulsion.

▶ Heitler–London / Exchange Interaction

For two H atoms a and b with electrons 1 and 2, the total Hamiltonian is H = H₀ + H', where H₀ describes the unperturbed atoms and H' the perturbation (inter-atom interactions including the Coulomb repulsion term 1/r₁₂).

The wavefunction must be antisymmetric (fermion). Two possibilities:

Ψ(1,2) = (1/√2)[ψa(1)ψb(2) ± ψa(2)ψb(1)]

'+' → symmetric spatial → antisymmetric spin (singlet S=0)
'−' → antisymmetric spatial → symmetric spin (triplet S=1)

The energy correction from first-order perturbation theory gives:
E = C ± J   (Coulomb integral C ± exchange integral J)

The exchange integral J = ⟨ψa(1)ψb(2)|H'|ψa(2)ψb(1)⟩

If J > 0: triplet (parallel spins) is lower → ferromagnetism
If J < 0: singlet (antiparallel spins) is lower → antiferromagnetism

The effective Hamiltonian can be written as: Hex = −2J S1·S2 (Heisenberg exchange). The factor of 2 and sign convention vary by textbook.

Non-Integer Magnetic Moments — Slater Band Model

Experimentally, Fe has 2.2 μB/atom and Ni has 0.54 μB/atom — non-integer! This is inexplicable with free-atom theory (must give integer μB). The Slater band model explains this: in a solid, the 3d and 4s bands overlap. Electrons redistribute between bands, giving non-integer 3d occupancies.

Slater Model for Ni (3d⁸4s²)
In the solid: 9.4 electrons in 3d band, 0.6 in 4s band. The 3d band has 5 spin-up levels.
Spin-up 3d: 5 electrons (full). Spin-down 3d: 4.4 electrons.
Net moment = (5 − 4.4) = 0.6 μB ✓ (experiment: 0.54 μB)

Band Theory — Effective Mass & Metal/Insulator/Semiconductor

30 / 04 / 2026 – 06 / 05 / 2026  ·  Kittel Ch. 7–8

Number of States in a Band

For a finite 1D crystal of N atoms with lattice constant a, applying periodic BCs: k = 2πn/L, |k| ≤ π/a → kmax = π/a = πN/L. Allowed k values: ±2π/L, ±4π/L, ..., ±Nπ/L → N values total. With spin, maximum electrons in one band = 2N.

Important Rule
For a crystal with N primitive unit cells, each band can hold exactly 2N electrons (2 spins × N k-states). This directly determines whether a material is a metal, insulator, or semiconductor. In 3D, for a crystal with N unit cells: ns = 2N, np = 6N, nd = 10N, nf = 14N electrons per respective band.

Effective Mass of Electrons

In a periodic lattice, an electron is not truly free — its response to an external force is modified by the lattice potential. We define an effective mass m* that captures this modification.

Electron velocity (group velocity of wave packet): v = (1/ℏ) dE/dk ①
From F = eE and Newton's law: v = (1/eE)(dE/dt). Combined with ①: dE/dt = eE · v → dk/dt = eE/ℏ ③
Acceleration: a = dv/dt = (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(dk/dt) = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)(eE) ④
Comparing F = m*a = eE:
m* = ℏ² / (d²E/dk²)
Physical Meaning of Effective Mass
• m* = m: free electron (E = ℏ²k²/2m, constant curvature)
• m* > m: electron in lower band half — lattice "slows" it down
• m* < m: electron near band edge — lattice "helps" it
• m* < 0: electron in upper half of band — d²E/dk² < 0. Velocity decreases with increasing k. The electron decelerates under a force that would normally accelerate it. This is the origin of holes.

Effective Number of Free Electrons

The effective number of free electrons in a band (those contributing to conductivity):

Neff = Σk fk = (2mL/πℏ²) dE/dk|k₁

For a full band (k1 = π/a): dE/dk = 0 at the zone boundary → Neff = 0 → no conductivity. This is why completely filled bands do not conduct!

Why Na is a Metal and Diamond is an Insulator
Na has 1 valence electron per atom. The 3s band holds 2N electrons, but Na provides only N → half-filled. Neff is maximum → good metal.

Diamond (C) has 4 valence electrons. Two bands (bonding and antibonding sp³ hybrid bands), each holding 2N electrons. 4N electrons fill both bands completely. The gap between valence and conduction band is 5.5 eV — no thermal excitation at room temperature → insulator.

Be has 2 valence electrons → normally insulator (filled 2s band). But the 2s and 2p bands overlap in Be due to the crystal structure → electrons spill into 2p band → conducting metal!

Check Your Understanding

Why is CuO paramagnetic while Cu₂O is diamagnetic?
CuO contains Cu²⁺ (3d⁹). With 9 electrons in the d-subshell (ℓ=2), using Hund's rules: fill mℓ = 2,1,0,−1,−2 each with ↑↓, then one extra ↑ in mℓ=2 → S = 1/2, L = 2, more than half-filled → J = L+S = 5/2. Non-zero J → non-zero magnetic moment → paramagnetic.

Cu₂O contains Cu⁺ (3d¹⁰). All 10 d-electrons fill the subshell completely → S = 0, L = 0, J = 0 → no magnetic moment → diamagnetic (only the universal Langevin diamagnetic contribution from closed shells).

Drude Model

Derive the Drude expression for electrical conductivity σ = ne²τ/m from first principles.
Assumptions: Electrons are free classical particles. Scattering is instantaneous. Between collisions, no electron-electron or electron-ion interaction. After collision, velocity is thermal (random).

Equation of motion: The electric field E exerts force eE. Scattering gives a resistive force −mv/τ (proportional to velocity, τ = mean free time):
m(dv/dt) = eE − mv/τ

Steady state (dv/dt = 0): eE = mvd/τ → vd = eEτ/m

Current density: J = nevd = ne × (eEτ/m) = (ne²τ/m)E = σE

Therefore: σ = ne²τ/m

For Cu: n = 8.5×10²² /cc, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, m = 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg, τ = 4×10⁻¹⁴ s → σ ≈ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹. ✓
Describe the failures of the Drude model. Which failure is the most fundamental?
Failure 1: Low-temperature behavior. Drude gives ρ ∝ 1/τ, and as T → 0, τ → ∞ → ρ → 0. This seems fine, but the required mean free path λ = vthτ ~ 1000 Å at 2K — far larger than the ~3 Å lattice spacing. Why doesn't the electron scatter off every atom? Drude has no answer. (QM resolution: Bloch waves propagate undisturbed through a perfect lattice.)

Failure 2 (Most fundamental): Conductivity of insulators. If insulators have electrons and Drude's n is nonzero, why is σ ~ 0? Drude cannot distinguish between metals and insulators — it predicts conductivity for all materials with electrons. This requires band theory.

Failure 3: Hall effect sign for divalent/trivalent metals. Drude predicts RHne = −1 (negative) for all metals. But Be, Al give positive RH → holes. Drude has no concept of holes.

Failure 4: Electronic specific heat. Classical theory gives Cve = (3/2)kB per electron — contributing 3/2 R per mole. Experimentally, Cve ≈ 0.01 R per mole (100× too small). Sommerfeld's Fermi-Dirac approach gives Cve ≈ (π²/2)(T/TF)kB ≪ kB.

The most fundamental failure is the inability to distinguish metals from insulators — this requires quantum mechanics and band theory.

Hall Effect

Derive the Hall coefficient RH from the Drude model and explain what it tells you about charge carriers.
Setup: E along ŷ, B = B₀ẑ. Current flows in ŷ direction. The steady-state Drude equation with both E and B:
m dv/dt = e[E + v × B] − mv/τ = 0

Using J = nev → v = J/ne, the x-component gives:
eEx = −(eB₀/ne)Jy + (m/neτ)Jx
Ex = −(B₀/ne)Jy + (m/ne²τ)Jx

Hall configuration: No current flows in x (closed circuit): Jx = 0, Jy = J. Then:
Ex = −(1/ne)B₀J = ρHJ

Hall resistivity: ρH = −B₀/ne. Hall coefficient:
RH = ρH/B₀ = −1/ne

Interpretation:
• Negative sign: electrons are negative carriers
• Magnitude gives n directly: n = −1/(RHe)
• For positive RH: holes are the dominant carriers (cannot be explained by Drude)
• Cu: RHne = −1.5 (close to −1; slight deviation due to Fermi surface geometry)
• Al: RHne = +0.3 (band theory: 3 valence e⁻, but complex Fermi surface creates hole pockets)

Fermi–Dirac & Density of States

Derive the density of states D(E) ∝ √E for a 3D free electron gas.
Step 1: k-space setup. With periodic BCs, allowed k-values form a cubic lattice with spacing 2π/L. Each k-state occupies volume (2π/L)³ = 8π³/V in k-space.

Step 2: States in spherical shell. D(k)dk = number of states with k in [k, k+dk]:
D(k)dk = 2 × (4πk²dk) / (2π/L)³ = Vk²dk/π² (factor 2 for spin)

Step 3: Convert to energy. E = ℏ²k²/2m → k = (2mE/ℏ²)^(1/2). Differentiating: 2k dk = (2m/ℏ²) dE → k dk = (m/ℏ²) dE.

D(E)dE = (V/π²) × k × (m/ℏ²) dE = (V/π²) × (2mE/ℏ²)^(1/2) × (m/ℏ²) dE

D(E) = C E^(1/2) = (V/2π²)(2m/ℏ²)^(3/2) E^(1/2)

This parabolic (√E) shape of D(E) is characteristic of free electrons in 3D. In 2D, D(E) = constant; in 1D, D(E) ∝ E^(−1/2). The actual DOS is different when band structure is included.
What is the Fermi energy and why is it important? Calculate EF₀ for a free electron gas.
Definition: EF₀ is the highest energy occupied by electrons at T = 0. At T = 0, the Fermi-Dirac function is a step: f(E) = 1 for E < EF₀, 0 for E > EF₀.

Derivation: Total electron count N = ∫₀^(EF₀) D(E) dE:
N = C ∫₀^(EF₀) E^(1/2) dE = C (2/3) EF₀^(3/2)
→ EF₀ = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²N/V)^(2/3) = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)^(2/3)

For Cu: n = 8.5×10²² /cc = 8.5×10²⁸ /m³
EF₀ ≈ 7 eV. Fermi temperature TF = EF₀/kB ≈ 80,000 K.

Importance:
• Most electronic properties (conductivity, specific heat, magnetism) are determined by electrons near EF
• Since TF ≫ Troom, the electron gas is always "degenerate" — classical Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics are completely wrong
• The Fermi velocity vF = ℏkF/m ≈ 10⁶ m/s (much faster than thermal velocity assumed by Drude)
• The Fermi level determines the chemical potential and hence electrochemical behavior of materials

Magnetism

Derive the Brillouin function for quantum paramagnetism and show that it reduces to the Curie law for high temperatures.
Setup: Each atom has total angular momentum J with (2J+1) quantized projections mJ = −J,...,+J. Applied field H, potential energy E = −μzH = −gμBmJH.

Let x = gJμBH/kBT. The partition function:
Z = Σ e^(mJ/J)x = e^(Jx) × Σ₀^(2J) e^(−nx/J) = sinh[(J+½)x/J] / sinh(x/2J)

Magnetization M = ngJμB⟨mJ/J⟩ = ngJμB d(ln Z)/d(Jx):

M = ngJμB BJ(a) where a = gJμBH/kBT and
BJ(a) = [(2J+1)/2J] coth[(2J+1)a/2J] − [1/2J] coth(a/2J)

High-T limit (a ≪ 1): coth(y) ≈ 1/y + y/3 for small y:
BJ(a) ≈ [(2J+1)/2J][2J/(2J+1)a + (2J+1)a/6J] − [1/2J][2J/a + a/6J]
= 1/a + [(2J+1)²/6J²]a − 1/a − a/12J²
= [(4J²+4J+1)/6J² − 1/12J²] a = [(J+1)/3J] a

M ≈ ngJμB × (J+1)a/3J = ng²μB² J(J+1)H / 3kBT

χ = M/H = C/T where C = ng²μB² J(J+1)/3kB — this is the Curie Law.

The effective magnetic moment: peff = g√J(J+1), so χ = nμB²peff²/3kBT.
Using Hund's rules, find J, L, S and the ground state term for Cr³⁺ and Mn²⁺. Calculate their effective Bohr magneton numbers.
Cr³⁺ (Z=24): [Ar]3d³
mℓ: 2 1 0 (−1, −2 empty); each with ↑
i) S = 3/2 (Σms = 3 × 1/2 = 3/2)
ii) L = Σmℓ = 2+1+0 = 3
iii) Less than half-filled → J = |L−S| = |3 − 3/2| = 3/2
Term: ⁴F3/2
g = 1 + [J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)] / [2J(J+1)]
= 1 + [(3/2)(5/2)+(3/2)(5/2)−3×4] / [2×(3/2)(5/2)]
= 1 + [15/4 + 15/4 − 12] / [15/2] = 1 + (15/2 − 12)/(15/2) = 1 − 1/5 = 2/5... (recalc)
Spin-only: peff = 2√(S(S+1)) = 2√(3/2×5/2) = 2√(15/4) = √15 ≈ 3.87 μB

Mn²⁺ (Z=25): [Ar]3d⁵
Half-filled d-shell: mℓ = 2,1,0,−1,−2, each ↑
i) S = 5/2
ii) L = Σmℓ = 0
iii) Half-filled → J = S = 5/2 (since L = 0)
Term: ⁶S5/2
Since L = 0 → only spin contributes: peff = g√J(J+1) = 2√(5/2 × 7/2) = 2×√(35/4) = √35 ≈ 5.92 μB
Experimental: ≈ 5.9 μB ✓ (perfect agreement)
What is the exchange interaction? Why can't magnetic dipole-dipole interaction explain ferromagnetism?
Why dipole-dipole fails:
The magnetic dipole-dipole interaction energy between two atomic moments at distance r ~ 3 Å apart:
Udip ~ μ₀μB²/r³ ~ (4π×10⁻⁷)(9.3×10⁻²⁴)²/(3×10⁻¹⁰)³ ~ 10⁻²⁵ J ~ 10⁻⁶ eV
Corresponding temperature: T ~ U/kB ~ 0.01 K.
But Fe is ferromagnetic up to θf = 1043 K → need energy ~ 0.09 eV → dipole energy is 10⁵ too small!

Exchange interaction:
For two electrons in orbitals ψa and ψb, the total wavefunction must be antisymmetric. The energy has two solutions:
E = C ± J (Coulomb integral C, exchange integral J)
The triplet (parallel spins, S=1) has energy C − J; singlet (antiparallel, S=0) has C + J.
If J > 0: triplet is lower → parallel alignment → ferromagnetism.
The exchange integral J ~ e²/4πε₀ × overlap integral ~ 0.1 eV. This matches the energy scale for ferromagnetism!

The effective Hamiltonian is: Hex = −2J S1·S2 (Heisenberg model). J > 0 gives ferromagnetic coupling; J < 0 gives antiferromagnetic.
Explain the Curie–Weiss law and what it tells us about ferromagnetism. How is θf related to the exchange interaction?
Derivation: For T > θf, the material is paramagnetic. Using the Weiss molecular field Heff = H + λM and the paramagnetic Brillouin result (small-argument limit):
M = nμ²(H + λM) / (3kBT)
M[1 − nμ²λ/3kBT] = nμ²H/3kBT
χ = M/H = C/(T − θ)
where C = nμ²/3kB (Curie constant) and θ = Cλ = nλμ²/3kB.

Key features:
• χ → ∞ as T → θ (the Curie temperature)
• Plot 1/χ vs T: straight line, slope = 1/C, x-intercept = θ
• Experimentally, θ < θf (paramagnetic Curie T is slightly below ferromagnetic Curie T due to short-range order above θf)

Relation to exchange:
The Weiss constant λ is related to the exchange integral J by:
λ = 2zJ/ngμB² (z = number of nearest neighbors)
So θf ∝ λ ∝ J — a larger positive exchange integral gives a higher Curie temperature. For Fe (θf = 1043 K), the exchange integral J ~ kBθf/z ~ 0.1 eV.

Band Theory

Derive the effective mass m* = ℏ²/(d²E/dk²) and explain when m* can be negative.
Step 1: Group velocity.
An electron in a band has velocity given by the group velocity of its wave packet:
v = dω/dk = (1/ℏ)(dE/dk) ①

Step 2: Response to external field.
Under electric field E, force F = eE (e is magnitude). The work done in time dt:
dE = F·v dt = eE v dt
Also dE = (dE/dk)(dk/dt) dt, so comparing:
(dE/dk)(dk/dt) = eE × (1/ℏ)(dE/dk) → dk/dt = eE/ℏ ②

Step 3: Acceleration.
a = dv/dt = d/dt[(1/ℏ)(dE/dk)] = (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(dk/dt)
Substituting ②: a = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²) × eE ③

Step 4: Define m*.
From Newton: F = m*a = eE → eE = m* × (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²) × eE
Therefore: m* = ℏ² / (d²E/dk²)

When m* is negative:
d²E/dk² < 0 in the upper half of a band (near the Brillouin zone boundary). Here, as k increases, the velocity actually decreases — the electron decelerates under a force that would normally accelerate it. This means it behaves as if it has negative mass. Equivalently, we can treat the "absence of such an electron" (a hole) as a positive charge carrier with positive effective mass. This is the fundamental origin of holes in semiconductor physics.
