Probing Matter — Scales & the Big Picture

Topic · What is matter made of?

The quest to understand matter is really the quest to answer one question: what is the smallest indivisible unit of matter? The ancient idea of atoms has been replaced — through centuries of deeper probing — by a hierarchy of structure that extends to scales far smaller than anything imaginable.

The Hierarchy of Matter

Why High Energy = Small Scale
By the de Broglie relation, λ = h/p. A probe can only resolve features larger than its wavelength. To probe smaller distances, you need shorter λ, which means larger momentum p, which means higher kinetic energy. This is why particle physics requires enormous accelerators — the LHC probes scales of ~10⁻⁹ m using protons accelerated to 6.5 TeV each.
The Goal of Particle Physics
To identify the truly elementary (structureless) constituents of matter and the fundamental forces between them. As of today, the Standard Model describes matter in terms of 6 quarks, 6 leptons, and 5 force-carrying bosons (photon, W±, Z⁰, gluon, Higgs). No substructure has been found in any of these — they appear point-like down to ∼10⁻¹⁸ m.

Questions — Section 1

Why is it not enough to just build a more powerful microscope to see inside a proton?
Optical microscopes are limited by the wavelength of visible light (∼400–700 nm = 4–7 × 10⁻⁵ cm). The proton is ∼10⁻¹³ cm — roughly 8 orders of magnitude smaller than visible light wavelengths. Even X-ray or electron microscopes don't get close. To probe inside a proton, you need a particle with de Broglie wavelength λ ≲ 10⁻¹³ cm, which by λ = hc/E gives E ≳ hc/(10⁻¹³ cm) ≈ 1.24 GeV. This is why particle accelerators (which can produce beams at GeV–TeV energies) are the "microscopes" of particle physics.
What does it mean for a particle to be "elementary"?
A particle is called elementary if it has no observed internal structure — it behaves as a point particle in all experiments performed so far. This is always provisional: historically, atoms were thought to be elementary (Greek: atomos = indivisible), then the nucleus, then the proton. Today, quarks and leptons are treated as elementary because no scattering experiment at any accessible energy has revealed any substructure. An operational definition: a particle is elementary if its cross-section at high energy is consistent with a point-like charge distribution.

Classification of Subatomic Particles

Topic · The particle zoo and how to organise it

By the mid-20th century, experiments had revealed dozens of subatomic particles. The first task was to organise them. The primary classification is based on spin (quantum of angular momentum) and whether the particle feels the strong nuclear force.

The Master Classification Tree

The Spin-Statistics Connection

The classification into fermions and bosons is not arbitrary — it is a consequence of the spin-statistics theorem (Pauli, 1940), one of the deepest results in quantum field theory:

Spin-Statistics Theorem
Fermions (half-integer spin: ½, 3/2, …) obey the Pauli Exclusion Principle — no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. Their wavefunction is antisymmetric under particle exchange: ψ(1,2) = −ψ(2,1). This underlies all of chemistry and the structure of matter.

Bosons (integer spin: 0, 1, 2, …) have no such restriction — many identical bosons can occupy the same state (Bose-Einstein condensation). Their wavefunction is symmetric: ψ(1,2) = +ψ(2,1). Force-carrying particles are bosons.

Antiparticles

Predicted by Dirac's relativistic wave equation (1928) and confirmed experimentally (1932), every particle has a corresponding antiparticle with the same mass and spin but all charges (electric, colour, lepton number, etc.) reversed.

Note — self-conjugate particles
Some particles are their own antiparticle — they are self-conjugate. Examples: the photon (γ), the neutral pion (π⁰), the Z⁰ boson, and the η meson. The neutron is not self-conjugate — it has a distinct antineutron with B = −1. The decisive test is baryon number B or lepton number L: if both are zero, the particle may be self-conjugate (though still may not be — depends on other quantum numbers).

Questions — Section 2

Why must at least two photons be produced in electron-positron annihilation? Why not just one?
In the centre-of-mass (CM) frame of the e⁺e⁻ pair at rest, the total momentum is zero. A single photon always carries nonzero momentum (p = E/c ≠ 0), so it would violate conservation of momentum. Therefore, at minimum, two photons must be emitted in opposite directions so their momenta cancel to give zero net momentum. The two photons each have energy E = mec² = 0.511 MeV (when the pair is essentially at rest). This 511 keV γ-ray line is a characteristic signature of positron annihilation used in PET scanning.
What is the difference between a baryon and a meson in terms of quark content and quantum numbers?
Baryons are composed of three quarks (qqq). Each quark has B = +1/3, so a baryon has B = 3 × 1/3 = +1. Baryons are fermions (half-integer spin) because combining 3 spin-½ particles gives half-integer total spin (½ or 3/2). Examples: p (uud), n (udd), Λ (uds), Ω⁻ (sss).

Mesons are composed of one quark and one antiquark (qq̄). A quark has B = +1/3, antiquark has B = −1/3, so B = 0 for mesons. Mesons are bosons (integer spin) since combining spin-½ and spin-½ gives spin 0 or 1. Examples: π⁺ (ud̄), K⁰ (ds̄), J/ψ (cc̄). All mesons are unstable.

Leptons — Properties & Discovery

Topic · The lightest fermions and their antiparticles

Leptons are the simplest elementary particles — structureless, spin-½ fermions that do not participate in the strong nuclear interaction. There are six leptons (plus six antileptons), arranged in three generations of increasing mass.

Key Properties of All Leptons
1. Point-like — no observed internal structure down to ∼10⁻¹⁸ m
2. Spin ½ — obey Fermi-Dirac statistics, subject to Pauli exclusion
3. No strong interaction — feel only gravity, weak force, and (if charged) electromagnetism
4. Three conserved lepton numbers: Le, Lμ, Lτ — each separately conserved (approximately)

Discovery of the Positron — Anderson's Experiment (1932)

The positron was the first antiparticle ever observed, confirming Dirac's 1928 prediction. Carl Anderson discovered it while studying cosmic rays using a cloud chamber in a magnetic field.

The Key Argument — Why Lead Was Needed
Without the lead plate, one cannot tell which direction the particle was moving. A negative particle moving upward curves the same way as a positive particle moving downward. Anderson inserted the lead plate precisely to break this degeneracy. Since momentum is lost in the plate (r = mv/qB decreases), the more curved (smaller radius) arc is the exit side. Combined with the curvature direction in the known B field, this uniquely determines both the charge sign and the direction of motion. The ionisation density along the track (proportional to the particle's charge²/v²) confirmed the particle had electron mass, not proton mass.

Lepton Number Conservation

Each generation of lepton carries its own conserved quantum number Le, Lμ, Lτ. For leptons: L = +1; for antileptons: L = −1; for all others: L = 0.

Example — Muon Decay
μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄e + νμ

Check Le: 0 → +1 + (−1) + 0 = 0 ✓
Check Lμ: +1 → 0 + 0 + 1 = 1 ✓

The process μ⁻ → e⁻ + γ is forbidden — it would violate Lμ = 1 → 0 and Le = 0 → 1. The branching ratio is <10⁻¹¹, an extraordinarily tight constraint.

Questions — Section 3

The muon has the same charge and spin as the electron but is 207 times heavier. It decays in 2.2 μs. Why does it not decay into just an electron and a photon?
The decay μ⁻ → e⁻ + γ is forbidden by lepton number conservation. Assigning Lμ(μ⁻) = +1 and Le(e⁻) = +1: the initial state has Lμ = 1, Le = 0. The final state has Lμ = 0, Le = 1. Both muon and electron lepton numbers change — this violates separate conservation of Lμ and Le. The allowed decay μ⁻ → e⁻ + ν̄e + νμ preserves both: Lμ changes 1 → 0 + 0 + 1 = 1 ✓ and Le changes 0 → 1 − 1 + 0 = 0 ✓. The neutrinos carry away the "missing" lepton numbers.
Why is the electron stable while the muon decays? What would happen if lepton number were not conserved?
The electron is stable because it is the lightest charged lepton. Any decay would have to produce a lighter charged particle — but there is none. The only lighter particle with charge −1 would need to be a lighter lepton, which doesn't exist. It also cannot decay into only neutral particles (photons) and conserve electric charge. So by conservation of electric charge alone, the electron is absolutely stable.

If lepton number were not conserved, the muon could decay via μ⁻ → e⁻ + γ (much faster, dominant channel). The branching ratio for this mode is <4.2 × 10⁻¹³, an exquisitely sensitive test. If seen, it would signal physics beyond the Standard Model (e.g., supersymmetric particles in loops). Experiments like MEG-II search for this decay.
Why did Anderson need a magnetic field in the cloud chamber to discover the positron?
The cloud chamber makes visible the ionisation trails left by charged particles passing through supersaturated vapour. Without a magnetic field, the trail is a straight line and tells you only the direction of travel — not the charge sign or momentum. In a magnetic field B, a particle of charge q and momentum p follows a circular arc with radius r = p/(qB). From r one gets the momentum. The sign of the curvature tells you the sign of the charge (positive charge curves one way, negative charge curves the other). Without B, Anderson could not have distinguished a positron from an electron.

Neutrinos — From Hypothesis to Oscillation

Topic · The ghost particles of the universe

The Beta Decay Puzzle — Why Pauli Needed a New Particle

In β-decay, a nucleus transforms: A(Z,N) → A(Z+1,N−1) + e⁻. If only the nucleus and electron exist in the final state, energy-momentum conservation requires the electron to have a unique, fixed energy (like in α-decay). But experiments revealed a continuous spectrum:

Pauli's Letter (1930) — The Desperate Remedy
Wolfgang Pauli wrote his famous letter beginning "Dear Radioactive Ladies and Gentlemen" at a physics conference in Tübingen in 1930, proposing a "neutron" (later renamed neutrino by Fermi) to save the law of conservation of energy in β-decay. He required the new particle to be:

(1) electrically neutral, (2) spin-½, (3) very light mass, (4) very weakly interacting. He admitted: "I have done a terrible thing; I have postulated a particle that cannot be detected." It took 23 years to prove him wrong.

Detection of the Electron Antineutrino — Cowan & Reines (1953)

The key reaction used was inverse beta decay:

p + ν̄e → e⁺ + n inverse β-decay

The signature: a positron annihilates immediately (two 511 keV photons), then the slow neutron is captured by cadmium a few microseconds later (more photons). This delayed coincidence is a very specific signature.

The Cowan-Reines Experiment — Setup
A nuclear reactor at Hanford, USA emits ∼5 × 10¹² ν̄e/cm²/s. A large tank filled with water + cadmium chloride was placed near the reactor core. Liquid scintillators surrounded the tank. Despite the enormous flux, only a few interactions per hour were observed — confirming that the interaction cross-section is ∼10⁻⁴³ cm² (extraordinarily small). Frederick Reines was awarded the Nobel Prize 1995.

Neutrino Oscillation and the Solar Neutrino Problem

The Sun generates energy via the proton-proton chain, producing only νe. The flux predicted by the Standard Solar Model (SSM) is well-calculated. But experiments (Davis's Homestake Mine chlorine detector, 1968) found only ⅓ of the predicted νe — the "Solar Neutrino Problem".

Why Oscillation Requires Mass
In quantum mechanics, a neutrino created as νe (a flavour eigenstate) is actually a quantum superposition of three mass eigenstates (ν1, ν2, ν3) with different masses m₁, m₂, m₃. Each mass eigenstate evolves with a different phase e−iEkt/ℏ. If all masses were equal (including zero), all phases would evolve identically and the superposition would never change — no oscillation. Only when Δm² = mi² − mj² ≠ 0 do the phases diverge, causing the flavour composition to change with time. Thus oscillation ↔ mass differences ↔ at least some mν ≠ 0.

The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Takaaki Kajita (Super-Kamiokande, Japan) and Arthur B. McDonald (SNO, Canada) for the discovery of neutrino oscillations.

Questions — Section 4

How does the detection of neutrinos via inverse beta decay work, and why is the cross-section so small?
Inverse beta decay: p + ν̄e → e⁺ + n. The cross-section is approximately σ ≈ GF²Eν²/π where GF ≈ 1.166 × 10⁻⁵ GeV⁻² is the Fermi constant. At typical reactor antineutrino energies (Eν ∼ few MeV), σ ∼ 10⁻⁴³ cm². To compare: the proton geometric cross-section is ∼10⁻²⁶ cm². So the neutrino cross-section is 10¹⁷ times smaller than the geometric size of the proton.

The smallness arises because neutrinos interact only via the weak force. The weak force is "weak" because its carriers (W±, Z⁰ bosons) are very heavy (∼80–90 GeV), suppressing the interaction amplitude at low energies by a factor of (E/MW)² ≪ 1. This is why a neutrino can travel through a light-year of lead with only 50% probability of interaction.
What experiment resolved the solar neutrino problem and how?
The Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (SNO) in Canada used 1000 tonnes of heavy water (D₂O). SNO could detect three reactions:

(1) Charged current: νe + d → p + p + e⁻ — sensitive only to νe
(2) Neutral current: νx + d → p + n + νx — sensitive to all flavours equally
(3) Elastic scattering: νx + e⁻ → νx + e⁻ — mostly νe but some contribution from νμ, ντ

The neutral current reaction (2) measures the total neutrino flux regardless of flavour. SNO found that the total flux agreed with SSM predictions, but the νe flux was only ∼1/3 of that. This proved that νe had oscillated into νμ and ντ en route — not that the Sun was producing fewer neutrinos. The solar neutrino problem was solved.

Hadrons — Mesons, Baryons & Strange Particles

Topic · The strongly interacting particle zoo

Hadrons are composite particles that interact via the strong nuclear force. By the early 1960s, over 100 hadron species had been discovered — earning the description "particle zoo". Understanding this zoo required the quark model.

Mesons
Quark content: q + q̄  (baryon number B = 0)
Spin: integer (0, 1, 2…) → bosons
All are unstable. Outside nuclei, they are short-lived products of high-energy collisions.
Heavier mesons decay into lighter mesons and ultimately into stable e⁻, ν, γ.
Baryons
Quark content: q + q + q  (baryon number B = +1)
Spin: half-integer (½, 3/2…) → fermions
The proton is the only stable baryon. All others eventually decay into protons.
Baryons with S ≠ 0 are called hyperons.

Strange Particles — The Production-Decay Puzzle

Kaons (K) and hyperons (Λ, Σ, Ξ, Ω) exhibited two bizarre properties when first observed in the early 1950s:

The Strange Behaviour
(1) Always produced in pairs: π⁻ + p → K⁰ + Λ is observed, but π⁻ + p → K⁰ alone never occurs.

(2) Production is fast, decay is slow: Strange particles are created via the strong interaction (τ ∼ 10⁻²³ s) but decay via the weak interaction (τ ∼ 10⁻¹⁰ s). This 13-order-of-magnitude discrepancy was completely mysterious until strangeness was introduced.

In 1953, Gell-Mann and Nishijima independently proposed a new additive quantum number strangeness S, conserved in strong and electromagnetic interactions but violated (by ΔS = ±1) in the weak interaction. This explained both puzzles simultaneously.

Questions — Section 5

The Δ⁺⁺ baryon has quark content uuu. Why does this apparently violate the Pauli Exclusion Principle, and how is it resolved?
The Δ⁺⁺ has spin-3/2, which means all three quarks have their spins aligned (spin-up). All three quarks are also of the same flavour (u) and in the same spatial ground state (l=0). This seems to put three identical fermions in the same quantum state — violating Pauli exclusion.

The resolution is colour charge. Each quark carries one of three colour charges: red (R), green (G), blue (B). The three u quarks in Δ⁺⁺ each have a different colour: one R, one G, one B. Their overall wavefunction is:

ψtotal = ψspatial × ψspin × ψcolour

The colour part ψcolour = (RGB − RBG + BRG − BGR + GBR − GRB)/√6 is totally antisymmetric under exchange. The spatial and spin parts are symmetric. The product is antisymmetric overall — satisfying Pauli. This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the existence of colour.
Why does π⁰ decay to two photons but π⁺ cannot?
π⁰ is electrically neutral (charge = 0), so it can decay to γγ (photons carry no charge — no charge conservation problem). The dominant decay π⁰ → 2γ (∼98.8%) proceeds via the axial anomaly (a quantum field theory effect involving quark triangle diagrams). The lifetime is 8.7 × 10⁻¹⁷ s — very short because it is an electromagnetic process.

π⁺ has electric charge +1. Photons are neutral, so π⁺ → γγ would violate charge conservation — strictly forbidden. The only way π⁺ can decay is by changing a quark flavour (u → d or d → u), which requires the weak interaction. Hence π⁺ decays weakly: π⁺ → μ⁺ + νμ (~99.99%), with a much longer lifetime of 26 ns.

Quantum Numbers — Organising the Particle Zoo

Topic · Conserved charges and selection rules

Quantum numbers are conserved additive charges that label particles and constrain which reactions are allowed. Each conservation law corresponds to a symmetry (Noether's theorem).

The Key Quantum Numbers

Baryon Number (B)
B = +1 for baryons, −1 for antibaryons, 0 for all others.
Conserved in all known interactions.
Equivalent: each quark has B = +1/3, antiquark B = −1/3.
Strangeness (S)
S = −1 for s quark, S = +1 for s̄ antiquark, 0 for u,d quarks.
Conserved in strong and EM interactions.
Violated (ΔS = ±1) in weak interactions.
Hypercharge (Y)
Y = S + B (Gell-Mann's definition).
After charm etc.: Y = S + B + C + B' + T.
Conserved same as strangeness.
Isospin (I, I₃)
Introduced by Heisenberg (1932). Nuclear force is charge-independent — p and n are two states of the same "nucleon" (isospin doublet I=½).
I₃ distinguishes multiplet members. Conserved in strong interactions only.

The Gell-Mann–Nishijima Formula

Independently derived by Gell-Mann (USA) and Nishijima (Japan) in 1953–1955, this formula relates the electric charge to other quantum numbers:

q/e = I₃ + (S + B)/2 = I₃ + Y/2 Gell-Mann–Nishijima relation

After the discovery of charm, bottom, and top quarks, the generalised formula is:

q/e = I₃ + (S + B + C + B' + T)/2 generalised G-N relation (C = charmness, B' = bottomness, T = topness)
▶ Verify the G-N formula for the proton, neutron, K⁺, and Ω⁻
Proton (p, uud): B=+1, S=0 → Y=1; I₃=+½ (member of nucleon doublet). q/e = +½ + 1/2 = +1 ✓

Neutron (n, udd): B=+1, S=0 → Y=1; I₃=−½. q/e = −½ + 1/2 = 0 ✓

K⁺ (us̄): B=0, S=+1 → Y=1; I₃=+½ (member of kaon doublet). q/e = +½ + 1/2 = +1 ✓

Ω⁻ (sss): B=+1, S=−3 → Y=−2; I₃=0 (singlet). q/e = 0 + (−2)/2 = −1 ✓

The formula works for all hadrons when the correct quantum numbers are assigned.

The Eightfold Way — Symmetry of Hadrons

Topic · SU(3) flavour symmetry, weight diagrams & the prediction of Ω⁻

By 1961, Murray Gell-Mann (USA) and Yuval Ne'eman (Israel) independently discovered that hadrons could be organised into geometrically beautiful families using the SU(3) Lie group. Gell-Mann named this the Eightfold Way, borrowing from the Buddhist Noble Eightfold Path. The theory not only organised known particles but predicted an unknown one — the Ω⁻.

Weight Diagrams
Hadrons with the same spin-parity JP are plotted on a 2D diagram with I₃ on the horizontal axis and Y (hypercharge) on the vertical axis. The resulting geometric pattern reflects the underlying SU(3) symmetry. Lines of constant electric charge Q = I₃ + Y/2 run diagonally across the diagram.

Pseudoscalar Meson Octet (JP = 0⁻)

Baryon Decuplet (JP = 3/2⁺) and the Prediction of Ω⁻

The Group Theory Behind It — SU(3)
The three lightest quarks (u, d, s) form a triplet that transforms as the fundamental representation 3 of the SU(3) symmetry group. Combining quarks and antiquarks according to the group theory rules gives:

Mesons (qq̄): 3 ⊗ 3̄ = 8 ⊕ 1 → an octet + a singlet (nonet of 9)
Baryons (qqq): 3 ⊗ 3 ⊗ 3 = 10 ⊕ 8 ⊕ 8 ⊕ 1 → a decuplet, two octets, a singlet

This explains why only 1, 8, and 10-member families appear — they are the dimensions of the allowed SU(3) representations.

Questions — Section 7

How did the Eightfold Way predict the Ω⁻? What properties were predicted and how well did the discovery confirm them?
By 1961, the baryon decuplet (JP = 3/2⁺) had 9 known members: Δ (S=0, I=3/2, 4 members), Σ* (S=−1, I=1, 3 members), Ξ* (S=−2, I=½, 2 members). The SU(3) decuplet must have exactly 10 members. The triangular pattern had an empty spot at S=−3, I=0 (singlet).

Gell-Mann predicted at the 1962 CERN conference: a new particle Ω⁻ must exist with (1) charge = −1 [from q/e = 0 + (−3+1)/2 = −1], (2) strangeness S = −3, (3) isospin I = 0 (singlet), (4) mass ≈ 1680 MeV/c² [by extrapolating the mass spacing ~150 MeV between rows: 1385 + 150 ≈ 1535 + 150 ≈ 1680], (5) decay via weak interaction (ΔS=1): Ω⁻ → Λ⁰ + K⁻ or Ξ⁰ + π⁻.

The Ω⁻ was discovered at Brookhaven in 1964 with mass 1672.5 MeV/c², charge −1, strangeness −3, and exactly the predicted decay modes. The agreement was spectacular and the discovery convinced the physics community that the Eightfold Way (and hence the quark model) was correct.

The Quark Model — Structure of Hadrons

Topic · Fractional charges, confinement & the discovery of all six quarks

In 1964, Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig (independently) proposed that hadrons are built from more fundamental particles. Gell-Mann named them quarks (from James Joyce's Finnegans Wake: "Three quarks for Muster Mark!"). Zweig called them aces.

Confinement — Why You Can Never See a Free Quark
Despite extensive searches, no free quark has ever been detected. The force between quarks (carried by gluons) does not decrease with distance — it is approximately constant at large distances, like a rubber band. To pull two quarks apart, you must supply enough energy to create a new quark-antiquark pair from the vacuum before the quarks separate. So instead of getting a free quark, you always get new hadrons. This property is called colour confinement, and it is a non-perturbative property of Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD).

Evidence for Quarks — Deep Inelastic Scattering (1968–69)

The first direct (though indirect!) evidence came from the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC). This was the modern analogue of Rutherford's gold foil experiment.

The J/ψ — "November Revolution" (1974)

Why J/ψ Was So Significant
The J/ψ meson (cc̄) was extraordinarily narrow in width (Γ ≈ 0.093 MeV) — its lifetime was 1000× longer than expected for a particle of its mass. This is explained by the OZI rule: J/ψ → hadrons requires the cc̄ pair to annihilate, which is suppressed if the products cannot contain c quarks. The slow decay served as proof that the charm quantum number was a real new conserved quantity, and cc̄ was a genuine charmonium bound state (the QCD analogue of positronium). This discovery confirmed the GIM mechanism and established the quark model on firm footing — it is called the "November Revolution" of particle physics.

Questions — Section 8

Why is the top quark so special compared to other quarks? What happens to a top quark before it can form a hadron?
The top quark has mass ≈ 172 GeV/c² — heavier than a gold atom. Its lifetime is approximately 5 × 10⁻²⁵ s, which is shorter than the QCD hadronisation time scale (∼1/ΛQCD ∼ 10⁻²³ s). This means the top quark decays (via t → W⁺ + b, essentially 100% of the time) before it has time to form a hadron. Therefore, there are no "top mesons" or "top baryons" — the top quark is the only quark we can study in its "bare" state, without the confusing effects of confinement. This makes the top quark a unique laboratory for testing QCD and electroweak physics at high scales.
What is Bjorken scaling and what does it tell us about quark structure?
In deep inelastic scattering e + p → e + X, the cross-section is written in terms of dimensionless structure functions F₁(x,Q²) and F₂(x,Q²), where Q² is the momentum transfer and x = Q²/(2Mν) is the Bjorken scaling variable (M = proton mass, ν = energy transfer). Bjorken predicted in 1968 that at large Q², the structure functions should become functions of x only — independent of Q². This is called Bjorken scaling.

It was observed at SLAC and has a simple interpretation: if the proton contains point-like constituents (partons/quarks), then the cross-section at high Q² is just the sum of electron-parton cross-sections, which depends only on the fraction x of proton momentum carried by the struck quark. The observed scaling confirmed the proton has a granular structure — point-like quarks inside. (Technically, scaling is violated logarithmically at very large Q² due to QCD gluon emissions, which provides a further precision test of QCD.)

Bohr's Compound Nucleus — Ghoshal's Experiment

Topic · Independence hypothesis & nuclear reaction cross-sections

Niels Bohr (1936) proposed the compound nucleus model for nuclear reactions: when a projectile a strikes target nucleus X, a highly excited intermediate nucleus C* is formed. The key claim — the independence hypothesis — is that C* decays independently of how it was formed.

Bohr's Independence Hypothesis
A nuclear reaction proceeds in two completely separate stages:

Formation: a + X → [C*] — the projectile merges with the target. The available energy is shared among all nucleons (typically 8+ MeV per nucleon for a 64-nucleon compound). The compound nucleus "forgets" how it was formed.
Decay: [C*] → Y + b — the compound nucleus decays by emitting particles. The decay probability depends only on the excitation energy, angular momentum, and parity of C*, not on which projectile formed it.
Mathematically: σ(a+X → Y+b) = σc(a+X) × Γb/Γtotal

Ghoshal's Experimental Verification

S.N. Ghoshal (1950) devised an elegant test. He found two different ways to produce the same compound nucleus [⁶⁴Zn*]:

The Cross-Section Formulae

σ(p,n) = σc(p) × Γn/Γ      σ(α,n) = σc(α) × Γn/Γ single neutron emission
σ(p,np) = σc(p) × Γnp/Γ      σ(α,np) = σc(α) × Γnp/Γ neutron + proton emission
σ(p,2n) = σc(p) × Γ2n/Γ      σ(α,2n) = σc(α) × Γ2n/Γ two neutron emission

Dividing corresponding equations eliminates the partial width Γx/Γ, giving the key prediction:

σ(α,n) / σ(p,n) = σ(α,np) / σ(p,np) = σ(α,2n) / σ(p,2n) = σc(α) / σc(p) Ghoshal's independence hypothesis test — all ratios must be equal
Ghoshal's Result
When Ghoshal measured the excitation functions (cross-section vs energy), the shapes of the curves for the same exit channel but different entrance channels were identical (when plotted against the energy above threshold for compound nucleus formation). The ratios were indeed constant — beautifully confirming Bohr's compound nucleus model. This remains one of the cleanest experimental verifications of a nuclear model.

Questions — Section 9

State and explain Bohr's independence hypothesis. Why is the compound nucleus formed at such high excitation energy?
Statement: When a projectile is captured by a target nucleus to form a compound nucleus C*, the probability of a particular decay mode of C* is independent of how C* was formed. Mathematically: σ(a+X→Y+b) = σc(a) × Γb/Γ, where σc(a) is the cross-section for compound nucleus formation and Γb/Γ is the branching ratio for decay to channel b.

Why high excitation? When the projectile enters the target nucleus, it immediately undergoes multiple collisions with the nucleons inside. Each nucleon-nucleon collision shares the kinetic energy among more particles. With A~60 nucleons, the initial projectile energy (say 10 MeV) gets distributed among all nucleons, giving each only ~0.17 MeV — far below the ~8 MeV binding energy per nucleon needed to escape. So the compound nucleus is "trapped" in a highly excited state. The energy is quickly thermalised, and the system retains no memory of the initial configuration — just total energy, angular momentum, and parity.
In Ghoshal's experiment, the energy scales for p+⁶³Cu and α+⁶⁰Ni reactions are different. How did Ghoshal compare the cross-sections?
The same compound nucleus [⁶⁴Zn*] is formed in both cases, but at different incident energies. The excitation energy E* of the compound nucleus is:

E*(p+⁶³Cu) = Q1 + Tp × mp/(mp + mCu) [CM kinetic energy + Q-value]
E*(α+⁶⁰Ni) = Q2 + Tα × mα/(mα + mNi)

Ghoshal converted the laboratory projectile energies to excitation energies of the compound nucleus. He then plotted both cross-sections as functions of E* (the common variable — excitation energy of [⁶⁴Zn*]). The shapes of corresponding curves (same exit channel, different entrance) were identical when plotted this way, confirming the independence hypothesis. The ratio of cross-sections was constant = σc(α)/σc(p), consistent with the Coulomb barrier penetration formula.
