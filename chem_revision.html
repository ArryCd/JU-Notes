01
CFT for Octahedral, Tetrahedral & Square Planar

Crystal Field Theory models the electrostatic interaction between the central metal ion and the surrounding ligands (treated as point charges). It explains colour, magnetism, and stability of coordination compounds.

Octahedral

d-orbital Splitting — Δo

Ligands approach along ±x, ±y, ±z axes. Orbitals pointing towards ligands experience greater repulsion.

eg: dz², dx²−y² → +0.6Δo (+6Dq)
t2g: dxy, dxz, dyz → −0.4Δo (−4Dq)

Barycentre rule: 2(+6Dq) + 3(−4Dq) = 0 ✓

Tetrahedral

d-orbital Splitting — Δt

Ligands approach along face diagonals. No orbitals point directly at ligands — splitting is smaller.

e: dz², dx²−y² → −0.6Δt (−6Dq')
t2: dxy, dxz, dyz → +0.4Δt (+4Dq')
Δt = (4/9)Δo ≈ 0.44Δo

Note: e is lower, t₂ is higher — inverted vs octahedral.

Square Planar

d-orbital Splitting (derived from octahedral by removing two axial ligands)

Removing z-axis ligands lowers dz² drastically. Energy order (lowest to highest):

dxz, dyz < dz² < dxy < dx²−y²

The HOMO–LUMO gap (dxy → dx²−y²) = Δsp ≈ 1.3Δo. Very large splitting → almost always low spin, 4 pairs for d8.

⚠ Key Comparison
Δsp ≈ 1.3Δo >> Δo > Δt ≈ 0.44Δo. Tetrahedral complexes are almost always high spin because Δt is too small to force pairing.
PRACTICE QUESTIONS — CFT
Q1. Why is Δt = (4/9)Δo and not equal to Δo?
Three reasons combine:
  • Fewer ligands: tetrahedral has 4 vs 6 in octahedral (factor ~2/3).
  • No direct approach: no tetrahedral orbital points directly at a ligand (factor ~2/3 again).
  • Combined: (2/3)(2/3) = 4/9 ≈ 0.44.
So Δt = (4/9)Δo.
Q2. In an octahedral field, which orbitals are in the eg set and why are they raised in energy?
dz² and dx²−y² form the eg set. Ligands in an octahedral complex approach along the x, y, and z axes. These two orbitals have their lobes pointing directly toward the ligands, so they experience maximum electrostatic repulsion → raised by +0.6Δo (= +6Dq) above the barycentre.
Q3. How does square planar geometry arise from octahedral? Sketch the energy level diagram.
Start with an octahedral complex. Gradually remove the two axial (z-axis) ligands to infinity. As they leave:
  • dz² drops sharply (loses axial repulsion).
  • dx²−y² rises (equatorial ligands now dominate even more).
  • dxy rises moderately; dxz and dyz drop.
Final order (↑ energy): dxz ≈ dyz < dz² < dxy < dx²−y².
The large gap between dxy and dx²−y² (≈1.3Δo) is why d8 metals with strong-field ligands always adopt square planar geometry and are diamagnetic.
02
CFSE Calculation for dn Systems

Crystal Field Stabilization Energy (CFSE) is the net energy gained by a complex due to the d-orbital splitting. It equals the total energy of electrons in split levels minus their energy at the hypothetical barycentre.

Octahedral CFSE
Each t2g electron: −0.4Δo (−4Dq)
Each eg electron: +0.6Δo (+6Dq)

CFSE = (−0.4 × nt₂g + 0.6 × neg)Δo

Add +P per extra pair if comparing to free ion for pairing energy correction.

Tetrahedral CFSE
Each e electron: −0.6Δt (−6Dq')
Each t2 electron: +0.4Δt (+4Dq')

CFSE = (−0.6 × ne + 0.4 × nt₂)Δt

Since Δt = 4/9 Δo, convert: multiply by 4/9 to express in Δo.

📌 Exam Tip — CFSE Calculation Steps
1. Determine dn (oxidation state → count d electrons).
2. Fill orbitals using Hund's rule (high spin) OR pair up (low spin, strong field ligands).
3. Count nt₂g and neg (oct) or ne and nt₂ (tet).
4. Apply formula. For tetra, use Δt = 4/9 Δo to convert to Δo units.
PRACTICE QUESTIONS — CFSE
Q1. Calculate CFSE for [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (high spin, d⁶) in units of Δo.
Fe²⁺ is d⁶. H₂O is a weak-field ligand → high spin.
HS d⁶ configuration: t2g⁴ eg²
CFSE = (4 × −0.4 + 2 × 0.6)Δo = (−1.6 + 1.2)Δo = −0.4Δo
Q2. Calculate CFSE for [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ (low spin, d⁶). CN⁻ is a strong-field ligand.
Co³⁺ is d⁶. CN⁻ is strong field → low spin.
LS d⁶: t2g⁶ eg⁰
CFSE = (6 × −0.4 + 0 × 0.6)Δo = −2.4Δo (+ 2P for extra pairing energy cost).
Net CFSE is much larger in magnitude than HS → complex is highly stable and kinetically inert.
Q3. What is the CFSE of a tetrahedral d⁵ complex (high spin) in terms of Δo?
Tetrahedral d⁵ HS: e² t2³
CFSE = (2 × −0.6 + 3 × 0.4)Δt = (−1.2 + 1.2)Δt = 0
This makes sense — d⁵ HS in both geometries has zero CFSE (half-filled, symmetrical). This explains why Mn²⁺ (d⁵) doesn't show strong preference for either geometry.
Q4. For a d⁸ metal ion, compare octahedral vs tetrahedral CFSE. Which is preferred?
Oct d⁸ (always HS): t2g⁶ eg² → CFSE = (6×−0.4 + 2×0.6)Δo = −1.2Δo
Tet d⁸ HS: e⁴ t2⁴ → CFSE = (4×−0.6 + 4×0.4)Δt = (−2.4 + 1.6)×(4/9)Δo = −0.8×(4/9)Δo ≈ −0.36Δo
Octahedral CFSE (−1.2Δo) >> Tetrahedral (−0.36Δo) → d⁸ strongly prefers octahedral (or square planar with strong-field ligands like CN⁻).
03
Normal & Inverse Spinel Calculations

Spinels have the general formula AB₂O₄. The oxygen ions form an FCC lattice with tetrahedral (A-sites, 8 per unit cell, 1/8 occupied) and octahedral (B-sites, 16 per unit cell, 1/2 occupied) holes.

Normal Spinel

A[B₂]O₄

A²⁺ occupies Tetrahedral sites.
B³⁺ occupies Octahedral sites.

Example: MgAl₂O₄
Mg²⁺ → Tet | Al³⁺ → Oct

Degree of inversion λ = 0

Inverse Spinel

B[AB]O₄

Half of B³⁺ occupies Tetrahedral sites.
A²⁺ + remaining B³⁺ occupy Octahedral sites.

Example: Fe₃O₄ (magnetite)
Fe³⁺[Fe²⁺Fe³⁺]O₄
Fe³⁺ → Tet | Fe²⁺,Fe³⁺ → Oct

Degree of inversion λ = 1

How to Predict — CFSE Method

Step-by-step: Calculate site preference energy

  1. Identify the two metal ions (A²⁺ and B³⁺) and their d-electron counts.
  2. Calculate CFSE for each ion in tetrahedral (Δt) AND octahedral (Δo) sites.
  3. Calculate octahedral site preference energy (OSPE) = CFSE(oct) − CFSE(tet).
  4. The ion with larger OSPE goes to the octahedral site.
  5. If both have significant OSPE → inverse; if A²⁺ has little/no OSPE → normal.
Worked Example: Fe₃O₄

Why is magnetite (Fe₃O₄) an inverse spinel?

Fe₃O₄ = Fe²⁺Fe₂³⁺O₄. Need to place Fe²⁺ (d⁶) and Fe³⁺ (d⁵) in tet and oct sites.

Fe³⁺ (d⁵, HS): CFSE(oct) = 0 | CFSE(tet) = 0 → OSPE = 0
Fe²⁺ (d⁶, HS): CFSE(oct) = −0.4Δo
            CFSE(tet) = −0.27Δo [from −0.6×(4/9)Δo]
            OSPE(Fe²⁺) = −0.4 − (−0.27) = −0.13Δo (prefers oct)

Fe³⁺ has zero OSPE → no preference → it can go into tetrahedral. Fe²⁺ prefers octahedral. Result: inverse spinel Fe³⁺[Fe²⁺Fe³⁺]O₄.

Worked Example: ZnFe₂O₄

Why is zinc ferrite a normal spinel?

Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰): CFSE everywhere = 0 → OSPE = 0
Fe³⁺ (d⁵, HS): CFSE everywhere = 0 → OSPE = 0

Both have zero OSPE. However, Zn²⁺ has an electrostatic preference for tetrahedral sites (it's a closed-shell d¹⁰ ion with no CFSE contribution). ZnFe₂O₄ is a normal spinel: Zn²⁺[Fe₂³⁺]O₄.

PRACTICE QUESTIONS — SPINELS
Q1. What is the difference between normal and inverse spinel? Give one example of each.
Normal spinel A[B₂]O₄: A²⁺ ions in tetrahedral holes, B³⁺ in octahedral holes. Example: MgAl₂O₄ (Mg²⁺ in tet, Al³⁺ in oct).

Inverse spinel B[AB]O₄: B³⁺ split between tet and oct; A²⁺ in octahedral. Example: Fe₃O₄ (Fe³⁺ in tet, Fe²⁺+Fe³⁺ in oct).
Q2. Predict the structure (normal/inverse) of NiFe₂O₄. Ni²⁺ is d⁸.
Ni²⁺ (d⁸): CFSE(oct, HS) = −1.2Δo | CFSE(tet, HS) = (−0.6×4 + 0.4×4)×(4/9)Δo = −0.8×(4/9) ≈ −0.36Δo
OSPE(Ni²⁺) = −1.2 − (−0.36) = −0.84Δo → very strong octahedral preference

Fe³⁺ (d⁵ HS): OSPE = 0.

Ni²⁺ strongly prefers octahedral → Fe³⁺ goes to tetrahedral. Result: Inverse spinel: Fe³⁺[Ni²⁺Fe³⁺]O₄.
Q3. Why does Co₃O₄ adopt a normal spinel structure?
Co₃O₄ = Co²⁺Co₂³⁺O₄.
Co³⁺ (d⁶, low spin with oxygen): CFSE(oct, LS) = −2.4Δo → huge octahedral preference.
Co²⁺ (d⁷, HS): CFSE(oct) = −0.8Δo | CFSE(tet) ≈ −0.27Δo → OSPE = −0.53Δo.

Both prefer octahedral, but Co³⁺ preference is much stronger. Co³⁺ occupies all octahedral sites → Co²⁺ must go to tetrahedral → normal spinel: Co²⁺[Co₂³⁺]O₄.
04
Electronic Transitions
Type I

d–d Transitions

Electrons jump between split d-orbitals (t2g ↔ eg). Energy = Δ (visible light) → colour.

hν = Δo (or Δt)
// Absorption of specific wavelength

Selection rules (formally forbidden, but weakly observed):

  • Laporte rule: Δl = ±1 (g→g forbidden)
  • Spin rule: ΔS = 0
  • Relaxed by: vibronic coupling (oct), mixing (tet has no inversion → stronger bands)
Type II

Charge Transfer (CT) Transitions

Electron moves between metal and ligand. Very intense (ε ~ 10³–10⁵ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) — Laporte allowed.

  • LMCT: Ligand → Metal (ligand oxidized, metal reduced)
  • MLCT: Metal → Ligand (metal oxidized)
KMnO₄: O→Mn LMCT → intense purple
K₂Cr₂O₇: O→Cr LMCT → intense orange
Case Study — Co²⁺

Why is [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ pink but [CoCl₄]²⁻ blue?

[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺: Co²⁺ (d⁷), octahedral, Δo ≈ 9000 cm⁻¹
Absorbs red/orange (≈500 nm) → transmits pink/rose

[CoCl₄]²⁻: Co²⁺ (d⁷), tetrahedral, Δt ≈ 3100 cm⁻¹
Absorbs orange/yellow (≈600–650 nm) → transmits blue
  • Cl⁻ is a weak-field ligand → smaller Δ → absorption shifts to longer wavelength (lower energy).
  • Tetrahedral geometry: Δt ≈ 0.44 Δo → even smaller splitting.
  • Also: tetrahedral bands are more intense (no inversion centre → Laporte rule less strictly forbidden).
LMCT Details

KMnO₄ & K₂Cr₂O₇ — Ligand to Metal CT

Both MnO₄⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻ are d⁰ metal centres — no d-d transitions possible. Their intense colours are entirely due to LMCT:

MnO₄⁻: O(2p) → Mn(3d) LMCT
Absorbs green (≈525 nm) → intense purple/violet

Cr₂O₇²⁻: O(2p) → Cr(3d) LMCT
Absorbs blue/violet (≈350–450 nm) → intense orange

High oxidation states (+7 for Mn, +6 for Cr) → low-lying empty d-orbitals → easy electron transfer → low energy CT → visible absorption → strong colour.

PRACTICE QUESTIONS — ELECTRONIC TRANSITIONS
Q1. Why are d–d transitions Laporte-forbidden, yet coordination compounds are coloured?
d–d transitions involve Δl = 0 (d→d), violating the Laporte selection rule (Δl = ±1 required). In a centrosymmetric (octahedral) complex, g→g transitions are forbidden.

However, the rule is relaxed by:
  • Vibronic coupling: molecular vibrations momentarily destroy the inversion centre, mixing d and p character → weakly allowed transitions.
  • Spin-orbit coupling: mixes states of different multiplicity.
Result: small but non-zero molar absorptivities (ε ~ 1–100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹) → pale colours observed.
Q2. Why are charge-transfer bands so much more intense than d–d bands?
CT transitions involve electron movement between ligand and metal orbitals (e.g., O 2p → metal d). These are transitions between orbitals of different symmetry and different atom type, so:
  • They are Laporte-allowed (Δl = ±1 satisfied for the transition as a whole).
  • There's also better orbital overlap across the charge-transfer pathway.
ε (CT) ~ 10³–10⁵ vs ε (d–d) ~ 1–100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Hence KMnO₄ is intensely coloured at small concentrations.
Q3. [Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is very faintly pink (ε ≈ 0.03). Explain this extremely weak absorption.
Mn²⁺ is d⁵ in a high-spin complex. All d orbitals are singly occupied (t2g³ eg²). Any d–d transition must involve electron spin-flip (changing a parallel-spin electron to antiparallel) because all possible target configurations have a different spin multiplicity.

This means transitions are both Laporte-forbidden (g→g) and spin-forbidden (ΔS ≠ 0). Doubly forbidden → ε ≈ 0.01–0.1 → extremely pale colour.

Square Planar Magnetic Moments

Square Planar: Almost Always Diamagnetic for d⁸

In square planar geometry, the dx²−y² orbital is so high in energy that all 8 d-electrons pair into the four lower orbitals:

d⁸ sq. planar: (dxz,dyz)⁴ (dz²)² (dxy)² (dx²−y²)⁰
n = 0 → μ = 0 BM (diamagnetic)

Examples: [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, [PdCl₄]²⁻, [PtCl₄]²⁻, [AuCl₄]⁻

PRACTICE QUESTIONS — MAGNETIC MOMENT
Q1. A complex has μ = 4.90 BM. What is the likely d-configuration?
μ = 4.90 BM → solve: √[n(n+2)] = 4.90 → n(n+2) = 24 → n = 4 unpaired electrons.
This corresponds to high-spin d⁴ octahedral (t2g³ eg¹) or high-spin d⁶ octahedral (t2g⁴ eg²). Examples: Cr²⁺ (d⁴), Fe²⁺ (d⁶) as high-spin complexes.
Q2. [CoF₆]³⁻ has μ ≈ 5.0 BM while [Co(CN)₆]³⁻ is diamagnetic. Explain.
Both are Co³⁺ (d⁶) octahedral complexes. The difference is the field strength of the ligand:
  • F⁻ is weak field: Δo < P → high spin → t2g⁴ eg² → 4 unpaired → μ ≈ 4.90 BM.
  • CN⁻ is strong field: Δo > P → low spin → t2g⁶ → 0 unpaired → diamagnetic.
This is a classic example of why we must determine strong/weak field before counting unpaired electrons.
Q3. Calculate μ for [NiCl₄]²⁻ (tetrahedral). Ni²⁺ is d⁸.
Tetrahedral Ni²⁺ d⁸: Since Δt is always small, tetrahedral complexes are always high spin.
Tetrahedral d⁸: e⁴ t2⁴ → fill 4+4 = 8 electrons with max unpaired in each level.
e: ↑↓ ↑↓ (4 electrons, 0 unpaired in e set since 4 fill 2 orbitals)
t2: ↑↓ ↑↓ ↑↓ wait — t2 has 3 orbitals, 4 electrons → 2 paired + 2 unpaired? No:
t2⁴: ↑↓ ↑ ↑ = 2 unpaired in t2 | e⁴: ↑↓ ↑↓ = 0 unpaired.
Total: n = 2 → μ = √[2(4)] = √8 ≈ 2.83 BM.
This matches experimental values for tetrahedral Ni²⁺ complexes.
06
Structure Prediction from Ligands

For a given metal ion, the geometry adopted depends on the field strength of the ligand. The key insight is the relationship between CFSE and the pairing energy P.

Ni²⁺ (d⁸) — Three Geometries

The Classic Ni²⁺ Example

Octahedral
Ligands: H₂O, NH₃, en
[Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺, [Ni(H₂O)₆]²⁺
Bond angles: ~90°
μ ≈ 2.83 BM (2 unpaired)
Tetrahedral
Ligands: Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻
[NiCl₄]²⁻, [NiBr₄]²⁻
Bond angles: ~109°28'
μ ≈ 2.83 BM (2 unpaired)
Square Planar
Ligands: CN⁻, dmgH, diphosphines
[Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, Ni-dimethylglyoximate
Bond angles: 90° (planar)
μ = 0 BM (diamagnetic — all electrons paired)
Decision Rule

When does Ni²⁺ go square planar?

Square planar requires the Δsp ≈ 1.3Δo to exceed the pairing energy P. For Ni²⁺:

  • Weak field (Cl⁻, Br⁻): Δ small → tetrahedral preferred (avoids pairing, fewer ligands).
  • Intermediate field (H₂O, NH₃): octahedral → maximises number of ligands, modest CFSE.
  • Strong field (CN⁻, PR₃): Δsp large enough to force pairing → square planar, diamagnetic, maximum CFSE for d⁸.
Ligand Field Example Ligands Ni²⁺ Geometry Unpaired e⁻ Min. Bond Angle
Weak Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, NCS⁻ Tetrahedral 2 109°28′
Medium H₂O, NH₃, py, en Octahedral 2 90°
Strong CN⁻, CO, dmgH⁻, PR₃ Square Planar 0 90° (planar)
📌 General Rule for d⁸ Metals
2nd and 3rd row transition metals (Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺, Au³⁺) are always square planar regardless of ligand, because their larger d-orbitals give intrinsically large Δ. First-row d⁸ (Ni²⁺) can be all three geometries depending on ligand field strength.
PRACTICE QUESTIONS — STRUCTURE PREDICTION
Q1. Predict the geometry of [Ni(en)₂]²⁺ and [NiCl₄]²⁻. Explain your reasoning.
[Ni(en)₂]²⁺: en (ethylenediamine) is a medium-to-strong bidentate ligand. With 2 bidentate ligands, coordination number = 4. en constrains bond angles close to 90°. But actually [Ni(en)₂]²⁺ tends toward square planar when only 4 N donors present in 2D plane. With strong en ligands → square planar geometry is possible; typically square planar, μ ≈ 0 or slightly paramagnetic depending on exact Δ.

[NiCl₄]²⁻: Cl⁻ is a weak-field ligand. Δ is small, pairing energy not overcome. Geometry: tetrahedral. μ ≈ 2.83 BM (2 unpaired).
Q2. [PtCl₄]²⁻ is square planar and diamagnetic, while [NiCl₄]²⁻ is tetrahedral and paramagnetic. Why?
Both are d⁸ ions with the same ligand (Cl⁻). The difference is the row of the periodic table:
  • Pt²⁺ is a 3rd-row transition metal: 5d orbitals are more diffuse and larger → intrinsically much larger crystal field splitting regardless of ligand strength.
  • For Pt²⁺, even weak-field Cl⁻ creates Δ large enough to force square planar geometry and full pairing.
  • For Ni²⁺, a 1st-row metal: 3d orbitals are smaller → Cl⁻ creates insufficient splitting → remains tetrahedral HS.
As a rule: all 2nd/3rd row d⁸ metals = square planar. 1st row d⁸ = geometry depends on ligand.
Q3. Why is it said that Ni²⁺ with NH₃ gives octahedral geometry (min. angle 90°), with Cl⁻ gives tetrahedral (min. 109°28'), and with CN⁻ gives square planar?
The bond angles directly reflect the geometry:
  • Octahedral: 6 ligands arranged at 90° to each other (along ±x, ±y, ±z). Minimum (and most common) L-M-L angle = 90°. NH₃ is medium field → 6 NH₃ = octahedral.
  • Tetrahedral: 4 ligands at tetrahedral angles. The ideal tetrahedral angle is 109°28'. Cl⁻ is weak field → tends to give tetrahedral.
  • Square planar: 4 ligands in a plane, all at 90° to each other. Strong field CN⁻ creates Δ large enough to pair all electrons and stabilize this geometry enormously for d⁸.
The angles are a geometric consequence of the coordination geometry — they're a diagnostic tool!

CLASS TEST — ALL 8 QUESTIONS ANSWERED
Q1 [2 marks] — Predict the structure of NiFe₂O₄ spinel (normal or inverse).
NiFe₂O₄ contains Ni²⁺ (d⁸) and Fe³⁺ (d⁵).

Step 1 — OSPE of Fe³⁺ (d⁵, HS):
CFSE(oct) = 0, CFSE(tet) = 0 → OSPE = 0 (no preference).

Step 2 — OSPE of Ni²⁺ (d⁸, HS):
CFSE(oct): t2g⁶ eg² → (6×−0.4 + 2×0.6)Δo = −1.2Δo
CFSE(tet): e⁴ t2⁴ → (4×−0.6 + 4×0.4)×(4/9)Δo = −0.8×(4/9) ≈ −0.356Δo
OSPE(Ni²⁺) = −1.2 − (−0.356) = −0.844Δo → strong octahedral preference.

Conclusion: Ni²⁺ occupies octahedral sites. Fe³⁺ (no preference) goes to tetrahedral. Structure: Inverse Spinel — Fe³⁺[Ni²⁺Fe³⁺]O₄.
Q2 [1 mark] — Explain the structure of [CoCl₄]²⁻.
Co²⁺ is a d⁷ ion. Cl⁻ is a weak-field ligand (low in the spectrochemical series).

  • Coordination number = 4, and since Cl⁻ creates a small Δ, tetrahedral geometry is favoured over square planar (no gain in CFSE sufficient to force pairing).
  • Geometry: Tetrahedral. Four Cl⁻ ligands at bond angles of ~109°28'.
  • d⁷ HS tetrahedral config: e⁴ t2³ → 3 unpaired electrons → μ = 3.87 BM (paramagnetic).
  • The complex is intensely blue because tetrahedral d–d transitions are less Laporte-forbidden (no inversion centre) → higher molar absorptivity than octahedral.
Q3 [1 mark] — How can you differentiate between tetrahedral and square-planar Ni²⁺ complexes through magnetic measurement?
Ni²⁺ is d⁸ in both cases. The distinction is simple and direct:

Unpaired e⁻: 2
μ = 2.83 BM
Paramagnetic
SQUARE PLANAR
Config: all paired
Unpaired e⁻: 0
μ = 0 BM
Diamagnetic
Measure the magnetic moment. If μ ≈ 2.83 BM → tetrahedral. If μ = 0 → square planar.
Q4 [1 mark] — NaCl is colourless but FeCl₃ is yellow coloured. Give the reason.
NaCl: Na⁺ is a closed-shell ion (d⁰ or noble-gas configuration) with no d-electrons. There are no d–d transitions or charge-transfer transitions in the visible range → colourless.

FeCl₃: Fe³⁺ is a d⁵ ion. Although d–d transitions are doubly forbidden for d⁵ HS (spin and Laporte), the yellow colour of FeCl₃ is primarily due to Ligand-to-Metal Charge Transfer (LMCT):
  • Cl⁻ has filled p-orbitals that can donate electron density to the partially-filled 3d orbitals of Fe³⁺.
  • This Cl(3p) → Fe(3d) CT transition absorbs in the violet/blue region (≈400–450 nm) → the complementary colour transmitted is yellow.
In short: NaCl has no available d-orbitals for transitions; FeCl₃ has LMCT in the visible range.
Q5 [2 marks] — Give one example of an acidic buffer. Explain how it resists changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid and strong base are added.
Example: CH₃COOH / CH₃COONa (acetic acid / sodium acetate buffer), pH ≈ 4.74 (pKₐ of acetic acid).

The buffer contains a reservoir of both the weak acid (CH₃COOH) and its conjugate base (CH₃COO⁻).

When strong acid (H⁺) is added:
The conjugate base neutralises it:
CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH
The added H⁺ is consumed, [H⁺] barely changes → pH stays nearly constant.

When strong base (OH⁻) is added:
The weak acid neutralises it:
CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O
The added OH⁻ is consumed, [OH⁻] barely changes → pH stays nearly constant.

Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:
pH = pKₐ + log([A⁻]/[HA])
Since both [A⁻] and [HA] are large reservoirs, small additions cause only tiny ratio changes → minimal pH shift.
Q6 [1 mark] — Explain the role of syrupy phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) in the titration of Fe²⁺ with KMnO₄.
H₃PO₄ is added to serve two purposes:

1. Remove interfering colour of Fe³⁺:
The titration of Fe²⁺ with KMnO₄ produces Fe³⁺ as a product. Fe³⁺ is yellow-brown in solution, which would mask the faint pink endpoint colour of excess KMnO₄.
Fe³⁺ + H₃PO₄ → [Fe(HPO₄)]⁺ (colourless complex)
H₃PO₄ forms a colourless phosphate complex with Fe³⁺, removing its colour and making the pink endpoint visible clearly.

2. Prevent oxidation of Cl⁻:
If the solution contains Cl⁻ (e.g., from HCl used for dissolving the sample), KMnO₄ could oxidise Cl⁻ to Cl₂ (a side reaction). Using H₃PO₄/H₂SO₄ instead avoids introducing Cl⁻. H₃PO₄ also maintains the acidic medium required for the reaction:
MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O
Q7 [1 mark] — Why is a small amount of Mg-EDTA complex added during estimation of Ca²⁺ using Eriochrome Black-T (EBT) indicator?
The problem: EBT forms a stable but wine-red complex with Ca²⁺ at the start. However, the Ca²⁺–EBT complex is so stable that EDTA cannot easily displace EBT from Ca²⁺ at the endpoint — the endpoint colour change (wine-red → blue) would be sluggish or not sharp.

The solution — Mg-EDTA displacement trick:
Mg-EDTA + Ca²⁺ → Ca-EDTA + Mg²⁺ (displacement, since Ca-EDTA is more stable)
Mg²⁺ + EBT → Mg-EBT complex (wine red, forms readily)
The added Mg-EDTA liberates Mg²⁺ which binds EBT readily. The Mg²⁺–EBT complex gives a sharp colour. At the endpoint, EDTA easily displaces EBT from Mg²⁺ (weaker complex) → solution turns pure blue sharply.

In short: Mg²⁺ acts as a bridge to give a clean, sharp endpoint because Mg–EBT is less stable than Ca–EDTA but more reactive toward EDTA at the endpoint than Ca–EBT would be.
Q8 [1 mark] — Give an example of a metal ion indicator.
A metal ion indicator (metallochromic indicator) is a compound that forms a coloured complex with metal ions and shows a distinct colour change at the equivalence point in complexometric titrations.

Examples:
  • Eriochrome Black-T (EBT) — most common; wine-red with Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺; turns blue at endpoint. Used in water hardness determination (EDTA titration).
  • Murexide — used for Ca²⁺ alone; pink with Ca²⁺, turns violet/purple at endpoint.
  • Xylenol Orange — used in acidic medium for Pb²⁺, Zn²⁺, Bi³⁺.
The indicator must form a less stable complex with the metal than EDTA does, so EDTA can displace it at the endpoint.

EDTA Complexometric Titration

Complexometric titration uses complex-ion formation between a metal ion (analyte) and a ligand (titrant). EDTA (H₄Y, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) is the standard titrant — a hexadentate ligand that forms extremely stable 1:1 chelate complexes with almost every metal ion.

EDTA Structure & Charge

H₄Y ⇌ H₂Y²⁻ + 2H⁺ (in solution)

EDTA has 6 donor atoms: 2 nitrogen + 4 carboxylate oxygens. The fully deprotonated form Y⁴⁻ is the active chelating species.

Mn+ + H₂Y²⁻ ⇌ [MY](n−4)+ + 2H⁺

Overall charge on complex = (n − 4)+
where n = metal oxidation state

Chelated complexes are far more stable than non-chelated ones (chelate effect — large ΔS of formation due to release of water molecules and entropy gain).

📌 End Point vs Equivalence Point
Equivalence point: the theoretical point where moles of titrant = moles of analyte (stoichiometric). End point: the experimentally observed point (colour change of indicator). End point is slightly beyond equivalence point — indicator needs a small excess to change colour.
Type 1 — Direct Titration

Most common; metal titrated directly with EDTA

  • Solution buffered to correct pH (e.g. pH 10 with NH₄⁺/NH₄OH buffer).
  • Metal ion + indicator → coloured complex (wine-red).
  • Titrate with standard EDTA until indicator is displaced → colour changes to free indicator colour (blue for EBT).
  • Auxiliary complexing agents (e.g. triethanolamine) may be added to prevent metal hydroxide precipitation at high pH.
Type 2 — Back Titration

Used when direct titration is not feasible

Used when: (a) metal precipitates at titration pH, (b) metal–indicator complex too stable to give sharp endpoint, (c) reaction with EDTA is too slow.

  • Add known excess standard EDTA to metal solution.
  • Buffer to desired pH.
  • Back-titrate excess EDTA with standard Zn²⁺ or Mg²⁺ solution using EBT indicator.
  • Metal consumed = total EDTA added − excess EDTA back-titrated.
n(M) = n(EDTA added) − n(EDTA back-titrated)
Type 3 — Replacement / Substitution

For metals that react poorly with metal ion indicators

The metal Mn+ is reacted with Mg-EDTA (or Zn-EDTA):

Mn+ + Mg-EDTA → [M-EDTA](n−4)+ + Mg²⁺

Condition: Mn+–EDTA stability > Mg²⁺–EDTA stability. The released Mg²⁺ is titrated with standard EDTA using EBT indicator. Mg²⁺ released ≡ amount of Mn+ originally present.

Example: Ca²⁺ determination — Ca²⁺ reacts poorly with EBT, so Mg-EDTA is used to generate titratable Mg²⁺.

Type 4 — Alkalimetric / Acid-Liberation

EDTA releases H⁺ on complexation → titrate with NaOH

Mn+ + H₂Y²⁻ ⇌ [MY](n−4)+ + 2H⁺

The 2 H⁺ ions released per metal ion are titrated with standard NaOH. The solution must be carefully neutralised before the experiment so background H⁺ doesn't interfere. This method does not need a metal-ion indicator.

Metal Ion Indicators — Properties Required

Four essential conditions for a good metallochromic indicator

  1. Solution must be strongly coloured when all metal ions form complex with indicator.
  2. Colour reaction must be specific or at least selective for the metal ion.
  3. Metal-indicator complex must have a much stable colour but less stable than the metal-EDTA complex, so EDTA can displace indicator at endpoint.
  4. Colour contrast between free indicator and metal-indicator complex must be sharp and visible.

Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ Mixture Analysis — Full Procedure

Separating Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in a mixture

Step 1: Titrate whole solution (Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) at pH 10 with EDTA/EBT → total volume V₁
Step 2: Adjust pH to 12.3 with NaOH → Mg(OH)₂ precipitates, only Ca²⁺ remains in solution
Step 3: Titrate Ca²⁺ only using Calcon indicator at pH 12.3 → volume V₂

∴ Ca²⁺ ≡ V₂ | Mg²⁺ ≡ (V₁ − V₂)
PRACTICE QUESTIONS — EDTA TITRATION
Q1. Why can't Ca²⁺ be determined directly using EBT as indicator?
Two reasons:
  • Ca²⁺–EBT complex is too stable: The Ca–EBT complex, once formed, is not easily displaced by EDTA at the endpoint → sluggish, indistinct endpoint.
  • EBT works best at pH 7–11: At this pH, Ca²⁺ alone doesn't react sharply with EBT (reaction is slow and endpoint is poor).
Solution: add a small amount of Mg-EDTA. Ca²⁺ displaces Mg²⁺ from Mg-EDTA (since Ca-EDTA is more stable). The released Mg²⁺ binds EBT readily, giving a sharp wine-red colour. At the endpoint, excess EDTA displaces EBT from Mg²⁺ easily → sharp blue endpoint.
Q2. In a back titration for Al³⁺, 50 mL of 0.01 M EDTA is added to the Al³⁺ solution. The excess EDTA required 10 mL of 0.01 M Zn²⁺ to back-titrate. How many mmol of Al³⁺ were present?
mmol EDTA added = 50 × 0.01 = 0.50 mmol
mmol EDTA back-titrated (excess) = 10 × 0.01 = 0.10 mmol
mmol Al³⁺ = 0.50 − 0.10 = 0.40 mmol

Since EDTA reacts 1:1 with Al³⁺, 0.40 mmol of Al³⁺ was present.
Q3. Why is an auxiliary complexing agent (e.g. tartrate) sometimes added in direct EDTA titration?
At the pH required for the titration (e.g. pH 10 for Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺), many metal ions can form insoluble hydroxides — for example Pb(OH)₂ or Al(OH)₃ — which precipitate out of solution and are unavailable for titration.

An auxiliary complexing agent like tartrate or citrate forms a weak, soluble complex with the metal ion, keeping it in solution at high pH without competing with EDTA at the endpoint (since the auxiliary complex is much weaker than M-EDTA). This ensures an accurate titration.
Q4. Why is Calcon used at pH 12.3 for Ca²⁺ titration, and what happens to Mg²⁺ at this pH?
Calcon (Eriochrome Dark Blue) has pKₐ values of 7.4 and 13.5. At pH 12.3 (NaOH medium), Calcon is in the HD⁻ form and forms a stable pink complex with Ca²⁺.

At pH 12.3, Mg²⁺ is precipitated as Mg(OH)₂ (white precipitate), completely removing it from solution. Only Ca²⁺ remains to be titrated. The colour change at the endpoint is pink → pure blue, which is sharp and distinct.

This is why Calcon is used for selective determination of Ca²⁺ in the presence of Mg²⁺.

Redox Titration — KMnO₄ & K₂Cr₂O₇

Redox titrations involve electron transfer between the analyte and titrant. The two main oxidising titrants are KMnO₄ (self-indicating, acidic medium) and K₂Cr₂O₇ (requires external indicator like BDS).

KMnO₄ — Permanganate Titration

Self-indicator; acidic medium (H₂SO₄)

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
E° = +1.51 V (very strong oxidiser)

Nernst: E = 1.51 + (0.059/5) log([MnO₄⁻][H⁺]⁸/[Mn²⁺])
  • KMnO₄ is deep purple; Mn²⁺ is nearly colourless.
  • First drop excess → permanent faint pink = endpoint.
  • H₂SO₄ used (not HCl — KMnO₄ would oxidise Cl⁻).
  • High [H⁺] increases formal potential → stronger oxidiser.
K₂Cr₂O₇ — Dichromate Titration

Needs redox indicator (BDS); acidic medium

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O
E° = +1.33 V
  • Cr₂O₇²⁻ is orange; Cr³⁺ is green → colour change at endpoint is masked.
  • Needs external redox indicator: BDS (Barium diphenylamine sulfonate).
  • H₃PO₄ + H₂SO₄ added; H₃PO₄ complexes Fe³⁺ → colourless + lowers E°(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) → sharper endpoint.
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ is a primary standard (stable, accurate weighing); KMnO₄ is not.
BDS Indicator — Mechanism

How BDS works in K₂Cr₂O₇ titration of Fe²⁺

BDS is a redox indicator. In its reduced (colourless) form, it does not react initially with Fe²⁺ because BDS has a lower E° — it waits. The reaction sequence is:

Before endpoint: Fe²⁺ + Cr₂O₇²⁻ → Fe³⁺ + Cr³⁺ (solution stays green from Cr³⁺)
At endpoint: all Fe²⁺ consumed. Cr₂O₇²⁻ now oxidises BDS
BDS (colourless) → BDS-oxidised (violet/purple) → ENDPOINT

Indicator needs E° higher than the reductant (Fe²⁺) but lower than the titrant (Cr₂O₇²⁻).

Also: H₃PO₄ is added to complex yellow Fe³⁺ → [Fe(HPO₄)]⁺ (colourless), which removes interfering colour before the BDS endpoint appears.

Cu²⁺ Determination — Iodometric Method

Why direct EDTA titration of Cu²⁺ is avoided; iodometry is used instead

Cu²⁺ forms a stable complex with Fast Sulphon Black F indicator, but Ni²⁺ reaction is poor. More importantly, direct EDTA titration of Cu²⁺ is slow. Instead:

CuSO₄ + excess KI → Cu₂I₂↓ (white) + I₂
I₂ + excess KI → I₃⁻ (keeps I₂ in solution)
I₂ then titrated with standard Na₂S₂O₃

S₂O₃²⁻ + I₂ → S₄O₆²⁻ + I⁻ [Indicator: Starch — blue → colourless]

This is iodometry (I₂ generated in the reaction, then used). Direct use of I₂ = iodimetry. Note: CuI precipitate makes solution cloudy (whitish) — no clear endpoint visible without starch.

Why does Cu²⁺ react with I⁻ even though E°(Cu²⁺/Cu⁺) < E°(I₂/I⁻)?
Normally Cu²⁺ (E° = 0.15 V) cannot oxidise I⁻ since I₂/I⁻ has E° = 0.54 V. But CuI precipitates (Ksp = 10⁻¹²), drastically lowering [Cu⁺]. From Nernst: formal potential of Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ system increases to ~0.85 V, exceeding E°(I₂/I⁻). This makes the reaction thermodynamically feasible.
Mn²⁺ Estimation (Back Titration with KMnO₄)

Why Mn²⁺ cannot be directly titrated with KMnO₄

KMnO₄ + Mn²⁺ → MnO₂ (brown ppt) forms in a redox comproportionation reaction. This is not a clean endpoint. Instead:

Step 1: Mn²⁺ + NaBiO₃ + HNO₃ → MnO₄⁻ (purple/pink) + Bi³⁺
[This detects/colours Mn²⁺, colour = defect of Mn²⁺]
Step 2: Add measured excess Mohr's salt (FeSO₄) solution → say 50 mL
[Fe²⁺ reduces MnO₄⁻ back]
Step 3: Titrate excess Fe²⁺ with KMnO₄ → let volume = V₁
Step 4: Blank — 50 mL Mohr's salt titrated separately with KMnO₄ → V₂

Volume of KMnO₄ for Mn²⁺ = (V₂ − V₁)
PRACTICE QUESTIONS — REDOX TITRATION
Q1. Why is HCl not used to acidify KMnO₄ titrations?
KMnO₄ is a powerful oxidising agent (E° = +1.51 V). In HCl medium, it would oxidise Cl⁻ to Cl₂:
2KMnO₄ + 16HCl → 2KCl + 2MnCl₂ + 5Cl₂ + 8H₂O
This consumes extra KMnO₄ (not just for the analyte) → positive error in titration. Therefore, dilute H₂SO₄ is used instead (SO₄²⁻ is not oxidised by KMnO₄). H₃PO₄ can also be used safely.
Q2. Why is K₂Cr₂O₇ preferred over KMnO₄ as a primary standard?
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ can be obtained in high purity, dried, and weighed accurately — it is a true primary standard.
  • KMnO₄ is not a primary standard: it reacts with trace organic matter in water, its solutions decompose slowly (MnO₂ precipitates), and it must be standardised before use.
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ solutions are stable indefinitely; KMnO₄ solutions change over time.
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ can be used with HCl (dichromate does not oxidise Cl⁻ under normal conditions since E°(Cr₂O₇²⁻) < E°(Cl₂/Cl⁻)).
Q3. In an iodometric titration of Cu²⁺: 25 mL of CuSO₄ solution reacts with excess KI. The I₂ produced required 18.4 mL of 0.1 M Na₂S₂O₃. Find molarity of CuSO₄.
Reactions:
2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI↓ + I₂     (1 mol I₂ per 2 mol Cu²⁺)
I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻     (1 mol I₂ per 2 mol thiosulfate)
mmol S₂O₃²⁻ = 18.4 × 0.1 = 1.84 mmol
mmol I₂ = 1.84/2 = 0.92 mmol
mmol Cu²⁺ = 2 × 0.92 = 1.84 mmol
Molarity CuSO₄ = 1.84/25 = 0.0736 M

pH Calculations — Acids, Bases & Salts
Strong Acid / Strong Base

Direct from concentration — but careful at very low concentrations!

[H⁺] = Ca (for strong acid) if Ca ≫ 10⁻⁷
pH = −log(Ca)

For very dilute strong acid (C ≤ 10⁻⁶ M) — cannot ignore water contribution:
[H⁺] = (C + √(C² + 4Kw)) / 2    [Kw = 10⁻¹⁴]

Example: 10⁻⁸ N HCl → [H⁺] ≈ 10⁻⁶·⁷⁹ (not 10⁻⁸ — water H⁺ dominates!)
Weak Acid (HA)

Derive from equilibrium — degree of dissociation α

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻
At eq: (1−α)C      αC      αC

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] = (αC·αC)/((1−α)C) = α²C/(1−α)

Since α ≪ 1:   Ka ≈ α²C
∴ α = √(Ka/C)
[H⁺] = αC = √(Ka·C)

pH = ½pKa − ½log(C)    [or: pH = ½(pKa − log C)]

Valid when α ≪ 1, i.e. Ka ≪ C (dilution doesn't cause full dissociation). Always verify.

Weak Base (B)
pOH = ½pKb − ½log(Cb)
pH = 14 − pOH     [since pH + pOH = 14 at 25°C]

Or: pH = 14 − ½pKb + ½log(Cb)
Salt of Weak Acid + Strong Base (e.g. CH₃COONa)

Hydrolysis — basic solution

CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻   (hydrolysis)

Kh = Kw/Ka
[OH⁻] = √(Kw·C/Ka)
pOH = ½(pKw − pKa − log C) = ½(14 − pKa − log C)
pH = 14 − pOH = 7 + ½pKa + ½log C
Salt of Strong Acid + Weak Base (e.g. NH₄Cl)

Hydrolysis — acidic solution

NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺

[H⁺] = √(Kw·C/Kb)
pH = 7 − ½pKb + ½log C     (note: acidic → pH < 7)

PRACTICE QUESTIONS — pH CALCULATIONS
Q1. Find pH of 100 mL 0.1N acetic acid (pKa = 4.76) + 25 mL 0.1N NaOH mixed together.
This is a buffer problem (partial neutralisation of weak acid).

mmol CH₃COOH initially = 100 × 0.1 = 10 mmol
mmol NaOH added = 25 × 0.1 = 2.5 mmol
NaOH neutralises CH₃COOH: 2.5 mmol acid consumed → 2.5 mmol CH₃COONa formed

After reaction: CH₃COOH = 10 − 2.5 = 7.5 mmol | CH₃COO⁻ = 2.5 mmol
Total volume = 125 mL
[Acid] = 7.5/125 = 0.06 N | [Salt] = 2.5/125 = 0.02 N

Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.76 + log(0.02/0.06) = 4.76 + log(1/3) = 4.76 − 0.477 = 3.66
(This matches TKM Sir's example directly!)
Q2. What is the pH of 10⁻⁸ N HCl? (Don't just say 8!)
At this ultra-low concentration, HCl contributes [H⁺] = 10⁻⁸ M, but water autoionisation contributes ~10⁻⁷ M. We cannot ignore water.

Use the exact formula:
[H⁺] = (C + √(C² + 4Kw)) / 2
= (10⁻⁸ + √(10⁻¹⁶ + 4×10⁻¹⁴)) / 2
= (10⁻⁸ + √(4.0001×10⁻¹⁴)) / 2
≈ (10⁻⁸ + 2.00002×10⁻⁷) / 2
≈ (10⁻⁸ + 2×10⁻⁷) / 2 ≈ 1.05×10⁻⁷ M
pH = −log(1.05×10⁻⁷) ≈ 6.98 (slightly acidic, not 8!)
Q3. Calculate pH of 0.01 M acetic acid (Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵).
[H⁺] = √(Ka × C) = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.01) = √(1.8×10⁻⁷) = 4.24×10⁻⁴ M
pH = −log(4.24×10⁻⁴) = 3.37

Check α: α = √(Ka/C) = √(1.8×10⁻⁵/0.01) = √(1.8×10⁻³) ≈ 0.042 = 4.2% ≪ 1 ✓ (approximation valid)
Q4. 0.1 N NaOH (10 mL) + 0.1 N CH₃COOH (25 mL) mixed. Find pH. (pKa = 4.76)
mmol NaOH = 1 | mmol CH₃COOH = 2.5
NaOH completely consumed, neutralising 1 mmol of acid:
Remaining: CH₃COOH = 1.5 mmol | CH₃COO⁻ = 1 mmol | Total volume = 35 mL

[HA] = 1.5/35 | [A⁻] = 1/35
pH = 4.76 + log(1/1.5) = 4.76 − 0.176 = 4.58
Q5. Find pH of 0.1 M NH₄Cl (pKb of NH₃ = 4.74).
NH₄Cl is a salt of strong acid (HCl) and weak base (NH₃) → acidic hydrolysis.
Formula: pH = 7 − ½pKb + ½log C
= 7 − ½(4.74) + ½log(0.1)
= 7 − 2.37 + ½(−1)
= 7 − 2.37 − 0.5 = 4.13

Buffer Solutions — Theory, Capacity & Indicators

A buffer solution resists changes in pH when small amounts of strong acid or base are added. It consists of a weak acid + its conjugate base (acidic buffer) or a weak base + its conjugate acid (basic buffer).

Acidic Buffer

Weak acid + conjugate base salt

Example: CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])
     = pKa + log([salt]/[acid])

Made by: partially neutralising weak acid with strong base (NaOH), or dissolving weak acid + its sodium salt.

Basic Buffer

Weak base + conjugate acid salt

Example: NH₃ + NH₄Cl

pOH = pKb + log([BH⁺]/[B])
        = pKb + log([salt]/[base])
pH = 14 − pOH

Used when pH > 7 is required (e.g. pH 10 for EDTA titrations of Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺).

How a Buffer Works — Mechanism

CH₃COOH / CH₃COONa system

In solution: CH₃COOH (partial dissociation) + CH₃COO⁻ (from complete dissociation of salt) + Na⁺ + H⁺ all present.

ADD STRONG ACID (H⁺)
CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH

Conjugate base reservoir consumes H⁺. [CH₃COOH] increases slightly, [CH₃COO⁻] decreases slightly. Ratio changes minimally → pH barely changes.

ADD STRONG BASE (OH⁻)
CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O

Weak acid reservoir neutralises OH⁻. [CH₃COOH] decreases slightly, [CH₃COO⁻] increases slightly. Ratio changes minimally → pH barely changes.

Buffer Capacity (β)

A measure of how well a buffer resists pH change

β = moles of strong acid (or base) needed to change pH by 1 unit

Buffer capacity is MAXIMUM when [salt] = [acid] i.e. pH = pKa
(for basic: when [salt] = [base] i.e. pOH = pKb)

Higher total buffer concentration → higher capacity

Buffer is most effective within ±1 pH unit of pKa: pKa − 1 ≤ pH ≤ pKa + 1. Outside this range, the buffer becomes ineffective.

Acid-Base Indicators

HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (weak acid indicator)

The undissociated form HIn and dissociated form In⁻ have different colours. The ratio determines which colour is seen:

pH = pKIn + log([In⁻]/[HIn])

When [In⁻] = 10×[HIn]: log = +1 → colour of In⁻ seen → pH = pKIn + 1
When [HIn] = 10×[In⁻]: log = −1 → colour of HIn seen → pH = pKIn − 1

∴ Indicator transition range: pKIn ± 1

For sharp endpoint: choose indicator whose pKIn ≈ pH at equivalence point of the titration.

PRACTICE QUESTIONS — BUFFERS & INDICATORS
Q1. Why is buffer capacity maximum when pH = pKa?
At pH = pKa, [salt] = [acid] (Henderson–Hasselbalch gives log(1) = 0). At this composition:
  • Adding acid: maximum [A⁻] available to neutralise it (ratio shifts from 1:1 to something smaller — log changes by only a small amount).
  • Adding base: maximum [HA] available to neutralise it.
Mathematically, the derivative d(moles base)/d(pH) is maximised when [HA] = [A⁻]. Away from this point, one component is depleted and the other dominates — the buffer becomes less effective. So the buffer works best symmetrically around pH = pKa.
Q2. Why is phenolphthalein used for NaOH + HCl titrations but methyl orange for NaOH + acetic acid? (Or is it the other way?)
Strong acid + Strong base (HCl + NaOH): equivalence point is at pH 7. Both phenolphthalein (range 8.2–10) and methyl orange (range 3.1–4.4) work here because the pH jumps steeply from ~4 to ~10 around the equivalence point — both indicator transitions fall within this steep jump. Either can be used.

Strong base + Weak acid (NaOH + CH₃COOH): equivalence point is at pH ~9 (basic, salt hydrolysis). Use phenolphthalein (range 8.2–10) — its colour change occurs right at the equivalence point. Methyl orange would change colour long before the equivalence point (at pH ~4) and give a false endpoint.

Weak base + Strong acid (NH₃ + HCl): equivalence point at pH ~5. Use methyl red or methyl orange.
Q3. A buffer is prepared by mixing 200 mL of 0.2 M CH₃COOH with 100 mL of 0.2 M NaOH. Find its pH. (pKa = 4.76)
mmol CH₃COOH = 200 × 0.2 = 40 mmol
mmol NaOH = 100 × 0.2 = 20 mmol
NaOH reacts: 20 mmol acid neutralised → 20 mmol CH₃COO⁻ formed
Remaining: [HA] = 20 mmol, [A⁻] = 20 mmol, total volume = 300 mL

Since [HA] = [A⁻], log([A⁻]/[HA]) = log(1) = 0
pH = 4.76 + 0 = 4.76

This is exactly at the pKa — maximum buffer capacity point.
Q4. What is the transition range of an indicator with pKIn = 6.2? In what type of titration would you use it?
Transition range = pKIn ± 1 = 5.2 to 7.2

This is close to neutral — suitable for titrations with equivalence point near pH 6–7. For example: weak acid + weak base titrations where the equivalence point falls near neutral, or litmus-like indicators in certain acid-base combinations. However, weak acid + weak base titrations generally give a poor endpoint; this indicator would be more useful for titrations of intermediate-strength acids or bases where the equivalence point falls in this range.
