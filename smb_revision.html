01
H-atom Interacting with EM Radiation
Semi-classical treatment overview
The Setup

The semi-classical treatment treats the atom quantum mechanically but the EM field classically. The fully quantum treatment is called the Maxwell H-atom QM approach.

  • Atom has discrete energy levels (1s, 2s, …)
  • Moves lower → upper = absorption (absorbs photon)
  • Bound state energies are negative
  • Stationary state = probability is time-independent (energy perfectly defined)
Why does e⁻ jump from 2p → lower?

Statistical / transition lifetime argument. It's not a single definite time — it's a statistical lifetime.

Why spontaneous emission?

QM cannot explain it (though it can solve it mathematically). QFT (2nd quantisation) properly explains spontaneous emission.

QM vs QFT — Key Distinction
QM (1st quantisation)QFT (2nd quantisation)
Atom structure quantised (energy levels)EM field also quantised
Cannot explain spontaneous emission from first principlesExplains spontaneous emission via vacuum fluctuations
🧠 Mnemonic
QM = "One thing quantised (atom)"
QFT = "Two things quantised (atom + field)"
1st quantisation → atom structure. 2nd quantisation → field itself. QFT fixes what QM can't: spontaneous emission.
02
EM Field & Vector Potential
Fields, gauges, and the form of Ā
Fields in terms of potentials
ELECTRIC FIELD
E(r,t) = −∇φ(r,t) − ∂A/∂t (r,t)
MAGNETIC FIELD
B(r,t) = ∇ × A(r,t) ← A is the vector potential
Form of the Vector Potential A
A(ω,r,t) = A₀(ω) [e^{i(k·r − ωt + δω)} + e^{−i(k·r − ωt + δω)}] ê
  • ê = direction of A = direction of polarisation
  • Δω = range (bandwidth) — different ω not in same phase
  • A₀(ω) has a peak at ω = ω₀ (nearly monochromatic)
ℹ For simplest case: monochromatic wave with fixed ω. In general, φ = 0 and A(r,t) is superposition of plane waves.
GENERAL PULSE (linear polarisation, Δω bandwidth)
A(r,t) = ∫ ê A₀(ω) [e^{i(k·r−ωt+δω)} + e^{−i(k·r−ωt+δω)}] dω

Nearly monochromatic → not strictly so, because of uncertainty principle.

Gauge Transformation (Coulomb Gauge)

We can add an arbitrary scalar χ to the potential and take curl(χ) = 0, so:

B' = ∇ × A' = ∇ × A + ∇ × (∇χ) = B (gauge invariance)

We choose χ such that ∇·A = 0 → this is the Coulomb gauge (also called radiation gauge).

🧠 Remember
"E comes from BOTH φ and A (time-deriv)"
"B comes ONLY from A (curl)"
E has two contributions; B has only one. Coulomb gauge: ∇·A = 0.
03
Hamiltonian of H-atom in EM Field
TDSE → perturbation split
Hamiltonian — Charged Particle in EM field (spin ignored)
⚠"Spin not considered" does NOT mean spin = 0. It means the Schrödinger equation does not consider spin.
H = (1/2m)(p̂ − qA)² + eφ

For electron: q = −e, so:

H = (1/2m)(iℏ∇ + eA)² − eφ
Under Coulomb Gauge + Weak Field Approximation

∇·A = 0 (Coulomb gauge), and we neglect the A² term (weak field approx).

The TDSE becomes:

−(ℏ²/2m)∇²ψ − (Ze²/4πε₀r)ψ − (eℏe/m) A·∇ψ = iℏ ∂ψ/∂t

We split this as: H = H₀ + H'

H₀ (unperturbed)H' (perturbation)
Unperturbed H-atom Hamiltonian
(kinetic + Coulomb attraction)		 H' = −(eℏ/m) A·∇ = (e/m) A·p̂
(interaction with EM field)
★H' is the perturbation Hamiltonian — this drives transitions between states. We use Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory (TDPT).
🧠 Mnemonic — The Split
H = H₀ + H'
"Hydrogen Zero = old atom, H-prime = EM hits it"
H₀ is what you already know (H-atom). H' is the NEW part from EM radiation. Only H' causes transitions.
04
Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory
Transition probability & absorption/emission conditions
Expanding the perturbed wavefunction

Unperturbed eigenstates: φₖ(r) e^{−iEₖt/ℏ} with H₀φₖ = Eₖφₖ

We expand the total wavefunction as:

ψ(r,t) = Σₖ Cₖ(t) φₖ(r) e^{−iEₖt/ℏ}

The Cₖ(t) are to be determined by first-order perturbation.

Initial Condition

At t = 0, electron is in state ψₐ:

Cₖ(t=0) = δₖₐ → Cₐ(0) = 1, all others = 0
First-Order Perturbation: C_b(t)

After perturbation, probability of finding system in state ψ_b = |C_b(t)|²

C_b'(t) = (1/iℏ) ∫₀ᵗ H'_ba(t') e^{iω_ba t'} dt'
WHERE: ω_ba = (E_b − E_a)/ℏ
C_b'(t) = −(ie/2ℏm) ∫₀ᵗ ⟨ψ_b|A·∇|ψₐ⟩ e^{iω_ba t'} dt'
When Do Integrals Contribute? → Energy Conservation

After substituting A and integrating over ω and t':

Integral TermConditionMeaning
∫e^{i(ω_ba − ω)t'} dt' ω_ba = ω → E_b = Eₐ + ℏω ABSORPTION
∫e^{i(ω_ba + ω)t'} dt' ω_ba = −ω → E_b = Eₐ − ℏω EMISSION
ℹThis is the Principle of Detailed Balance — transitions happen only when energy is exactly conserved (within Δω bandwidth).
🧠 Mnemonic — Absorption vs Emission
ABS-orption: E_b = Eₐ + ℏω (goes UP, gains energy)
EMI-ssion: E_b = Eₐ − ℏω (comes DOWN, loses energy)
+ℏω = absorbed a photon, went higher. −ℏω = emitted a photon, came down.
05
The Matrix Element M_ba & Dipole Approximation
Why e^{ik·r} ≈ 1
The Matrix Element M_ba
M_ba = ⟨ψ_b | e^{ik·r} ê·∇ | ψₐ⟩

We expand the exponential:

e^{ik·r} = 1 + i(k·r) + i²(k·r)²/2! + ···
Why can we set e^{ik·r} ≈ 1?

Check the magnitude of k·r:

  • For Bohr radius: r ~ ½ Å = 10⁻⁸ cm
  • For visible light: k = 2π/λ ~ 10⁵ cm⁻¹
  • So k·r ~ 10⁻³ ≪ 1
★Since k·r ≪ 1, we can approximate e^{ik·r} ≈ 1. This is the Electric Dipole Approximation.
Result Under Dipole Approximation
M_ba = −(mω_ba/ℏ) eê·⟨ψ_b|r|ψₐ⟩

The term ⟨ψ_b|r|ψₐ⟩ is the position matrix element — this is what determines if a transition is allowed or forbidden.

Higher Approximations (expanding the series)

If we keep more terms in the expansion of e^{ik·r}, we get:

  • 1st term → Electric Dipole (E1) approximation
  • 2nd term → Magnetic Dipole (M1) + Electric Quadrupole (E2)
  • 3rd term → Quadrupole approximation, etc.

Note: The ⟨ψ_b|A|ψₐ⟩ term is ALWAYS present for all orders; it's the dominant one.

🧠 Key Numbers to Remember
r ~ 10⁻⁸ cm (Bohr radius ~ ½ Å)
k ~ 10⁵ cm⁻¹ (visible light)
k·r ~ 10⁻³ ≪ 1 → DIPOLE APPROX VALID

Selection Rules for Dipole Transitions
Δl = ±1 and Δm rules
Setup

For H-atom: ψₐ ~ R_{n₁l₁} Y_{l₁}^{m₁}(θ,φ) and ψ_b ~ R_{n₂l₂} Y_{l₂}^{m₂}(θ,φ)

The matrix element ⟨ψ_b|r|ψₐ⟩ is non-zero only for certain quantum number combinations.

Angular Momentum Selection Rule
Δl = l₂ − l₁ = ±1

This comes from the angular integral ⟨ψ_b|r|ψₐ⟩ being non-zero only when Δl = ±1.

Magnetic Quantum Number Selection Rules

Depends on ê (polarisation vector direction):

CASE (i): ê ∥ z-axis
Δm = m₂ − m₁ = 0

π-lines (linear polarisation along z)

CASE (ii): ê ⊥ z-axis, k̂ ∥ z
Δm = m₂ − m₁ = ±1

σ-lines (circular polarisation)

⚠If there is no pulse, there is no interaction. We consider there is some pulse in the background which contributes to the field.
Transition Diagram (from notes)
n=3, l=2 (3d): m = 2, 1, 0, −1, −2
m=2m=1m=0m=−1m=2
↓ transitions ↓
n=2, l=1 (2p)
↓ transitions ↓
n=1, l=0 (1s)
■ Δm=0 → π-lines (red lines in notes) ■ Δm=±1 → σ-lines (diagonal lines)
🧠 Selection Rules Summary
DIPOLE: Δl = ±1 (always)
π-lines: Δm = 0 (ê ∥ z)
σ-lines: Δm = ±1 (ê ⊥ z)
"Parallel polarisation → Pi (π) → Δm = 0. Perpendicular → Sigma (σ) → Δm = ±1"

Many-Electron Atoms
Central field approximation & effective potential
The Full Hamiltonian (after CM reduction)
H = Σᵢ [−(1/2)∇ᵢ² − Z/rᵢ] + Σᵢ>ⱼ (e²/4πε₀ rᵢⱼ)
1e PART

Kinetic energy + nuclear attraction → Central potential

2e PART (pairs)

Electron-electron repulsion → Non-central potential

⚠The non-central repulsion term makes the Schrödinger equation analytically unsolvable for many-electron atoms.
The Central Field Approximation

Based on the independent particle model — each electron behaves as if in H-atom with an effective potential:

V_eff(rᵢ) = −Z/rᵢ + S(rᵢ) ← S(rᵢ) = screening function

A single form is not enough → indistinguishable particles require antisymmetry.

Behaviour of V_eff(r)
RegionBehaviourReason
r → 0 (near nucleus)V(r) ~ −Z/rElectron sees full nuclear charge
r → ∞ (far from nucleus)V(r) ~ −Z−(N−1)/r = −1/rScreened by (N−1) other electrons; N = number of electrons
r intermediateComplicated/difficultPartial screening
Central Field Hamiltonian Split
H = Hc + Hn
Hc = Σᵢ [−(1/2)∇ᵢ² − Z/rᵢ + S(rᵢ)] (sum of H-like parts)

Hn = remaining part of repulsion potential (not accounted for by Hc)

Solution: Product Wavefunction (Hartree)

For Hc, we use separation of variables. The complete solution:

Ψc(r₁,r₂,...,rN) = uc₁(r₁) uc₂(r₂) ... ucN(rN) — (Eq. 2)
Ec = Ec₁ + Ec₂ + ··· + EcN

Names: Hartree, Fock, Slater — key people associated with this approach.

★This product wavefunction (Eq. 2) does NOT satisfy Pauli Exclusion Principle — it must be anti-symmetrised!
🧠 Mnemonic — Central Field Key Points
"Independent particles → Product wavefn → Anti-symmetrise → Slater Det"
The chain: independent particle model → product state → but must be antisymmetric → use Slater determinant.
08
Pauli Exclusion Principle & Slater Determinant
Anti-symmetrisation via permutation operator
Why Anti-symmetrize?

According to PEP, the complete wave function of a many-electron system must be anti-symmetric. But the product wavefunction (Eq. 2) is NOT anti-symmetric.

We anti-symmetrize using a permutation operator — forming a determinantal wave function.

The Slater Determinant (with spin)

Each single-particle function includes spin: φ_ci(qᵢ) = ucᵢ(rᵢ) χᵢ where χᵢ is the spin part.

SLATER DETERMINANT (N×N)
ΨA(q₁,...,qN) = (1/√N!) |φc₁(q₁) φc₁(q₂) ... φc₁(qN)| |φc₂(q₁) φc₂(q₂) ... φc₂(qN)| | ... | |φcN(q₁) φcN(q₂) ... φcN(qN)|

For 3 electrons, there are 3! = 6 permutations. Normalization factor: 1/√N!

Properties of Slater Determinant (3 key properties)
  • 1
    Row exchange → sign change
    If qᵢ ⇌ qⱼ (exchange two particle coordinates), the SD becomes negative. This establishes anti-symmetry. ✓
  • 2
    Two identical rows → ΨA = 0
    If two quantum number sets (Ci, Cj) are the same → SD = 0. This IS the Pauli Exclusion Principle: no two electrons can have identical quantum numbers.
  • 3
    Two identical columns → ΨA = 0
    If rᵢ = rⱼ (two electrons at the same position): SD = 0. Two electrons cannot be at the same place — Coulomb repulsion. (qᵢ = qⱼ)
🧠 Mnemonic — 3 Properties of SD
S.A.C. — "Swap, Anti, Collapse"
1. SWAP rows → sign flips (antisymmetry)
2. SAME rows → zero (Pauli: same q-numbers forbidden)
3. SAME cols → zero (Coulomb: same position forbidden)

Hartree-Fock & Self-Consistent Field
Variational method + iterative solution
The SCF Procedure
  • 1
    Start with a trial potential
    Begin with a trial expression for the potential energy.
  • 2
    Variational method → solve SE
    Using variational method, find the wavefunction ψ.
  • 3
    Get charge distribution
    From ψ, obtain the charge distribution using electrostatics.
  • 4
    New potential → feed back into SE
    From charge distribution, determine the form of potential. Feed this back into SE iteratively.
  • 5
    Repeat until convergence
    Repeat steps 2–4 until solution of desired accuracy is achieved. This is the self-consistent field.
Hartree-Fock Approach

The H-F method adopts SCF to solve a system of integro-differential equations to find the many-electron wave function.

★ Limitation of HF: The Hartree-Fock method does NOT account for electron correlation.
ΔE_correlation = E_exact − E_HF [Non-relativistic]
This difference is measured experimentally: ΔE → E_expt
🧠 SCF Mnemonic — "TVCP Loop"
Trial → Variational → Charge → Potential → (loop back)
T: trial potential. V: variational solve. C: charge distribution. P: new potential. Then loop. HF's big weakness: misses correlation energy.

Q1. Why can't QM explain spontaneous emission? What can?+
QM can solve it mathematically but cannot explain it from first principles because it only quantises the atom (1st quantisation). QFT (2nd quantisation) explains spontaneous emission properly — it quantises the EM field itself, revealing vacuum fluctuations that drive emission.
Q2. Write the two Maxwell equations for E and B in terms of potentials φ and A.+
E(r,t) = −∇φ(r,t) − ∂A/∂t(r,t)
B(r,t) = ∇ × A(r,t)
Q3. Write H = H₀ + H'. What is H'?+
H' = −(eℏ/m) A·∇ = (e/m) A·p̂
It is the perturbation Hamiltonian — the interaction of the H-atom with the EM field. H₀ is the unperturbed H-atom Hamiltonian.
Q4. What condition must hold for absorption? For emission?+
Absorption: ω_ba = ω → E_b = Eₐ + ℏω (upper state gains ℏω)
Emission: ω_ba = −ω → E_b = Eₐ − ℏω (releases ℏω)
This comes from the Principle of Detailed Balance.
Q5. Justify the dipole approximation using numbers.+
For visible light: k = 2π/λ ~ 10⁵ cm⁻¹. Bohr radius r ~ 10⁻⁸ cm.
So k·r ~ 10⁻³ ≪ 1.
Therefore e^{ik·r} ≈ 1 + i(k·r) + ... ≈ 1. This is the electric dipole approximation.
Q6. State the selection rules for electric dipole transitions.+
Δl = ±1 (always, from angular integral)
Δm = 0 → π-lines (when ê ∥ z-axis)
Δm = ±1 → σ-lines (when ê ⊥ z-axis, k̂ ∥ z)
Q7. State three properties of the Slater Determinant.+
1. Swap two rows (exchange two particle coordinates) → SD changes sign → anti-symmetry established.
2. Two rows equal (same quantum numbers Ci = Cj) → ΨA = 0 → Pauli Exclusion Principle.
3. Two columns equal (same position ri = rj) → ΨA = 0 → Coulomb repulsion prevents co-location.
Q8. What is the limitation of the Hartree-Fock method?+
HF does NOT account for electron correlation.
ΔE_correlation = E_exact − E_HF (non-relativistic)
This correlation energy is the gap between the exact energy and what HF predicts.
Q9. What does "self-consistent field" mean?+
Start with trial potential → use variational method to solve SE → get wavefunction → compute charge distribution → derive new potential → feed back into SE → iterate until solution converges to desired accuracy. This iterative convergence is the SCF.
Q10. Why is the product wavefunction (Hartree) insufficient?+
The product wavefunction Ψc = uc₁(r₁)uc₂(r₂)...ucN(rN) does not satisfy the Pauli Exclusion Principle — it is not anti-symmetric under particle exchange. We must anti-symmetrise it using a permutation operator, which produces the Slater Determinant.
